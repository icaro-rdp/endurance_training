# 82: Supplements and Cycling: Which Ones Really Work? (Part 1)

## Overview and Physiological Context

The sports supplement market is saturated with commercial claims that often outpace rigorous scientific validation. In endurance cycling, where marginal gains can dictate competitive outcomes, distinguishing scientifically supported ergogenic aids from ineffective or counterproductive compounds is paramount.

The Australian Institute of Sport (AIS) ABCD framework provides the gold standard for classifying sports supplements based on empirical scientific evidence:
- **Group A:** Strong scientific evidence for use in specific situations (e.g., caffeine, creatine, beta-alanine, sodium bicarbonate, dietary nitrates).
- **Group B:** Emerging support requiring further research (e.g., ketone esters, curcumin, tart cherry).
- **Group C:** Little to no proof of meaningful ergogenic benefits (e.g., BCAA, glutamine, L-carnitine).
- **Group D:** Banned substances or high contamination risk.

This episode evaluates the physiological mechanisms, dosing protocols, practical trade-offs, and evidence base for four foundational Group A supplements.

## 1. Caffeine

Caffeine (1,3,7-trimethylxanthine) is one of the most thoroughly researched and effective ergogenic aids across endurance, threshold, and repeated-sprint cycling events.

```
       Caffeine Ingestion (3-6 mg/kg)
                    │
   ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
   ▼                                 ▼
Adenosine Receptor Antagonism    Sarcoplasmic Reticulum
(Central Nervous System)         Calcium Ion Kinetics
   │                                 │
   ▼                                 ▼
• Reduced Perceived Exertion (RPE) • Enhanced Contractile Force
• Increased Motor Unit Firing Rate • Delayed Peripheral Fatigue
```

### Physiological Mechanisms
1. **Central Adenosine Receptor Antagonism:** Crosses the blood-brain barrier to competitively bind to adenosine A1 and A2A receptors, preventing adenosine-mediated central fatigue, lowering Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE), and sustaining motor unit recruitment.
2. **Enhanced Calcium Mobilization:** Modulates calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum in skeletal muscle fibers, improving excitation-contraction coupling.
3. **Substrate Kinetics:** Minor acute elevation in free fatty acid mobilization; however, the predominant ergogenic effect remains neuromuscular and central.

### Evidence-Based Protocols
- **Dosage:** 3 to 6 mg/kg body mass. Doses exceeding 9 mg/kg do not confer additional performance benefits and induce tachycardia, tremors, and gastrointestinal distress.
- **Timing:** Ingested 45 to 60 minutes prior to target efforts or strategically administered in smaller doses (1–2 mg/kg / 50–100 mg) during the final 1–2 hours of long races.
- **Genetic Inter-Individual Variability:** Polymorphisms in the *CYP1A2* gene (fast vs. slow caffeine metabolizers) and the *ADORA2A* gene modulate individual responsiveness and anxiety thresholds.

## 2. Creatine Monohydrate

While traditionally associated with anaerobic power sports and bodybuilding, creatine monohydrate offers distinct physiological utility in modern cycling disciplines.

### Physiological Mechanisms and Benefits
- **Phosphocreatine (PCr) Resynthesis:** Phosphocreatine provides the fastest substrate for ATP regeneration via the creatine kinase reaction during maximal short-duration efforts (5–15 seconds). Creatine supplementation elevates muscle total creatine and PCr stores by 15–30%.
- **Repeated Sprint Ability (RSA):** Accelerates PCr resynthesis during brief recovery periods, substantially improving power output during repeated attacks, criterium surges, and bunch sprints.
- **Glycogen Resynthesis Facilitation:** Enhances post-exercise muscular glycogen resynthesis when co-ingested with high-carbohydrate meals.

### The Body Mass Trade-Off in Cycling
- **Intracellular Osmotic Fluid Retention:** Creatine draws water into the intracellular skeletal muscle compartment, typically causing a transient mass increase of 0.8–1.8 kg.
- **Discipline Specificity:** Highly beneficial for track cyclists, criterium racers, and punchy rollering courses; requires careful periodization for pure lightweight climbers where power-to-weight ratio (W/kg) on steep gradients is the primary determinant.

### Protocol
- **Maintenance / Low-Dose Approach:** 3 to 5 g/day taken continuously with a carbohydrate-containing meal. Muscle saturation is reached smoothly within 3–4 weeks without acute water-retention spikes.
- **Acute Loading (Alternative):** 20 g/day split into 4 x 5 g doses for 5–7 days, followed by 3–5 g/day maintenance.

## 3. Beta-Alanine

Beta-alanine is the rate-limiting amino acid precursor in the intramuscular synthesis of **carnosine** (beta-alanyl-L-histidine), a critical intracellular dipeptide buffer.

### Physiological Mechanisms
- **Intracellular Proton (H+) Buffering:** During high-intensity efforts spanning 1 to 4 minutes (severe domain / VO2max), anaerobic glycolysis generates metabolic protons (H+), lowering intracellular muscle pH (acidosis) and inhibiting phosphofructokinase (PFK) and actin-myosin cross-bridge cycling.
- **Carnosine Saturation:** Chronic beta-alanine supplementation increases muscle carnosine concentrations by 40–80%, significantly expanding intracellular buffering capacity.

### Protocol and Considerations
- **Dosage:** 3.2 to 6.4 g/day administered over a loading phase of 4 to 8 weeks.
- **Paresthesia Management:** Ingestion of single doses >1.6 g causes transient, harmless paresthesia (tingling sensation in skin/extremities) via activation of MRGPRD receptors. Managed by using sustained-release formulations or splitting daily intake into 4 x 1.6 g doses.

## 4. Sodium Bicarbonate (Extracellular Buffer)

Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) acts as an extracellular buffering agent to counteract metabolic acidosis during high-intensity cycling.

```
       Sodium Bicarbonate Ingestion (0.2-0.3 g/kg)
                          │
                          ▼
            Elevated Blood Bicarbonate (HCO3-)
                          │
                          ▼
        Increased Blood pH (Extracellular Alkalosis)
                          │
                          ▼
   Accelerated H+ / Lactate Efflux from Working Muscle
                 (Via MCT1 / MCT4 Transporters)
                          │
                          ▼
     Preserved Glycolytic Flux & Delayed Neuromuscular Failure
```

### Mechanisms and Application
- **Extracellular Alkalosis:** Ingesting sodium bicarbonate elevates blood bicarbonate (HCO3-) concentration and extracellular pH.
- **Proton Efflux Gradient:** This creates a steeper pH gradient across the sarcolemma, accelerating the efflux of metabolic protons (H+) and lactate from exercising muscle fibers via Monocarboxylate Transporters (MCT1/MCT4).

### Gastrointestinal Side Effects and Modern Solutions
- **Standard Challenge:** Ingestion of basic sodium bicarbonate reacts with gastric hydrochloric acid, generating massive carbon dioxide (CO2) gas, severe nausea, vomiting, and osmotic diarrhea.
- **Maurten Bicarb System / Hydrogel Technology:** Encapsulates sodium bicarbonate within a pH-sensitive hydrogel matrix, allowing safe transit through the acidic stomach into the intestine before release, dramatically lowering GI distress.
- **Dosage & Timing:** 0.2 to 0.3 g/kg body mass ingested 60 to 90 minutes before high-intensity time trials, prologues, or criteriums.

## Summary Comparison of Tier 1 Supplements

| Supplement | Primary Mechanism | Target Target Efforts | Standard Dosage | Key Practical Caveat |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Caffeine** | Adenosine receptor antagonism; CNS drive | All endurance, TT, sprints | 3–6 mg/kg (45–60 min pre) | Sleep disruption; anxiety in slow metabolizers |
| **Creatine** | Phosphocreatine resynthesis; cellular hydration | Sprints, repeated surges, track | 3–5 g/day chronic | 1–2 kg body mass gain due to water retention |
| **Beta-Alanine** | Intracellular carnosine synthesis | 1–4 min severe domain efforts | 3.2–6.4 g/day (4–8 wks) | Requires chronic loading; tingling paresthesia |
| **Sodium Bicarb**| Extracellular H+ buffering; MCT efflux | 2–10 min maximal efforts, TTs | 0.2–0.3 g/kg (60–90 min pre) | High risk of GI distress without hydrogel/enteric delivery |

## Key Takeaways and Practical Recommendations

- **Prioritize the Foundation:** No supplement compensates for inadequate training consistency, deficient caloric/carbohydrate intake, or poor sleep architecture.
- **Context Determines Utility:** Match the supplement to the physiological demands of the event: creatine for track/criteriums, sodium bicarbonate for prologues and short TTs, beta-alanine for punchy short climbs, and caffeine for all-around endurance performance.
- **Test Before Racing:** Never experiment with acute ergogenic protocols (especially sodium bicarbonate or high-dose caffeine) on race day; validate gastrointestinal tolerance extensively during simulated training sessions.

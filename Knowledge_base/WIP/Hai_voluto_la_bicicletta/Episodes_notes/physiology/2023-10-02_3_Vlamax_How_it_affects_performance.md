# 3: VLamax: Physiological Mechanisms, Performance Impact, and Training Modulation

## Overview and Physiological Definition of VLamax

While maximal aerobic power ($) represents the oxidative ceiling of endurance performance, the **maximal glycolytic rate ({max}$)** represents the maximal rate at which skeletal muscle can convert glucose and glycogen into lactate and pyruvate via anaerobic glycolysis (expressed in /L/s$).

In exercise physiology, {max}$ serves as a primary governor of substrate partitioning, lactate accumulation dynamics, and the positioning of the anaerobic threshold relative to $. The metabolic interplay between $ (combustion/oxidative power) and {max}$ (glycolytic flux) dictates an athlete's metabolic phenotype.

---

## 1. Physiological Mechanisms and Metabolic Interplay

According to physiological models of metabolism (such as the Mader-Heck bioenergetic model):
- **Glycolytic Flux:** Anaerobic glycolysis is not an "on/off" switch reserved only for sprints; it is continuously active across all exercise intensities. Every molecule of glucose broken down through glycolysis produces pyruvate and lactate, yielding rapid ATP.
- **The Threshold Determinant:** Lactate produced by glycolytic fast-twitch fibers is cleared and oxidized by mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) in oxidative fibers and the heart. The anaerobic threshold ($) is the precise metabolic equilibrium point where lactate production equals maximum oxidative clearance capacity.
- **The Inverse Relationship with Threshold:**
  - When {max}$ is **high**, glycolytic lactate production is elevated at every submaximal power output. This floods the oxidative system with pyruvate/lactate earlier, shifting the anaerobic threshold to a **lower percentage of *.
  - When {max}$ is **low**, glycolytic flux is restrained. Pyruvate production remains low, allowing fat oxidation ($) to remain high, and shifting the anaerobic threshold to a **high percentage of * (e.g., 	ext{--}90\%$).

## 2. Reference Values and Athlete Phenotypes

{max}$ is measured in laboratory or field testing via maximal sprint sprints (typically a 15- to 20-second all-out sprint from a stationary or low-cadence start, tracking post-exercise peak blood lactate accumulation over time).

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Low** | $< 0.40$ | High fat oxidation, high fractional utilization of $, low anaerobic reserves, slow glycogen depletion. | Grand Tour GC contenders (e.g., Jonas Vingegaard), pure climbers, time trialists, Ironman triathletes. |
| **Moderate** | zsh.40 - 0.60$ | Balanced aerobic/anaerobic profile, capable of punchy accelerations and solid threshold sustainability. | Puncheurs, classic riders, all-rounders (e.g., Julian Alaphilippe, Mathieu van der Poel). |

## 3. Training Strategies to Modulate VLamax

Depending on the rider's discipline and metabolic profile, a coach must decide whether to **lower** or **raise** {max}$.

### Strategies to Lower VLamax (Aerobic Shift / Glycolytic Suppression)
1. **High-Volume Low-Intensity Endurance (Zone 2):**
   - Accumulating substantial time in Zone 2 ($< LT_1$) stimulates mitochondrial biogenesis in Type I and Type IIa fibers, increasing oxidative enzymes and suppressing baseline glycolytic drive.
2. **Low-Cadence High-Torque Efforts (Salite Forza Resistenza / SFR):**
   - Performing seated hill climbs at low cadence (	ext{--}50\,rpm$) and sub-threshold power increases force application per pedal stroke.
   - This recruits Type II glycolytic fibers at low metabolic rates, forcing them to function in an oxidative, low-lactate environment, promoting an oxidative phenotype shift (Type IIx $ightarrow$ Type IIa).
3. **Sub-Threshold and Sweet Spot Intervals:**
   - Sustained intervals at 	ext{--}92\%$ FTP increase mitochondrial density and lactate clearance kinetics (MCT-1 and MCT-4 transporters).
4. **Nutritional Periodization (Low-Carbohydrate Availability):**
   - Fasted training or "train-low" strategies reduce cellular glycogen and downregulate key glycolytic enzymes (e.g., phosphofructokinase / PFK), enhancing cellular lipid oxidation machinery.

### Strategies to Raise VLamax (Anaerobic / Sprint Enhancement)
1. **Short, Maximal Explosive Intervals (SIT):**
   - Maximal 10- to 30-second all-out sprints with complete passive recoveries (e.g., 	ext{--}6 	imes 15	ext{--}20\,s$ all-out with 	ext{--}5\,min$ rest).
2. **Heavy Resistance Gym Training:**
   - Maximal strength squats, deadlifts, and explosive Olympic lifts recruit high-threshold motor units (Type IIx) and increase creatine kinase and glycolytic enzyme activity.
3. **High Glycogen Availability:**
   - Ensuring replete muscle glycogen stores before high-intensity sessions encourages maximal glycolytic flux and prevents metabolic down-regulation.

---

## 4. Coaching Implications and Tactical Context

- **Individual Response Variations:** The same interval workout will elicit radically different metabolic strains in riders with different {max}$ values. A high-{max}$ rider performing a 10-minute threshold effort will accumulate significantly more systemic lactate and experience severe glycogen depletion compared to a low-{max}$ rider.
- **Race Demands Rule Prescription:** A low {max}$ is an advantage on long mountain passes or time trials, but a distinct disadvantage in flat criteriums or tactical sprint finishes where repeated anaerobic surges are required to bridge gaps or launch the winning sprint.

---

## Key Takeaways and Practical Recommendations

- **Dual-Engine Framework:** Performance is a delicate balance between aerobic capacity ($) and anaerobic glycolytic capacity ({max}$).
- **Targeted Modulation:** Endurance riders seeking higher FTP and greater fat oxidation must actively suppress {max}$ via high volume, tempo, and low-cadence torque work, whereas sprinters must maintain or boost {max}$ through explosive efforts and gym strength.
- **Nutritional Awareness:** High-{max}$ athletes burn carbohydrates rapidly even at moderate intensities and require higher intra-ride carbohydrate fueling rates to avoid early glycogen depletion.

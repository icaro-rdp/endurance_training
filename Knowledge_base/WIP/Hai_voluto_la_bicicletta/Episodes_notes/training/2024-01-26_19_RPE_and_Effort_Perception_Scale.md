# 19: Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE): Psychobiology, Scaling Models, and Training Auto-Regulation

## Overview and Historical Development

In an era dominated by power meters, heart rate monitors, and wearable biometrics, subjective sensation is frequently undervalued. However, the **Rate of Perceived Exertion ($RPE$)** remains one of the most powerful, scientifically validated tools for monitoring internal training load, auto-regulating workouts, and identifying overtraining.

Introduced in the 1960s by Swedish psychologist **Gunnar Borg**, RPE quantifies the conscious sensation of how strenuous an exercise bout feels. In this episode, Daniele Bazzana and Stefano Nardelli examine the psychobiology of perceived effort, compare scaling models (Borg 6–20 vs. CR10/CR100), and explain how integrating RPE with objective power and heart rate data creates a complete athlete monitoring system.

---

## 1. The Psychobiology of Perceived Exertion

According to the **Psychobiological Model of Endurance** (Marcora, 2008):
- **Effort Perception is Central:** Perceived exertion is not merely a reflection of afferent feedback from working leg muscles or heart rate. Instead, it is generated centrally in the brain via **corollary discharge (efference copy)** from the motor cortex to the sensory cortex.
- **The True Limiter of Endurance:** Athletes terminate endurance exercise not because skeletal muscles experience total mechanical failure, but when perceived exertion reaches the maximum level of effort the athlete is willing or able to tolerate.
- **Influencing Factors:** Mental fatigue, lack of sleep, environmental heat, glycogen depletion, and psychological stress all increase RPE for a given submaximal power output.

## 2. Comparing RPE Scales: Borg 6–20 vs. Borg CR10 / CR100

| Scale | Range | Numerical Structure | Physiological Anchor / Application |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Borg 6–20 Scale** | $6 - 20$ | Linear progression ($6 = \text{No exertion}, 20 = \text{Maximal}$) | Designed to correlate with Heart Rate ($HR \approx RPE \times 10$). A rating of $15$ corresponds roughly to $150\text{ bpm}$ and Anaerobic Threshold in healthy young adults. |
| **Borg CR10 Scale** | $0 - 10$ | Category-Ratio (exponential scaling from $0 = \text{Rest}$ to $10 = \text{Maximal}$) | Aligns with non-linear physiological responses (blood lactate, ventilation). Most common scale in modern digital training software (TrainingPeaks, WKO). |
| **Borg CR100 (Centimax)**| $0 - 100$ | Fine-grained percentage-like resolution | Used in advanced academic research and fine-tuned subjective rating protocols. |

## 3. The Power – Heart Rate – RPE Triad: Diagnostic Triangulation

Coaches and athletes should evaluate training sessions using the **triangulation of external load, internal physiological load, and subjective load**:

```
                   External Work (Power Output [Watts])
                                  ▲
                                 / \
                                /   \
                               /     \
                              ▼       ▼
    Internal Cardiovascular               Internal Perceptual
       (Heart Rate [bpm])  ◄────────────►  (RPE [Borg 0-10])
```

### Diagnostic Patterns and What They Reveal:
1. **High Power + Stable/Low HR + Low RPE:** Peak aerobic fitness and high physical freshness.
2. **Normal Power + Low HR + Very High RPE (Autonomic Burnout):** Classic sign of **parasympathetic saturation / non-functional overreaching**. The heart rate is suppressed and cannot rise, while effort feels crushing.
3. **Low Power + High HR + High RPE:** Indicates dehydration, core temperature overheating, acute glycogen depletion, or underlying systemic illness/infection.

---

## 4. Session-RPE (sRPE) and Workload Quantification

Quantifying daily training load via **Session-RPE (Foster et al., 2001)**:

$$\text{Session Load (Arbitrary Units)} = \text{Session RPE (0--10)} \times \text{Duration (Minutes)}$$

- *Example:* A $120\text{ minute}$ ride rated at an $RPE$ of $6$ yields a training load of $720\text{ AU}$.
- **Key Advantage:** Captures total internal stress (including heat, psychological stress, and muscular fatigue) that power-based TSS metrics often fail to capture.

---

## Key Takeaways and Practical Recommendations

- **Never Ignore Subjective Sensation:** RPE provides crucial information that power meters and heart rate monitors cannot detect on their own.
- **Watch for Suppressed HR with High RPE:** When power feels exceptionally difficult and your heart rate refuses to climb, take immediate recovery—your autonomic nervous system is overloaded.
- **Log RPE Consistently:** Regularly recording a 1–10 score after every workout enables long-term tracking of internal strain and prevents non-functional overreaching.

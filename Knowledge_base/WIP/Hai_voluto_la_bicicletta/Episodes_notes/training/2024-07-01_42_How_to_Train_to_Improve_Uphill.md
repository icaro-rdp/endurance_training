# 42: How to Train to Improve Climbing Performance

## Overview and Physiological/Training Context

Climbing is widely regarded as the ultimate test of cycling ability and forms the decisive terrain in Grand Tours, Granfondos, and competitive road events. When the road tilts upward, the physical forces governing cycling undergo a dramatic shift: aerodynamic drag ($CdA$), which dominates on flat terrain at high speeds, becomes secondary to the force of gravity resisting vertical displacement. Consequently, the primary determinant of uphill velocity is the athlete's **power-to-weight ratio (Watts per kilogram, W/kg)**.

In this episode, Daniele Bazzana and Stefano Nardelli break down the science of climbing performance. They dissect the physiological parameters that govern climbing speed, analyze the role of body mass, discuss the biomechanical differences between flat and uphill pedaling, and address common training misconceptions—most notably the belief that improving climbing requires endlessly accumulating vertical meters in training.

---

## 1. Physics and Physiological Determinants of Climbing

On uphill gradients exceeding 5–6%, cycling speed is primarily dictated by overcoming gravitational resistance:

$$	ext{Power} \approx (m_{\text{rider}} + m_{\text{bike}}) \cdot g \cdot \sin(\theta) \cdot v + F_{\text{rolling}} + F_{\text{drag}}$$

Where:
- $(m_{\text{rider}} + m_{\text{bike}})$ is the total system mass.
- $g$ is gravitational acceleration ($9.81 \text{ m/s}^2$).
- $\sin(\theta)$ represents the hill gradient.
- $v$ is climbing velocity.

### Key Physiological Determinants:
1. **VO2max:** Sets the ultimate upper ceiling of aerobic power delivery.
2. **Fractional Utilization at Functional Threshold Power (FTP):** The percentage of VO2max that can be sustained continuously on long climbs (typically 75–85% in well-trained amateurs, 85–90%+ in elite climbers).
3. **Glycolytic Rate ($V\text{La}_{\text{max}}$):** A moderate-to-low $V\text{La}_{\text{max}}$ reduces unnecessary lactate accumulation, conserves limited muscle glycogen stores, and elevates the power output at maximum fat oxidation ($	ext{FatMax}$).
4. **Metabolic Durability:** The ability to sustain target W/kg after expending 2,000–3,500+ kJ of mechanical work earlier in the ride.

## 2. The Fallacy of Excessive Elevation Gain in Training

A common error among amateur cyclists is measuring climbing preparation solely by accumulated vertical elevation gain (D+).

- **Fatigue vs. Work Quality:** Accumulating massive elevation gain on steep grades often forces riders into prolonged low cadences, high muscular fatigue, and compromised interval quality.
- **Flat vs. Uphill Intervals:** From a cardiovascular and metabolic standpoint, a 20-minute threshold interval performed at 300 W on a flat road induces the same aerobic stimulus as 300 W on an 8% climb. However, doing structured intervals on flats or gentle false flats allows precise power control without being dictated by terrain variations or steep ramps.
- **Specific Climbing Adaptations:** While metabolic fitness can be developed on flats, uphill riding is necessary to adapt to uphill pedaling kinematics, body positioning, and muscle recruitment patterns.

## 3. Biomechanics and Pedaling Kinematics Uphill

Riding uphill alters the biomechanical relationship between the rider and the bicycle.

- **Changes in Effective Pedaling Cadence:** On steep gradients, low inertial load (flywheel speed) creates a "dead spot" in the pedal stroke where momentum drops rapidly between downstrokes. This increases peak effective pedal force (torque) for the same average power output.
- **Optimal Cadence Selection:** Grinding at extremely low cadences (<60–65 RPM) increases neuromuscular strain and accelerates the recruitment of easily fatigable fast-twitch (Type II) fibers. Climbers should choose gearing (e.g., 34x30 or 34x34) that permits a self-selected cadence of **75–90 RPM** on steep gradients.
- **Seated vs. Standing Climbing:**
  - *Seated climbing* is metabolically more efficient, lowering oxygen consumption ($\sim 5-8\% \text{ lower VO2}$) and preserving glycogen.
  - *Standing climbing* increases peak power and torque by utilizing body weight, but increases heart rate and oxygen cost due to upper-body and core recruitment. Standing should be used strategically for steep pitches, hairpin accelerations, or brief postural relief.

---

## 4. Power-to-Weight Optimization: Balancing Watts and Kilograms

Optimizing W/kg requires careful balance between increasing power output and reducing body mass.

- **The Pitfall of Severe Caloric Deficit:** Aggressive weight loss often results in loss of lean muscle mass, hormonal disruption (RED-S), reduced immune function, and compromised power output.
- **Power First, Body Composition Second:** Athletes should prioritize progressive overload and proper fueling to maximize threshold power. Weight loss should occur gradually through periodized daily nutrition without compromising intra-workout fueling.

---

## 5. Pacing Dynamics on Long Climbs

Effective pacing on mountain passes dictates whether an athlete sets a personal record or suffers a catastrophic late-climb collapse.

- **Even vs. Negative Pacing:** The most efficient pacing strategy on long climbs (15–60+ minutes) is even pacing near or slightly below critical power/FTP.
- **The "Over-the-Top" Error:** Many riders surge excessively at the bottom of a climb when fresh, generating high blood lactate and severe metabolic acidosis that cripples power output in the final third of the ascent.
- **Using VAM (Velocità Ascensionale Media):** VAM ($	ext{m/h} = \frac{\text{Elevation Gain (m)}}{\text{Time (hours)}}$) provides a practical gauge of vertical ascent speed, highly correlated with W/kg on slopes greater than 6–7%.

---

## Key Takeaways and Practical Recommendations

- **Prioritize W/kg Sustainably:** Build the engine first; focus on increasing functional threshold power through structured intervals before pursuing aggressive weight reduction.
- **Do Not Chase Arbitrary Elevation Gain:** Structure intervals with clean power targets; utilize flat or moderately rolling terrain for high-quality workouts and reserve mountain climbs for specific pacing and kinematic adaptation.
- **Select Appropriate Gearing:** Equip your bike with compact chainrings and wide-range cassettes to maintain an efficient pedaling cadence (75–85+ RPM) on steep ascents.
- **Master Steady Pacing:** Avoid aggressive early surges on long climbs; maintain disciplined power distribution to prevent premature glycogen depletion and late-stage power collapse.

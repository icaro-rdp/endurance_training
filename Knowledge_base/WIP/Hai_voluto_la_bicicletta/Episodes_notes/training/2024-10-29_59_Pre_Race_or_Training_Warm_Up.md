# 59: Pre-Race and Training Warm-Up Protocols

## Overview and Physiological/Training Context

The warm-up (*riscaldamento*) prior to a competitive cycling race (such as a time trial, criterium, road race, or Granfondo) or before a high-intensity interval session is a fundamental component of athletic preparation. Despite its widespread practice, the warm-up is frequently executed incorrectly: athletes often either warm up too briefly and enter high-intensity efforts with sluggish oxygen kinetics, or warm up excessively (e.g., 45 to 60+ minutes on stationary rollers in the heat), inducing severe thermal stress, glycogen depletion, and premature fatigue.

In this episode, Daniele Bazzana and Stefano Nardelli break down the physiology of warming up. They examine how the warm-up modulates muscle temperature, oxygen uptake kinetics ($	ext{VO}_2$ priming effect), and neuromuscular recruitment, provide step-by-step warm-up protocols for various cycling disciplines, and detail pre-race hydration and fueling strategies.

---

## 1. Physiological Mechanisms of the Warm-Up

A structured warm-up prepares the body for high-intensity exercise through four primary physiological mechanisms:

### 1. Temperature-Dependent Mechanisms (The $Q_{10}$ Effect)
- **Elevated Muscle Temperature:** Active pedaling raises intramuscular temperature by 1.5°C to 2.5°C.
- **Enzyme Kinetics and ATP Turnover:** Elevated temperature accelerates the kinetic rate of metabolic enzymes (phosphofructokinase, glycogen phosphorylase, ATPase), speeding up glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation.
- **Reduced Muscle and Tendon Viscosity:** Warmer muscle tissue exhibits lower internal viscous resistance, increasing mechanical efficiency and contraction velocity.
- **Rightward Shift of Oxyhemoglobin Dissociation Curve (Bohr Effect):** Elevated local temperature and mild acidity facilitate more rapid oxygen release from hemoglobin to working muscle tissue.

### 2. Non-Temperature Mechanisms ($	ext{VO}_2$ Priming Effect)
- **Accelerating Phase II Aerobic Kinetics:** Performing a brief, controlled sub-maximal or supra-threshold effort accelerates the time constant ($	au$) of pulmonary oxygen uptake during subsequent efforts. This reduces the initial "oxygen deficit," sparing finite anaerobic phosphocreatine and glycogen stores at the start of a race.
- **Microvascular Perfusion:** Stimulates localized nitric oxide release and vasodilation in working muscle capillaries.

### 3. Neuromuscular Priming (Post-Activation Potentiation / PAP)
- Short, high-cadence or high-torque accelerations increase spinal motor neuron excitability, myosin light-chain phosphorylation, and motor unit firing frequency.

### 4. Psychological Focus and Cognitive Activation
- A structured routine narrows cognitive focus, reduces pre-race anxiety, and establishes target pedaling rhythms.

## 2. Common Warm-Up Mistakes

Bazzana and Nardelli highlight major pitfalls that compromise performance:

- **Mistake 1: Excessive Duration and Volume:** Spending 45 to 60 minutes on stationary rollers prior to a race. This wastes precious muscle glycogen, elevates core temperature, and causes unnecessary sweat/electrolyte loss.
- **Mistake 2: Overheating (Thermal Strain):** Warming up in direct sunlight without airflow or cooling fans. Hyperthermia significantly impairs subsequent endurance performance and accelerates cardiac drift.
- **Mistake 3: Excessive Intensity:** Performing all-out sprints or long threshold bouts that accumulate high blood lactate ($>6-8 \text{ mmol/L}$) and severe intramuscular acidosis just before the start.
- **Mistake 4: Long Transition Gap:** Finishing the warm-up 30–45 minutes before the race start. Within 15–20 minutes of inactivity, muscle temperature drops back toward baseline, and the $	ext{VO}_2$ priming effect dissipates.

## 3. Evidence-Based Warm-Up Protocols

Warm-up design should be tailored to the duration and intensity profile of the specific event:

### Protocol A: Standard Time Trial / Criterium / Short HIIT Warm-Up (20–25 Min)
- **0:00 – 10:00:** Progressive Zone 1 to Zone 2 smooth spinning (90–95 RPM).
- **10:00 – 14:00:** 4 minutes at Second Threshold / Sweet Spot (88–95% FTP) to stimulate aerobic kinetics.
- **14:00 – 17:00:** 3 minutes easy recovery in Zone 1.
- **17:00 – 19:00:** 2 x 10-second progressive sprint accelerations (110–120 RPM) with 50s easy recovery (neuromuscular priming).
- **19:00 – 22:00:** 3 minutes easy cool-down spin.

### Protocol B: Long Road Race / Granfondo Warm-Up (10–15 Min)
- Because road races and Granfondos last 3 to 6+ hours and often feature a neutral roll-out, an extensive warm-up is unnecessary.
- **Structure:** 10–12 minutes of easy progressive spinning with 1–2 brief 10-second rev-ups to ensure bike gears and legs are functioning smoothly.

---

## 4. Pre-Race Fueling, Hydration, and Cooling Strategies

- **Nutritional Priming:** During the 20-minute warm-up, sip 200–300 mL of water or electrolyte drink. Ingest a fast-acting energy gel (20–30 g CHO) 10–15 minutes before the start to ensure elevated circulating blood glucose entering the opening kilometers.
- **Thermal Management:** In hot conditions, use cooling vests (*ice vests*), cold towel wraps, and electric fans during stationary roller warm-ups to blunt core temperature rise while warming active muscles.

---

## Key Takeaways and Practical Recommendations

- **Keep Warm-Ups Concise (15–25 Min):** A 20-minute warm-up is sufficient to achieve maximal muscle temperature, enzyme activation, and $	ext{VO}_2$ priming without wasting glycogen.
- **Include Short Priming Bursts:** Include 3–4 minutes at sweet spot/threshold and 2 short accelerations (6–10s) to prime aerobic kinetics and motor unit recruitment.
- **Minimize the Start Line Gap:** Aim to finish your warm-up 10 to 15 minutes before the start to prevent muscle temperature decline.
- **Prevent Overheating:** Utilize cooling fans, shade, and ice vests during warm-ups in hot weather to prevent premature core temperature elevation.

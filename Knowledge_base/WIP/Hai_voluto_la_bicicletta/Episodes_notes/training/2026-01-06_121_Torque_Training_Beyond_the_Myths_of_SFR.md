# 121: Torque Training: Beyond the Myths of SFR

## Overview and Physiological/Training Context

In Italian cycling culture, few training methodologies have generated as much passionate debate as **SFR (Salite Forza Resistenza)**—low-cadence hill climbs (typically 40–50 rpm) popularized in the 1980s and 1990s. Traditionally touted as a primary tool for developing "muscular strength" on the bike, SFR has in recent years faced skepticism, with some coaches labeling it an obsolete or even harmful practice.

This episode reframes the entire discussion around modern sports science principles: renaming the category **Torque Training**. Daniele Bazzana and Stefano Nardelli deconstruct the physical and physiological realities of pedaling mechanics, explaining what happens when cadence drops at a given power output, how motor unit recruitment shifts, and how specific low-cadence stimuli can be strategically programmed for neuromuscular and metabolic adaptations without creating orthopedic overload.

---

## 1. Physics and Biomechanics: Power, Torque, and Angular Velocity

To understand torque training, one must analyze the fundamental mechanical equation of cycling propulsion:

$$\text{Power (Watts)} = \text{Torque (N}\cdot\text{m)} \times \text{Angular Velocity (rad/s)}$$

Where:
- $\text{Angular Velocity} = \text{Cadence (rpm)} \times \frac{2\pi}{60}$
- $\text{Torque} = \frac{\text{Power}}{\text{Angular Velocity}}$

### Mechanical Comparison at Constant Power (300 W)
- **At 90 RPM (High Cadence):**
  $$\omega = 90 \times \frac{2\pi}{60} \approx 9.42 \text{ rad/s} \implies \text{Torque} = \frac{300}{9.42} \approx 31.8 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$
- **At 60 RPM (Low Cadence / Torque):**
  $$\omega = 60 \times \frac{2\pi}{60} \approx 6.28 \text{ rad/s} \implies \text{Torque} = \frac{300}{6.28} \approx 47.7 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$
- **At 45 RPM (Classic SFR):**
  $$\omega = 45 \times \frac{2\pi}{60} \approx 4.71 \text{ rad/s} \implies \text{Torque} = \frac{300}{4.71} \approx 63.7 \text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$

Pedaling at 45 rpm requires **double the crank torque** (force per pedal stroke) compared to 90 rpm at the exact same metabolic power output.

## 2. Why Torque Training Is Not "Gym Strength"

A widespread misconception is that low-cadence intervals replace heavy resistance training in the gym:

- **Percentage of Maximal Voluntary Contraction (% MVC):** During a 300 W effort at 45 rpm, the peak effective force applied to the pedal typically represents only **15% to 25% of an athlete's maximal isometric/dynamic strength (MVC)**. Heavy gym resistance training targets $\ge 80\%$ 1RM to induce neural adaptations, motor unit synchronization, and myofibrillar hypertrophy.
- **Different Adaptation Targets:** Gym training builds maximal force ceiling, rate of force development (RFD), and tendon stiffness. On-bike torque training targets **submaximal motor unit recruitment patterns** and localized metabolic adaptations in specific cycling joint angles.

---

## 3. Neuromuscular Recruitment and Metabolic Adaptations

According to **Henneman's Size Principle**, motor units are recruited in order of increasing size and threshold: Type I (slow-twitch oxidative) $\rightarrow$ Type IIa (fast-twitch oxidative-glycolytic) $\rightarrow$ Type IIx (fast-twitch glycolytic).

1. **Premature Type IIa Recruitment:** When cadence is dropped from 90 rpm to 50 rpm at Tempo or Sweet Spot intensity, the high peak force per pedal stroke cannot be sustained solely by Type I fibers. The central nervous system is forced to recruit higher-threshold Type IIa motor units at lower power outputs than normal.
2. **Enhanced Oxidative Capacity in Fast-Twitch Fibers:** By repeatedly activating Type IIa fibers during aerobic/threshold conditions, torque intervals stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative enzyme upregulation (e.g., citrate synthase, cytochrome c oxidase), and capillary density within fast-twitch fibers, enhancing long-term durability and lactate clearance.
3. **Reduced Cardiovascular Strain:** At low cadences, the internal mechanical cost of moving the legs is reduced, resulting in slightly lower heart rates and oxygen consumption for the same power output, though localized muscular strain and perceived leg effort (RPE) are significantly higher.

## 4. Practical Torque Training Protocols

### A. Neuromuscular Standing Starts / Torque Sprints
- **Objective:** Maximal instantaneous torque development and rapid motor unit recruitment.
- **Protocol:** From a near-complete stop or walking pace (~5–10 km/h) in a heavy gear (e.g., 53x14 or 52x13):
  - **Effort:** 6 to 8 seconds maximal all-out sprint out of the saddle.
  - **Cadence:** Starts at ~30–40 rpm, finishing around 70–80 rpm.
  - **Recovery:** 2 to 3 minutes of easy spinning (complete phosphocreatine resynthesis).
  - **Volume:** 2 sets of 4–5 repetitions.
  - **Metabolic note:** Minimal glycolytic acidosis due to the brief duration ($<8$s).

### B. Sustained Hill Torque Intervals (Modern SFR / Low Cadence Tempo)
- **Objective:** Type IIa fiber aerobic conditioning, pelvic stability, and force endurance.
- **Protocol:** On a steady 5–7% gradient:
  - **Intensity:** 85–92% FTP (Tempo / Sweet Spot).
  - **Cadence:** 50–55 rpm (strictly seated).
  - **Duration:** 3 to 5 blocks of 4–8 minutes.
  - **Recovery:** 3–4 minutes at normal cadence (85–95 rpm) in Zone 2.
  - **Biomechanical focus:** Stable pelvis, no lateral torso swaying, neutral knee tracking.

## 5. Biomechanical Risks and Coaching Pitfalls

- **Patellofemoral & Knee Overload:** Low cadences drastically increase shear forces across the patellofemoral joint and patellar tendon. Athletes with pre-existing chondromalacia, tendonitis, or improper saddle height (too low or too far forward) face high injury risks.
- **Lower Back and Lumbar Spine Strain:** Excessive gear grinding often leads to compensatory lumbar flexion and pelvic rocking if core stability is inadequate.
- **Overestimating Specificity:** Modern road races and time trials require high cadence versatility (85–105 rpm). Exclusively grinding low gears degrades neuromuscular coordination and pedaling efficiency at high speeds.

---

## Key Takeaways and Practical Recommendations

- **Torque $\neq$ Gym Strength:** Low cadence intervals at 50 rpm utilize only 15–25% of MVC; they do not replace heavy resistance training for building maximal force or RFD.
- **Motor Unit Targeting:** The true physiological value of torque intervals lies in recruiting and training Type IIa fibers to operate aerobically under sustained force.
- **Strict Postural Control:** Perform sustained torque intervals strictly seated with a stabilized pelvis and neutral knee alignment to protect joints and prevent spinal compensations.
- **Periodize Cadences:** Combine heavy standing starts (neuromuscular power) and seated 50–55 rpm blocks with high-cadence spin-ups (110–130 rpm) to develop a complete torque-velocity profile.

# 153: Pedaling Cadence: Is There the Ideal One?

## Overview and Physiological Definition

In cycling coaching and folklore, pedaling cadence has long been a subject of intense debate—contrasting low-cadence "mashers" (e.g., Jan Ullrich pedaling at 70–75 RPM) with high-cadence "spinners" (e.g., Lance Armstrong and Chris Froome pedaling at 95–105 RPM).

At any fixed mechanical power output ($P$), power is the mathematical product of **torque** (tangential force applied to the crank arm, $\tau$) and **angular velocity** (cadence in radians per second, $\omega$):

$$P = \tau \times \omega$$

Changing pedaling cadence alters how the energetic cost of exercise is distributed between the **musculoskeletal/neuromuscular system** (peak pedal force, motor unit recruitment, glycogen usage) and the **cardiovascular/respiratory system** (heart rate, ventilation, oxygen consumption). This episode deconstructs the science of cadence selection, evaluates the gap between energetically optimal and freely chosen cadence, and provides guidelines for cadence training.

## 1. Energetically Optimal Cadence vs. Freely Chosen Cadence

### The Cadence Discrepancy
Exercise physiology laboratories consistently demonstrate a fundamental paradox:
- **Energetically Optimal Cadence (EOC):** The cadence that minimizes oxygen consumption ($\dot{V}O_2$) and maximizes gross mechanical efficiency at moderate power outputs (150–200 W) is surprisingly low—typically **55 to 65 RPM**.
- **Freely Chosen Cadence (FCC):** Experienced and elite road cyclists spontaneously choose cadences between **85 and 95+ RPM** on flat/rolling terrain.

```
  Oxygen Cost (VO2)
        ^
        |           \                 /
        |            \   Min VO2     /
        |             \  (55-65 RPM)/
        |              \-----------/
        |                           \
        |                            \  Freely Chosen Cadence (85-95 RPM)
        +----------------------------------------> Cadence (RPM)
```

### Why Do Cyclists Freely Choose an Energetically "Inefficient" Cadence?
1. **Minimizing Peak Pedal Force:** Higher RPM reduces the torque required per pedal stroke. This minimizes intramuscular pressure, allowing continuous capillary blood flow and preventing local ischemia.
2. **Type I Motor Unit Sparing:** Lower torque demands allow the central nervous system to rely primarily on oxidative Type I fibers, sparing glycogen-dense Type II fibers for critical surges.
3. **Cardiovascular System Capacity:** Elite cyclists possess immense cardiovascular delivery systems (high stroke volume, large $\dot{V}O_{2\text{max}}$). They can easily absorb the higher ventilatory and internal kinetic cost of high-RPM pedaling to spare their leg musculature from peripheral fatigue.

### The Gradient Effect and Inertia
On steep climbs, bicycle speed drops, altering the inertia of the rear wheel. The pedal stroke encounters "dead spots" at top and bottom dead centers. Cyclists naturally drop cadence to 70–80 RPM on steep gradients to maximize effective crank torque application during the power phase (1 o'clock to 5 o'clock).

## 3. Training Applications: Cadence Drills

Manipulating cadence is a potent tool for targeted physiological adaptations:

### 1. Low-Cadence High-Torque Intervals (SFR / Slow Cadence Repeats)
- **Protocol:** $4\text{--}6 \times 4\text{--}5\text{ min}$ at **40–55 RPM** in Sweet Spot / Low Threshold (85–92% FTP) on a 5–7% climb.
- **Adaptation:** High motor unit recruitment, enhanced lateral force transmission, tendon stiffness, and down-regulation of glycolytic flux ($V_{La\text{max}}$).

### 2. High-Cadence Neuromuscular Spin-Ups
- **Protocol:** $3 \times 10\text{ min}$ at **100–110 RPM** in Zone 2 with micro-burst spin-ups to 120–130 RPM.
- **Adaptation:** Improves intra- and inter-muscular coordination, smooths pedal stroke torque variations, and reduces co-contraction of antagonist muscles (hamstrings vs. quadriceps).

## Key Takeaways and Practical Recommendations

- **No Single Ideal Cadence Exists:** Optimal cadence is dynamic and shifts with power output, gradient, fatigue, and individual fiber-type phenotype.
- **Freely Chosen Cadence Spares Muscle:** Cycling at 85–95 RPM costs slightly more cardiovascular oxygen, but dramatically reduces peak muscle force and spares glycogen.
- **Cadence Naturally Scales with Power:** Expect cadence to rise as power increases (e.g., 75 RPM at 150 W vs. 95 RPM at 350 W).
- **Incorporate Both Cadence Extremes:** Use low-cadence torque work (40–55 RPM) to develop muscular force and threshold fractional utilization; use high-cadence drills (>105 RPM) to optimize neuromuscular coordination.

# 56: Concurrent Training: Integrating Strength and Endurance in Cycling

## Overview and Physiological/Training Context

Concurrent training—the simultaneous inclusion of heavy resistance (strength) training and endurance training within the same periodized program—has transitioned from a historical taboo into an essential performance pillar for modern cyclists. Historically, endurance athletes and coaches avoided gym lifting due to fears of gaining excess muscle mass, reducing mitochondrial density, or experiencing debilitating muscle soreness that interfered with cycling sessions.

In this episode, Daniele Bazzana and Stefano Nardelli break down the science and application of concurrent training. They explain the intracellular molecular signaling pathways governing strength vs. endurance adaptations, detail the "interference effect" between AMPK and mTOR, outline practical microcycle scheduling frameworks, specify exact lift selection and loading parameters, and explain why heavy strength training directly improves cycling economy, time-to-exhaustion (TTE), and sprint power.

---

## 1. Molecular Signaling: Resolving the Interference Effect

At the cellular level, endurance exercise and resistance exercise activate distinct, potentially conflicting intracellular signaling cascades:

### The Molecular Pathways:
- **The Endurance Pathway (AMPK / PGC-1alpha):** Prolonged low-intensity and high-intensity endurance cycling increases cellular AMP/ATP ratios and intracellular calcium flux ($Ca^{2+}$), activating **AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)** and Calcium/Calmodulin-Dependent Protein Kinase (CaMK). These kinases phosphorylate and activate **PGC-1alpha**, driving mitochondrial biogenesis, capillarization, and enhanced fat oxidation enzyme expression.
- **The Strength Pathway (mTORC1 / p70S6K):** High-load mechanical tension on muscle fibers activates the **mechanistic Target of Rapamycin Complex 1 (mTORC1)** and ribosomal protein S6 kinase (p70S6K), driving myofibrillar protein synthesis and neuromuscular adaptations.
- **The Classical Interference Model:** In vitro studies suggested that active AMPK phosphorylates the TSC2 complex and raptor, inhibiting mTORC1 and potentially blunting strength gains when endurance exercise immediately follows resistance training.

### Modern Resolution in Cyclists:
Modern sports science demonstrates that the molecular interference effect is primarily an issue of **residual neuromuscular fatigue and poor scheduling**, rather than absolute cellular incompatibility. When structured with appropriate recovery windows and loading parameters, endurance cyclists achieve substantial gains in strength, neuromuscular efficiency, and cycling economy without blunting mitochondrial adaptations.

## 2. Physiological Benefits of Strength Training for Cyclists

Heavy resistance training improves cycling performance through several distinct neuromuscular mechanisms:

1. **Enhanced Cycling Economy and Gross Efficiency:** Heavy lifting increases tendon stiffness and motor unit synchronization. Cyclists require lower oxygen consumption ($	ext{VO}_2$) and fewer motor units to produce the same submaximal wattage.
2. **Delayed Type II Fiber Recruitment:** By increasing the peak force capability of fatigue-resistant Type I slow-twitch fibers, the recruitment of easily fatigable fast-twitch (Type IIa/IIx) fibers is postponed to higher power outputs.
3. **Increased Rate of Force Development (RFD) and Peak Sprint Power:** Maximizes on-bike acceleration, standing start power, and final sprint velocity.
4. **Improved Durability and Fatigue Resistance:** Preserves power output and threshold sustainability after expending high cumulative work (>2,500–3,000 kJ).

## 3. Microcycle Scheduling: How to Sequence Sessions

Proper session sequencing within the weekly microcycle is critical to minimize interference:

### Rule 1: Prioritize Strength First When Fresh
- **Recommended Sequence:** Perform the gym strength session in the morning when the central nervous system is fresh, followed by an easy Zone 2 endurance ride 4 to 8 hours later (or on the following day).
- **Rationale:** Lifting with maximal concentric intent requires high neural freshness. Attempting heavy squats after a 3-hour cycling ride leads to compromised lifting mechanics, reduced motor unit recruitment, and high injury risk.

### Rule 2: Minimum 6- to 8-Hour Recovery Window
- If both strength and hard interval sessions must occur on the same day, separate them by at least 6 to 8 hours and consume a high-carbohydrate, protein-rich meal in between.

### Rule 3: Avoid Hard Intervals Post-Gym
- Never schedule high-intensity threshold or VO2max intervals within 24–48 hours of a heavy lower-body gym session; residual muscle damage (DOMS) impairs high-end power output.

## 4. Lift Selection, Loading Parameters, and Avoiding Hypertrophy

Endurance cyclists should focus strictly on neural strength and rate of force development rather than bodybuilding-style hypertrophy:

- **Core Compound Exercises:** Barbell back squats, Romanian deadlifts (RDLs), Bulgarian split squats, leg press, step-ups, and core anti-rotation drills.
- **Loading Parameters:**
  - **Intensity:** **80% to 88% of 1RM** (heavy loads).
  - **Repetitions:** **3 to 5 repetitions per set** (3–4 sets).
  - **Rest Intervals:** **2 to 3 minutes** between sets (ensuring full ATP-PCr restoration).
  - **Concentric Intent:** Move the barbell with maximal explosive intent on the concentric phase.
  - **Reps in Reserve (RIR):** Terminate sets with 1–2 reps in reserve; do not train to muscular failure.

---

## 5. Periodization Across the Season

- **Winter Base Phase (Preparatory):** 2 sessions per week. Focus on progressive overload, building maximum neural strength and movement proficiency.
- **Competitive Season (Maintenance):** 1 session per week (or 1 session every 10–14 days). Short, focused sessions (2–3 sets of 3 reps at 80% 1RM) maintain neural adaptations and tendon stiffness throughout the race season with zero residual fatigue.

---

## Key Takeaways and Practical Recommendations

- **Lift Heavy with Low Reps:** Target 3–5 repetitions at 80–85%+ 1RM to maximize neural recruitment and cycling economy without adding excess muscle mass.
- **Separate Sessions Intelligently:** Separate gym lifting from endurance cycling by at least 6–8 hours; perform gym strength first when the nervous system is fresh.
- **Do Not Train to Failure:** Always leave 1–2 repetitions in reserve (RIR 1–2) to avoid excessive structural muscle damage and prolonged DOMS.
- **Maintain Strength Year-Round:** Preserve winter strength gains during the racing season with 1 brief weekly maintenance session.

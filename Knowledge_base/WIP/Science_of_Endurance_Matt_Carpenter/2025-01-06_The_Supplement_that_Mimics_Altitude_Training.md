# The Supplement that Mimics Altitude Training?

Exogenous ketones have been growing in popularity in both research and the real world over the past decade, following reports of a ‘[mircale drink](https://www.cyclingweekly.com/news/racing/tour-de-france/questions-raised-use-ketone-miracle-drink-tour-de-france-431463)’ at the Tour de France, as well as early trials conducted on british athletes before London 2012 - all using exogenous ketones as a means to attempt to improve their performance. 

At these past timepoints, there were minimal studies on exogenous ketones (research really kicked off in 2016), meaning now, we have a growing body of research to investigate.

Ketones play a big role in my PhD, so it’s something we will discuss in much more detail in future in this newsletter. There’s so much to dig into, different types of ketone, different times of ingestion, but that’s for a future week.

Today I want to specially discuss what in my opinion, is the most interesting finding that may have endurance implications.

## The study in question: [Evans et al. (2022) - ](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/pdf/10.1152/ajpendo.00264.2022)**[Ketone monoester ingestion increases postexercise serum erythropoietin concentrations in healthy men](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/pdf/10.1152/ajpendo.00264.2022)**

Using the [delta g ketone monester ](https://www.deltagketones.com/en-gb?gad_source=1&gadid=&gclid=CjwKCAiA1eO7BhATEiwAm0Ee-BdTC_X0xOJa0_VMjWa059sX9-K5MPolfMLuLzup_vNzvsXiUF2f5xoCRaMQAvD_BwE&utm_campaign=17810403595&utm_content=&utm_medium=paid&utm_source=google&utm_term=), researchers had 9 participants consume a large dose of ketone ester (exact dose shown in the abstract above), straight after the 1 hour cycle ‘initially alternating between 50% and 90% of maximal aerobic capacity for 2 min each interval, and then 50% and 80%, and 50% and 70% when the higher intensity was unsustainable’.

Folloing ketone ingestion, the authors saw a significant rise (c. 20%) in EPO concentration. Outlined in the figures, taken directly from the study, shown below.

You can see in figure 3b, 7 of the 9 participants saw EPO production increase, with significant increases between 2-4 hours post consumption.

This is not something I had previously seen in ketone research. And the mechanism itself isn’t completely clear, with the authors stating 

  * _‘The mechanisms by which bHB may increase EPO remain unknown, but may include histone acetylation since bHB at physiological concentrations (1.2 mmol·L1 ) can induce fivefold increases in histone H3 acetylation in kidneys of mice and subsequently provide protection against oxidative stress’_

In any case, this increase in EPO concentration mass may have serious performance implications. I’ll explain why below.

## What is the relevance of increased EPO concentration for endurance athletes?

Erythropoietin (EPO) is a hormone primarily produced by the kidneys that stimulates red blood cell production in the bone marrow to enhance oxygen transport in the blood. 

Red blood cells provide oxygen to the working muscles and are critical for endurance performance. In short, increased EPO concentration would be expected to enhance red blood cell production, thereby improving the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity and improving endurance performance. This allows athletes to sustain higher intensities for longer periods by delaying fatigue and increasing VO2 max.

This is the reason that EPO injections, or other means of increasing red blood cell mass, were such a ubiquitous method of doping in endurance sport, and I’m sure, still are.

Tyler Hamilton in his book exposing doping in the Tour de France in the late 90’s gives graphic detail on lines of athletes getting blood transfusions. If you haven’t read it, [The Secret Race](https://www.worldofbooks.com/en-gb/products/secret-race-book-daniel-coyle-9780552169172?sku=GOR005054329&gad_source=1&gclid=CjwKCAiA1eO7BhATEiwAm0Ee-JbxVNjzFs4KHgf_c8vQv4XvQfQD8Zti2FGDdup_Uq5BO_EMzrWpuBoCtCYQAvD_BwE) is one of my favourite books of all time. Highly recommended.

Whilst EPO injections and blood transfusions are all banned under WADA regulations, athletes do commonly usitlise other methods of increasing EPO production as a means of stimulating red blood cell production. This is done by training at, or living at altitude, and is the reason many athletes spend time at altitude. 

## Altitude Training - a tried and tested method of increasing EPO.

Training at altitude increased red blood cell content by stimulating the body to produce more erythropoietin (EPO) in response to lower oxygen levels at high altitudes. The percent of oxygen in the air is identical, but air particles are less dense (hence the term, the air is thinner), and hence reduced oxygen delivery. EPO production is increased as a respoinse in order to maintain homeostasis in this environment.

Now, brining it back to exogenous ketone. This link to altitude training is important, as this is why this study implies that, to an extent, ketone ingestion may mimic altitude training in terms of increasing EPO. Which could enhance performance. This is one of only few studies on the subject, it’s unclear whether this effect is clinically meaningful, (whether it’s big enough to have a performance effect), and whether this research will be replicated. 

The most important consideration is the fact that authors only assessed the acute EPO response. This may not end up in greater red blood cell production. It may do, but it’s important to have this data before making the conclusion. **So whilst it’s not conclusive, it’s very encouraging.**

In fact, if future work does show increased red cell mass, it would put ketones in a very short list of interventions that increase red blood cell mass, alongside altitude training and various doping methods outlined above. A very exclusive list.

Being a single research study without a performance test and not measuring changes in red blood cell count directly, it’s important not to overhype this research. With the limited research conducted to date, it seems too early to recommend the use of exogenous ketones as a specific method to increase red blood cell mass.

Saying that, This study will hopefully result in a lot more research in chronic ingestion of ketones to look at whether this change may mean ketones are performance enhancing when taken everyday.

## Practical implications

This study alone is not sufficient to recommend daily ingestion of ketones (especially because [ketone esters ](https://www.deltagketones.com/en-gb/products/g-ketone-performance?selling_plan=3948937465)are still coming in at c.£30 per serving!), but it’s certainly an area to keep an eye on in future, and suggests that the most promising use of ketones in endurance performance may be chronic ingestion, rather than pre-exercise ingestion.

I’m hoping future research looks at whether cheaper methods of ketone ingestion (such as [1,3 butanediol](https://ketone.com/products/ketone-iq-classic-shot-peach?variant=44877613203625), a ketone precursor that’s been subject to less research, but a lot of marketing) can give the same effect, since this is much more cost effective. I’m not sure, and Ketone-IQ have been [using this research to make claims about there own ketone](https://help.ketone.com/article/h6u6uxy44a-what-are-the-effects-of-ketone-iq), which is problematic since they are physiologically different, and 1,3 butanediol doesn’t increase ketones to the same degree. Hopefully this is addressed in future. As soon as it is, you’ll know about it here.

There are other methods of ketone ingestion being researched. I’ve just finished some work on pre-exercise ketone ingestion. But this chronic use seems to be showing more potential for performance improvements. But it’s too early to state with any degree of confidence that ketones are performance enhancing, despite widespread use in endurance events such as the Tour de France [to this day.](https://www.fueltherideacademy.com/blog/why-are-tour-de-france-riders-using-ketones)

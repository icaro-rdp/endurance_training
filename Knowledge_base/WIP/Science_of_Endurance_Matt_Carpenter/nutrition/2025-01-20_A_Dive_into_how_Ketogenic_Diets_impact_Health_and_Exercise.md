# A Dive into how Ketogenic Diets impact Health and Exercise

I plan on making this blog a combination of updates on new publications, general thoughts on supplementation and dietary changes, and I will focus on topical supplements most of the time (seamoss next week…), but for today, I want to discuss a more general topic, based on my PhD work.

This means an introduction on the potential application of ketogenic diets, potential strengths and causes for concern, both in health and exercise.

This will be as general and unspecific as my blogs get, and probably slightly more scientific and less rambly, but I hope it’s interesting nonetheless.

[Subscribe now](https://mcarpenter.substack.com/subscribe?)

Ketogenic diets are a hot, yet controversial topic in the world of health and exercise performance. Whilst originally studied as a method of treating drug resistant epilepsy in the early 1900’s, todays advocates purport health and performance benefits of the diet.

There is controversy surrounding the impact of the diet on health, with a historic fear of fat concerning some. In spite of the contrary nature of the diet in this sense, many health markers tend to improve. For example, weight loss is often greater on ketogenic diets (1) when food is consumed ad libitum. A key mechanism for this is the shift to fat oxidation, which increases satiety, leading to fewer calories consumed (2). Further to this, circulating ketone bodies are being studied for amongst other things, a hunger reducing effect, which may be a further mechanism for the spontaneous reduction in calorie consumption evident in research. In addition, the ketogenic diet has shown to improve a number of markers of metabolic disease, as well as conditions such as diabetes, in which insulin requirements can be dramatically reduced, and in most cases eliminated through the use of a ketogenic diet.

An important caveat to this, often concerning medical professionals is the volatility of LDL cholesterol when following a ketogenic diet. Whilst meta-analysis showed no significant difference in LDL (3), the high degree of variability suggests caution should be taken when embarking on the diet. Thus, whilst research suggests that a ketogenic diet is a safe and effective tool to elicit weight loss and improve health, testing health markers is recommended to ensure safety of the diet.

**Exercise Performance**

When it comes to exercise performance, little else can spark such heated conversations like that of impact of ketogenic diets. With less research in this area, there is a great deal of hysteria around the potential use of ketogenic diets to enhance exercise performance, and at the other end of the spectrum, individuals who are vehemently ‘anti-keto’ for exercise performance. The key rationale for ketogenic diets in exercise performance are as follows:

  * Human fat stores are vast (>40,000 calories in lean individuals), compared to carbohydrate stores which are finite (~ 2000 calories)

  * Ketogenic diets lead to dramatic increases in fat oxidation, with a two-fold increase in fat oxidation in keto-adapted ultra-runners compared to non-adapted athletes (4)

  * The ability to tap into these fat stores are then clearly attractive to distance athletes, as it can allow for this essentially unlimited fuel source to be used, rather than having to continuously ‘top up’ carbohydrate stores.

  * This carbohydrate dependence can lead to extreme dietary requirements for carbohydrate during exercise, which is likely a contributor to GI distress in ultra-endurance competition.

  * Training with low carbohydrate availability induces numerous metabolic adaptations, such as increased mitochondrial biogenesis (5)

  * Power to weight ratio may improve if the diet causes weight loss without a subsequent loss of power output

The above factors could point to potential performance improvements, particularly in endurance-based activities.

However, research on the whole currently presents itself as equivocal, with the majority of research showing no effect following a ketogenic diet on most exercise modalities. This is interesting in itself, particularly in research on high intensity exercise, with a growing volume of research showing no performance impairment following keto-adaptation, in high intensity aerobic exercise (6), and HIIT (7,8).

This is a particularly noteworthy area of research, since it would be expected that exercise conducted at higher intensities be impaired by ketogenic diets, since carbohydrate remains the key fuel at high intensity. Thus, high intensity exercise _should_ be impaired by ketogenic diets, since skeletal muscle adaptations culminate in reduced activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase during exercise.

The finding that keto-adaptation seems to not impair high intensity exercise directly contradicts sport nutrition guidelines, making it a particularly hot topic. There may be however, a degree of nuance to this, as there is a lack of research into elite athletes, who are by nature, outliers. With just one study testing the performance effects of a ketogenic diet in elite athletes, showing a clear performance impairment (9). Whilst these athletes may not have been given sufficient time for keto-adaptation to occur, it is plausible that for many elite athletes, ketogenic diets do not represent an efficacious nutrition strategy.

Events in which ketogenic diets seem to be particularly popular are endurance based. Long duration activities such as ultra-endurance events have sparked great interest due to the desire to spare carbohydrate and access the virtually unlimited fat stores and reduce the need for in race feeding. And with research showing, on the whole, no reduction in performance, ketogenic diets may be an attractive option to those looking tomanage body weight, and increase fat oxidation whilst maintaining performance.

**Does that mean you don’t need ANY carbohydrate?**

Short Answer: If you want to perform optimally, carbohydrate is still king. Acute carbohydrate feeding before exercise is better than none at all when, even when keto-adapted.

Whilst the research to date highlights the fact that at least in sub-elite athletes, a ketogenic diet is unlikely to impair performance following keto-adaptation, it’s an interesting topic that will no doubt be subject to a great deal more research. It’s important to note that at present, high profile athletes/teams known to have utilised ketogenic diets, such as ultra-runner Zach Bitter, and MLS soccer team Columbus Crew, under the guidance of former high performance director Steve Tashjian have in almost all cases, re-introduced carbohydrates prior to performance.

Thus, whilst ketogenic diets remain an exciting tool predominantly in increasing fat oxidation and improving body composition, it seems very likely that acute carbohydrate will boost performance output in these individuals and are thus still are required to optimise performance. The question then comes down to whether living without carbohydrate and adding them in before competition can enhance exercise performance. This has been seldom explored in research, but a train keto, compete carb strategy may present the next area in which the keto diet may be used as a tool to enhance performance.

I hope this serves as an interesting and useful introduction to the topic. All comments and discussion are welcomed!

# My 2026 Supplement Predictions

The supplement industry is valued at nearly [200 billion pounds](https://consent.yahoo.com/v2/collectConsent?sessionId=3_cc-session_9a5af9e2-d233-450b-b667-11dbb6e1f940), so it’s no surprise that when you look on social media, you’re bombarded with supplements being hyped by influencers, scientists and others, often now being paid commission to convince you to buy the new performance or health elixir. 

The sad truth is that most supplements you see advertised will not make you stronger, faster or healthier. In fact, the vast majority of supplements that may work are often the ones spoken about the least.

I’m not anti-supplement at all, but I am pro informed choice. Anyone who has seen my social media page has hopefully gotten that impression (and if you’ve not, then I’ll try to improve for 2026!).

One topic I’ve been pondering over the Christmas period (whilst trying to complete some final PhD corrections and recovering from a nasty bout of COVID that managed to scoop myself, my wife and my 2 year old) is what supplements are about to hit the scene in 2026. I will follow with a post reflecting on 2025 trends, but in short, we saw creatine remarked as a brain food (evidence limited), broccoli sprouts went from unknown to all over social media (evidence encouraging), nootropics (complicated) and a push to increase carbohydrate intakes for athletes (evidence complex).

But typically, the supplements that make their way onto social media feeds will come as a result of prior research, so these things are generally predictable. Now some supplements have minimal research behind them, but I’m writing this as a test of myself in a way. I think by understanding the research on performance nutrition and social media, I can have a go at predicting what supplements will see a big rise in hype and usage across 2026. So here’s my attempt at it.

This isn’t neccesarily an endorsement of the supplements in question, I’ll briefly outline the evidence behind them, but it’s safe to say these are all supplements I’m ‘encouraged by’.

  1. **L-Theanine**

L-Theanine is a non-essential amino acid that’s found largely in tea-plants. There is no evidence that L-theanine can independently enhance performance, and nothing overly new has been published about it recently, so why can I see there being growing interest?

My reasoning is this: there is very strong evidence that caffeine can enhance performance across a large range of exercise modalities, but a substantial number of people suffer from anxiety or jitters when consuming moderate to large caffeine doses. There is pretty good evidence that consuming L-theanine alongside caffeine can allow for a synergistic effect, with co-supplementaition [reducing susceptibility to distraction](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1179/147683008X301513?casa_token=sYSO63JfxD4AAAAA:GWd-g2pW0x8IDUvg7ylTRytnEc5lwPPAqfdaeKyyVg6yU3r14wZujyt4Y6S6uirWT3-pJbkDHyL9ag). And since L-theanine may be anxiolytic, combining with caffeine may help reduce anxiety some experience following caffeine consumption.

The wider point therefore is that 2025 saw the emergence of the nootropic. People want brain supplements, but L-theanine seemed to barely be in the picture. To me this seemed counter-intuitive. Based on my reading of the literature, L-theanine is right up there as one of only very few nootropic style supplements that actually has evidence supporting its effect.

So as a means of benefitting from caffeine with less of the side effects, and as an independent nootropic, I can see L-theanine having a good year.

  2. **Cherries and berries**

**Tart Cherry Juice** and **New Zealand Blackcurrant Extract** have increasingly strong bodies of evidence suggesting they may enhance exercise recovery. This seems largely to be a result of the anthocyanins contained within them. Though recently New Zealand blackcurrants were demonstrated to enhance glycogen storage (Likely because they increase fat oxidation which allows more carbohydrate to be stored.

Both Tart cherry and blackcurrants fit into the category of functional foods, which gives it a leg up. In the world of marketing, natural works. Some (including myself) may find this an endless source of frustration, but it’s another reason that these two supplements could easily take off. 

They also fall into a category that I think creatine mastered last year: Performance enhancer that is also likely good for general health.

Most people aren’t elite athletes. So whilst you may want to improve your performance, something that offers performance and health benefits offers substantially more appeal to the sub-elite athletes than a purely performance based substance (see beta-alanine, sodium bicarb etc). The majority of my audience is 25+, I have recently turned 30 - and I can feel a rise in health consciousness across my age group (though maybe that’s me projecting).

But it allows for supplements to do both. It’s one reason why a supplement like broccoli sprouts is much easier for me to recommend despite limited evidence compared to something like a mushroom cordycep. Whilst broccoli sprouts have much better evidence, they also have a possible health benefit. So even if the performance benefits are minimal, you get something out of supplementation.

Tart cherry and blackcurrant extract obtain most of their benefits due to high concentrations of anthocyanins. Anthocyanins are also being researched across a broad range of health questions, namely around inflammation and all major diseases. This makes them more appealing than many other supplements.

Saying that, I only tend to recommend these to athletes going through periods of super high training loads to support completing the required training, or during a heavy period of matches (imagine a football player over this Christmas period, great time to use such supplements). It’s definitely not a daily use supplement if performance is the goal. 

  3. **Urolithin A**

Urolithin A hits all the key words for the time we live in. It’s a compound produced in the gut, it’s being studied for anti-aging and inflammatroy benefits, and the main mechanism is enhancing mitophagy (improving mitochondrial function by better ridding the body of the damaged mitochondria).

There is a growing understanding of the importance of the mitochondria beyond just the powerhouse of the cell (though endurance athletes are still very interested in that part), but the mitochondria is much mure, it’s seen mroeso as a motherboard now, exerting significant control of the cell. Healthy mitochondria is a vital part of aging and performance. So urolithin A may hit that jackpot of both performance and health enhancing. 

So far the health research is extremely promising, with benefits likely increasing as age increases. So this is already something I would be open to suggesting to older athletes. However data directly on performance is limited. One paper found no performance effects, though it did report improved recoevry and reduced markers of muscle damage. I would suspect that in an older training group, or over time, this may translate into improved performance.

So more research needed (and possible some reliable companies stocking it, as on a cursory glance I would not know who to trust to source Urolithin A right now), this could be one that explodes.

  4. **Personalised data: Carbs, sweat and more!**

Now this final prediction isn’t a supplement per se. It’s about the move to personalised data, which is absolutely a good thing, and exciting. We saw some movement towards personalised carbohydrate intake by the use of 13c tracers to understand individuals exogenous carbohydrate oxidation, and this technology is getting cheaper, so rather than everyone trying to copy the pro’s and get up beyond 100g/hour carbs, may will be able to understand their own carbohydrate oxidation capacity, and fuel specific to their own physiology. 

But this isn’t exclusive to carbs. There’s some really exciting companies doing similar in other worlds. For example, Flowbio have a swear sensor that based on sweat rates and sodium losses each session and by understanding the environment work is performed in, can predict sweat losses for upcoming races based on the type of race and the environment it’s being performed in. This is a really neat technology, particularly for ultra-athletes, and could be a super useful addition to someones arsenal - check them out [here](https://flowbio.com/?srsltid=AfmBOoopV8fVSVbhbCMk8tDVnkaS5M-jRz6q9RNes3KyjDtM7xbS6jXC) to get excited.

**Summary**

So there you have it, my predictions are as follows:

  1. L-theanine

  2. Tart cherry and blackcurrant extract

  3. Urolithin A

  4. Personalised sweat and carbohydrate data to guide fuelling.

These supplements aren’t magic, and inevitably as hype grows it gets beyond the scientific rationale, so be wary of that. But these are supplements I’m pretty excited by within both health and performance.

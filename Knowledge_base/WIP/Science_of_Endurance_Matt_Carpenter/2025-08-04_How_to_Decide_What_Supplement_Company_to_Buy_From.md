# How to Decide What Supplement Company to Buy From

There seems to be new supplement companies spawning every minute, sometimes selling something no-ones ever seen before, but usually selling something that’s already available. 

There are hundreds of places to buy some of the most common supplements. Creatine is a great example, take your pick, there are probably upwards of 1000 choices. Carb gels much the same… so who do you choose?

I’m going to outline I look out for when choosing both a supplement, and a supplement company to buy from:

## 1\. Consider what you’re looking to achieve out of a supplement, and choose something that supports your goals. 

The sad truth is that most supplements do not work. And by work, I mean, improve health and performance. There is a big takeaway here, in almost all circumstances, it’s best to buy individual supplements, rather than combinations of supplements, because the chances are that if you’re buying a product with 8 compounds in, most are doing nothing. 

The best example here is pre-workout. In most cases, pre-workout is a glorified caffeine drink. Almost everything else is filler.

They often contain beta alanine and caffeine, that both may enhance performance, but need to be taken daily, so are often easier to take separately to take the pressure of consuming a pre-workout every day!

So it’s usually better to choose products that are single ingredients that work, rather than big concoctions. This doesn’t mean you should avoid pre-workouts, just be aware that you’re largely just consuming a caffeine drink.

Another example may be a vasodilator such as beetroot juice. Rather than a supplement combining a bunch of compounds, simple beetroot juice is the compound that has an evidence base, so you can just buy the beetroot juice.

## 2\. Being confident your supplements aren’t contaminated

Once you’ve chosen the supplements you' want to purchase to improve your health and performance, it’s time to consider who to buy from. Now the number 1 factor here is to trust that what you’re consuming is what you think it is. Many supplements contain contaminants, they contain substances that aren’t on the label, some of which may be prohibited substances. 

Other supplements may not be dosed correctly, the recent [creatine gummy scandal](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7gLVOGJpSpE) is a great example of this. 

So how can you trust a company? Well there are a number of things to look out for:

  1. Informed sport testing. Informed sport test for banned substances, so if you’re an athlete this is essential. If you’re not an athlete this is still something I would consider highly desirable. No one wants banned substances in their body, you’re buying something to improve health or performance, you want to know what’s in it.

  2. In the absence of informed sport, other third party testing can be effective, sometimes even more effective (when done well), as informed sport will only tell you if there are banned substances. Third party etsting can confirm the exact make-up of a product.

As a note here, when I talk about contamination, this *usually* isn’t a big conspiracy with supplement companies being evil and knowlingly misleading people, it’s much more common that contamination occurs from factories and the production of the product. As a result, transparency in the production process is also a big green flag.

## 3\. Who Deserves Your Support? Promoting Evidence-Based Supplements.

Admittedly this is much more of a personal standpoint that the first two, but I always consider the trustworthiness of the company I’m buying from. There is so much misinformaiton in the fitness and supplement world, so I’m very conscious of trying to support companies that (at least in my view), are doing things the right way, and avoiding companies that I consider responsible for this misinformation.

I won’t give any examples of companies I avoid, but I will give a couple of examples of companies I think are good examples of doing things the right way. This isn’t me saying you should buy from these companies, but moreso, things I like to look out for:

  1. [NutritionX](https://www.nutritionx.co.uk/): NutritionX don’t sell much, but what they do sell has good evidence behind it. I would bet my (admittedly very low) life savings that you won’t find mushroom cordyceps on their product list anytime soon. They also have support from very well established scientists and practitioners, and have informed sport certification.

  2. [Pillar Performance](https://pillarperformance.uk/?srsltid=AfmBOoopJYoRbL2z35FToHrrKhpoCOhn-qpDDx_E-LHcxBcKJNSgobkl): It’s a similar story with Pillar, but with an additional green flag. They have recently advertised a PhD position to investigate the role of micronutrients within endurance performance. This to me is a big green flag, a company actively funding and supporting science and research. Also with some brilliant scientific minds at the company.

  3. [Flycarb](https://www.flycarb.com/?gad_source=1&gad_campaignid=22383902785&gbraid=0AAAAA_IdSwZBxtXz7QTxgBZtEJx1kxbaJ&gclid=CjwKCAjwkbzEBhAVEiwA4V-yqljsNieXksv9C-VOBBjJ2ojXt25G7sGyZ26x6bm3jjtgeD9IU5IOtxoCtMMQAvD_BwE): This is a slightly different one. Flycarb are a hyper specific company, focused solely around sodium bicarb. I see this as a great sign, focusing on one supplement that works, rather than trying to sell anything under the sun irrespectuve of evidence. They’re a small company doing things the right way, having recently gotten informed sport accreditation, which allows you to support a small business whilst being confident about the supplement you’re consuming. 

Again, these are just examples, and I don’t have any financial incentives in any of these companies, I just wanted to use these as examples of what to look out for.

The big takeaway I would hope people take from this is that it’s important to think about what you’re buying and who you’re buying from. Supplement contamination is still worryingly high, as is misinformation being spread by supplement companies.

I started my subtack and social media work to try and counter this, but it’s going to take some serious work to make meaningful change. I’m set on sharing this message, but we can all make an impact by avoiding supporting the companies that are making the situaiton worse, and calling out misinformation.

# Fibre is the New Protein - how to survive the new wave of misinformation

<abstract lang="en">
Fibre Marketing has Begun - Influencers Want You to Believe 1.7 grams of fibre is 'high'.
</abstract>

In October last year, I made a post predicting Fibre would become the new protein, with everything being labelld fibre, since it’s becoming more and more popular to fibremax, and more people are appreciating the importance of a diet high in fibre.

In that article, I argued that this was in part, a good thing. The more people talk about fibre, the more it gets around that focusing on fibre rich foods is important for health. In fact, many athletes do not consume sufficient fibre, which may be one reason there is an epidemic of ‘[fit but unhealthy’ athletes.](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27340616/>)

Well it’s only taken 8 or so months, and I’m now seeing marketing surge with fibre based foods, I have seen good examples (which I will go through here), but first I want to rant about the bad examples I’ve seen, because it’s so concerningly reminiscent of the wave of ‘protein bars’ that contain sometimes less than 10g protein. The classic, taking something good (fibre), putting the minimum amount you’re legally allowed to put in to call it high fibre, then let your marketing team get to work. 

### Ok time to start the rant…

I’m starting to see fibre everywhere on social media, and recently I came across a sponsored advert done by a (usually brilliant) running influencer advertising some high fibre crisps. I looked into them and saw they contained less than 2 grams of fibre, but thought little of it, they’re better than regular crisps and there’s little harm done.

But then another influencer appeared on my feed. One who is known for sharing (usually very good) science based content for runners. And this influencer was doing exactly the same, a sponsored ad for ‘high fibre crisps’, again, containing less than 2g of fibre. 

This is where it became too much. I will discuss below some great examples of companies jumping on the fibre bandwagon and doing it right, but less than 2g of fibre in a packet of crisps, though legally high fibre, is not morally high fibre. And any presumption that these are a health food (which is implied in the advertising) is something that in my view, we should be very cautious recommending. 

The whole point of an evidence-first account is that you’re supposed to be the person who reads _past_ the front of the pack. _Past_ the claims to provide information that people can use to improve health and performance, and better analyse a food packet to see what consumers are being sold. The moment you amplify a flashy front-of-pack number, you’re moving away from science and critical thinking, and over to the world of marketing.

And here’s my slightly smug admission: I called this. Back before Christmas I wrote on here that **fibre would be the new protein**. The nutrient every brand would suddenly discover it had and use to sell you things you were going to buy anyway, exactly like “high protein” ended up stamped on chocolate bars and cereals that were really just unhealthy products with a little bit of damage limitation in the form of protein. I didn’t think I’d be proven right _this_ quickly, but it’s a great example of how marketing works, and how to see through it.

## The crisps in question

The thing setting all this off is a wholegrain snack being promoted hard on the strength of its “High in Fibre” badge. I’m not going to pretend it’s junk. It’s made from ~82% wholegrains, it’s lower in fat than standard fried crisps, the flavourings are simple, and as a straight swap for a normal bag of crisps it’s a perfectly reasonable choice. Credit where it’s due, I have no issue with the crisps and if they were marketed not as a health food, but as a more healthful alternative to standard crisps, then I’d be happy as larry.

But “a better bag of crisps” and “a high-fibre food you should be eating _for the fibre_ “ are two very different claims, and the marketing is quietly nudging you from the first to the second.

Here are the actual numbers, straight off the back of the pack:

  * **Per 100g:** 6.7g fibre

  * **Per 25g bag (one serving):** **1.7g fibre** , 119 kcal

That’s it. One bag, the amount a normal human actually eats, gives you **1.7 grams of fibre**. The recommended intake in the UK is **30g a day** (and the average adult only manages about 19–20g, so most of us genuinely do need more). So one bag of “high fibre” crisps covers a grand total of **roughly 5–6% of your daily target.**

**This is highly misleading, it would be easy to watch a video and think you’re doing a good thing by changing nothing in your diet but adding these crisps, when in reality, is barely gets you closer to your fibre goal.**

## Here’s the clever bit (and why I can’t just call it a lie)

This is the part that is vital to understand, we’re deep in the marketing game here. And one of my goals with my social media postings is to help people see through marketing claims, whether it be from electrolyte companies, carbohydrate compaines or any other supplement company. 

The “high fibre” claim is **completely legal and technically true.** Under UK rules, a food can be called “high fibre” if it contains **at least 6g of fibre per 100g** (or 3g per 100 kcal). At 6.7g per 100g, these crisps clear the bar — just.

But look at _how_ they clear it. The threshold is measured **per 100 grams**. A serving is 25g. **100g of these crisps is four bags.** Nobody is sitting down to four bags of crisps for their fibre, and if they were, they’d also be taking on around 475 kcal and 21g of fat to get there.

And here’s the detail that gives the game away: there are two routes to a “high fibre” claim, per 100g, _or_ per 100 kcal. By weight, these scrape over the line (6.7 vs 6.0). By calories, they don’t even come close: about **1.4g of fibre per 100 kcal against a 3g threshold.** For an energy-dense snack, the per-100g rule flatters it enormously. The badge isn’t measuring “is this a good way to get fibre.” It’s measuring “is there technically enough fibre per 100g of dry weight to print the words.” Those are not the same question, and the brand is counting on you not noticing the difference.

This is _exactly_ what happened with protein. “High protein” on a 200-calorie bar that delivers 9g of protein, while a £0.20 egg gives you 6g — same trick, different nutrient. Fibre is just next in the queue.

## The bit that actually disappoints me

I expect this from marketing departments. It’s their job to find the most flattering true sentence on the spec sheet and put it in the biggest font. Fine.

What I find harder to swallow is **science-based creators lending their credibility to it.** The entire value of an evidence-first account is that you’re the person who reads the back of the pack so your audience doesn’t have to. The moment a “myth-busting” creator amplifies a per-100g claim on a per-25g product without saying a word about the serving size, they’re not busting the myth, they’re selling it.

## What “actually getting your fibre” looks like

Enough criticism, glad to get that one off my chest. Here’s the constructive half, because the thing is (and this could be what these same influencers could mention if they actually cared about people consuming more fibre - please accept my challenge if any of you are listening) - adding substantial quantities of fibre is actually quite easy. 

As a 30 year old fairly boring man, I had to accept a couple of years back that my fibre consumption had slacked massively, my diet was poor and I was no-where near the 30g a day recommendation. So rather than turning to a tiny packet of crisps, I turned to foods that actually contain meaningful amounts of fibre, **beans.**

My go-to (try before you laugh at me) is a **jacket potato with cheese and beans, but with some added chickpeas from the bold bean company, straight into the heinz baked beans.** Depending on how liberal I am with chickpeas (you can add any bean you want here, butter beans also work well), you can get well upwards of 20g fibre in one serving.

  * **Baked potato, skin on (medium–large):** ~5g

  * **Half a tin of baked beans (~200g):** ~7g

  * **Half a tin of chickpeas, stirred through the beans (~120g):** ~8g

  * **Grated cheese on top:** ~0g (it’s there for a good time)

**Total: roughly 20g of fibre in one meal** — about _two-thirds_ of your entire day’s target, off a single plate.

To get that same 20g from the crisps, you’d need to eat **around 12 bags**. 

A few other unglamorous fibre heavyweights worth keeping in rotation:

  * **Porridge oats** with berries and a spoon of mixed seeds — easy 8–10g before you’ve properly woken up

  * **A tin of lentils or kidney beans** thrown into any pasta sauce, chilli or soup — 7–9g almost for free

  * **Fava beans (picture of a packet I have below)** a snack that can genuinely call itself high fibre, 6g per serving. That’s a true health snack!

## How to not get caught out

The fibre flash is going to be _everywhere_ this year: bread, cereal, bars, snacks, drinks. So a quick filter for the next time you see it:

  1. **Ignore the front of the pack. Turn it over and read the “per serving” column** — that’s the number you’ll actually eat.

  2. **Anchor it to 30g a day.** If a serving gives you 1–2g, then don’t avoid it, but don’t rely on it to get you to your 30g.

  3. **Whole, boring foods win.** Beans, pulses, oats, wholegrains, fruit and veg with the skin on will out-fibre any processed product wearing the badge, usually for a fraction of the price.

Fibre genuinely is one of the most underrated things in your diet, and most of us really should eat more of it. That’s _precisely_ why it’s about to be used to sell you everything. Eat the better crisps if you fancy them, just buy them because they’re a nicer crisp, not because a clever bit of per-100g maths convinced you they’re medicine.

I told you fibre would be the new protein. Don’t say I didn’t warn you.

# Seamoss - Is the Social Media Hype Evidence-based?

<abstract lang="en">
Seamoss is being marketed widely, and is even used by some professional footballers. Today I will look into whether there may indeed be benefits to seamoss supplementation.
</abstract>

I can’t go onto social media without seeing an advertisment from a youtuber or health related account telling me that I have to start taking [Seamoss](<https://www.mylasmoss.com/collections/sea-moss-gel/products/sea-moss-gel-340g?variant=39849258123437&currency=GBP&utm_medium=product_sync&utm_source=google&utm_content=sag_organic&utm_campaign=sag_organic&utm_source=google&utm_medium=paid&utm_campaign=18014983629&utm_content=138670216063&utm_term=&gadid=616070408852&gad_source=1&gclid=CjwKCAiAzPy8BhBoEiwAbnM9O9Yuwj6GKFlPK9XIcfEMkEHoEaXE2Ii2pX_tLA7mKVNVqMrjCHZ6JxoCVRQQAvD_BwE>). It’s painfully obvious when these are ads because they use the same tagline about it having ‘[92 essential nutrients](<https://www.tiktok.com/@cheriejohnson75/video/7462855685965942046>)’. I originally assumed this was a standard fad, with money going the way of promotors. But then watching a [Tottenham Hotspur day in the life of Dejan Kulusevski](<https://www.tiktok.com/@spursofficial/video/7459433985874201888>), I noticed that (amongst many other things he and other athletes do with little or no evidence base), 42 seconds into the video he is passed his seamoss, and explains that it has 92 of the 102 essential minerals.

## What does Seamoss contain?

The Seamoss being sold goes by the scientific name ‘Chrondus Cristus’, and is a red seaweed that can be found on rocky shores in the Northern Atlantic. There is minimal research on this supplement, but there have been analysis of the mineral content. Over 20 years ago, [Ruperez analysed mineral content of Seamoss](<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0308814602001711?casa_token=_KRrJPWFk84AAAAA:UIvLKjPiEmfdhCeCEFMpcq4eYsSEW1gWMEWtvYqA7G0jarm2k8rb9C4DI2takTqbHv7HBAQOhRM>), and did indeed find that it was rich in certain minerals. Particularly sodium, but also containing potassium, calcium, magnesium, iron, zinc, manganese and copper (amounts shown in the table below under ‘Red seaweeds - Chrondus’.

What is noteworthy here is that whilst this does show decent mineral content, the amount of sodium is dominant over other minerals. Meaning it would be hard to consume high amounts of, say potassium, without very high intakes of sodium. 

This is a severe issue with Seamoss on first glance, sodium to potassium ratio is correlated to blood pressure, and high potassium intake seems to be something to strive for, Seamoss would not be a good method of doing this.

In addition to this, I find no support for this ‘92 minerals’ claim. It is classic marketing speak. Sodium is a mineral, but few people need more sodium. It’s praying on a lack of knowledge of the consumer, which doesn’t sit well with me.

This doesn’t mean it is useless. A recent publication concluded that ‘ _Chondus crispus_ stood out for its exceptional mineral content, particularly in terms of calcium (51,900 ± 4150 μg/g), iron (610 ± 49 μg/g), manganese (375 ± 30 μg/g), and zinc (113 ± 90 μg/g), making it a significant source of essential minerals. The amino acid analysis revealed _Chondus crispus_ as notably high in both essential and non-essential amino acids, suggesting its potential as a source of complete protein. 

This is encouraging, certainly. But there is still a huge amount of uncretainty over whether this would be beneficial to an already healthy individual. In terms of exercise performance, there is no evidence to suggest that there is any benefit to Seamoss. With regards to general health, research suggests that it does contain high amounts of certain minerals (calcium particularly), that mayb contribute to overall health. But I don’t see why Seamoss would be ones choice to increase calcium, iron or manganese intake. It also contains a lot of other minerals (such as sodium, and more notably, lead), that you would certainly want to avoid consuming in large amounts. So it is much simpler to opt for specific supplements for the minerals in question.

## **Conclusion:**

  
Don’t waste your money, don’t fall for the marketing. 

If more research comes out, I may change my mind. But for now, there is nowhere near sufficient evidence to recommend Seamoss.

# Taurine - the Underrated Performance Supplement?

<abstract lang="en">
Taurine gets very little attention when it comes to supplements to enhance performance, but the evidence is surprisingly strong.
</abstract>

## What is Taurine?

Taurine is an amino acid that’s commonly found in energy drinks, as well as some meats and seafood. It’s the most abundant amino acid in our muscles, and can make up 50-60% of the free amino acid pool.

In the muscle, it supports calcium handling, with calcium being an essential component of muscle contraction. It also has anti-oxidant effects, which have got the world of anti-aging/healthspan pretty excited.

But interestingly, research in mice suggests that preventing taurine uptake impaired endurance performance, leading to the suggestion that taurine supplementation could enhance performance.

So does it… let’s find out.

## What does the evidence say for Taurine

Scientists are still figuring out exactly how taurine helps with endurance, but a few promising ideas are emerging. When taken before exercise, taurine seems to help the body increase fat oxidation and reduce carbohydrate, which gives it real scope for marathons and upwards as a way of slowing down glycogen utilisation. 

Taurine also has an anti-oxidative effect, which may be the mediating factor. It can help protect muscle cells from stress and keep the mitochondria running smoothly, improving ATP turnover. This may lead to better energy output and less fatigue.

Whatever the mechanism, there is pretty good evidence for taurine improving endurance performance. The most comprehensive [meta-analysis](<https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-0896-2>) reported enhanced endurance performance in both chronic and acute supplementation.

Saying this, it’s important to note the magnitude was small, so it’s not a game changer, but it is (in that oh so overused and dubious phrase) a marginal gain.

Taurine isn’t magic, but the evidence above suggests there is scope for performance improvement.

In addition, this effect seems to be greater in the heat, as [it can increase sweat rate](<https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-025-05776-4\\>) (sweating is our bodies main way of maintaining/slowing the rise in core body temperature). 

## How should Taurine be taken?

Taurine can be taken every day, or just taken before performance. Taurine 1 hour before performance at a dose of 1-6g (I would recommend 4g, but data uses a range) is how I would take it based on the data.

You can also take taurine daily, and this can be quite convenient, but acute ingestion is likely more effective for performance.

As a result, I would present two options:

  1. Take 4g 1 hour before exercise

  2. Take 1-3g in a daily drink, then add an extra 3g 1 hour before performance. 

## So Should I Take Taurine?

Firstly, to be clear, this isn’t a supplement that will revolutionise your training, though I’m not sure there is a supplement that fits that criteria. I’m not here to sell magic pills. But based on the data, taurine may enhance endurance performance, and the chance of this greater in the heat, so here in august in the UK, there’s no better time.

It’s also cost effective, and being studied for potential health benefits (again a promising, but fairly understudied area), so the downsides of taking taurine are minimal.

Therefore, taurine is a supplement I often recommend.

But saying this, the Meta-analysis above suggested that taurine taken acutely was as effective as taking chronically, so it’s not like creatine where it must be taken every day. **4 grams 1 hour before exercise is what I would suggest as an evidence based approach.**

You can also take daily, but it’s unlikely to have a performance effect.

If this was as expensive as a beetroot shot or a ketone ester then it would be a different story, but Taurine is very cheap, so from a cost benefit analysis perspective, adding it to a drink daily (I often include it with my creatine) is a fairly inexpensive and easy way to get the potential benefits of taurine. 

Most supplements have no evidence, Taurine does. I would love to see a world where the big supplement companies spend less time marketing mushrooms, magic roll ons and seamoss, and more time on supplements that actually have supporting evidence, and Taurine is one of those.

My slightly conspiratorial side would suggest the low price and lack of hype from the world of supplement marketing are closely related, but who knows….

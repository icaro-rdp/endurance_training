# In Defence of Students

<abstract lang="en">
My day job is lecturing at a university - are today's students lazy and entitled? This article outlines my view.
</abstract>

I want to begin with a caveat, this post will not be related to endurance performance. If you are not interested in my experiences in higher education, then no offence taken, next podcast episode comes out this Tuesday. But this is a rant I have wanted to write for a long time, and what better place than Substack. In the capacity of my day job, as a Sport Science lecturer at the University of Northampton, I work teach undergraduate students, largely aged between 18-21.

There is a familiar refrain that echoes through society, held by many (though not all), often seen on social media, over family dinners and newspaper articles: _students today are lazy_. They lack resilience. They expect everything handed to them. It is often stated that they are a generation of snowflakes.

I see this refrain regularly, and every time I see it, without fail, I find myself thinking of the students I’ve taught since I began lecturing in 2018, and think of the students I teach day to day, who to my mind, are working harder, and longer hours than many (I contend), realise. Not only are they working harder, they are also working under more pressure, and in more precarious circumstances than previous generations. 

So let me make the evidence based case that students and more widely the ‘youth of today’, are as far from lazy as could possibly be imagined.

## They are working more than any generation before them

The most comprehensive annual survey of UK undergraduate experience is the Student Academic Experience Survey, conducted by the Higher Education Policy Institute (HEPI) and Advance HE. The 2025 edition, drawing on responses from over 10,000 full-time undergraduates, found that **68% of full-time students now undertake paid work during term time**. That figure was 56% just one year earlier, in 2024. In 2020, it was 42%. Go back to 2015, and it was 35%.

More students are working, and students are working longer hours. The cost of living crisis doesn’t skip generations, and with substantial changes to university funding, students often have no choice but to word to fund their life.

Does that mean they need to work to fund big nights out and a lavish student lifestyle? No. 16-24 year olds drink [less than](<https://www.statista.com/statistics/369808/alcohol-units-consumed-by-gender-and-age-in-england/#:~:text=In%202024%2C%20men%20in%20England,of%20alcohol%20in%20a%20week.>) those aged 55-74, as [this article](<https://www.qub.ac.uk/student-blog/gb-students/drinking-culture-university.html>) outlines well, students are drinking less than their predecessors. Money obtained from working more than previous generations is being spent on living costs. 

Let that trajectory sink in. In the space of a decade, the proportion of full-time students simultaneously holding down paid employment has nearly doubled. They don’t sound lazy to me.

And these are not token shifts. The HEPI/Advance HE survey for 2024 found that students with part-time jobs were averaging **48-hour working weeks during term time** when their employment was combined with lectures, seminars, and independent study. Students on intensive courses such as veterinary science and dentistry were averaging 56 hours. The 2024 survey reported that full-time undergraduates now spend an average of **42 hours per week** on paid work and study combined, up from 35 hours in 2021. Some subgroups, including students with caring responsibilities and mature students, regularly exceed 50 hours per week.

The HEPI Director, Nick Hillman, noted that students are now dedicating more time to their studies than when the survey first began in 2016, and that the data directly contradicts the popular myth that students are “snowflakes.”

These are not the habits of a lazy generation. And this data fits perfectly with conversations I have with students. I barely come across a student is not working. And many also engage in unpaid internships to further their future career prospects. Far from lazy and unmotivated, students I see are hard working and often developing excellent time management through having to balance studying, working and gaining industry experience. 

## The cost of living is forcing impossible choices

The reason so many students are working is not lifestyle inflation or a desire for luxuries. It is survival. The UK’s cost-of-living crisis has hit students with particular severity, and the data is stark.

A 2024 survey by the National Union of Students (NUS) of over 6,500 students found that **14% had used a food bank** in the 2023/24 academic year — double the 7% recorded in 2021/22. A separate survey by the Russell Group Students’ Unions found that 25% of students regularly go without food because they cannot afford it. The NUS reported that 62% of students who work do so specifically to afford food and bills. Three quarters were cutting back on socialising because they simply could not afford to see friends.

The Office for National Statistics confirmed the picture in its own student cost-of-living survey, with 92% of students reporting that their cost of living had increased compared to a year ago, and 91% were somewhat or very worried about rising costs. 78% were concerned that financial pressures would affect their academic performance. A third had taken on more debt, cut back on meals, and were using university buildings as warm banks to limit energy costs at home. Student loans simply don’t cover the costs of living and studying.

## A generation shaped by COVID-19

The “lazy student” narrative conveniently overlooks one of the most significant disruptions to education in modern history. Today’s undergraduates are the young people who lost substantial portions of their secondary education to lockdowns, school closures, and remote learning. Their social development, their transition to independence, their confidence in academic environments was disrupted at a crucial developmental stage.

The All-Party Parliamentary Group for Students described the current cohort as having experienced the “double misfortune of educational disruption from COVID and now the cost of living crisis.” This is not an excuse. It is context. And what it shows me is rather than a ‘lazy’ or ‘entitled’ generation, what I see is an unbelievably resilient generation. 

Despite all the reasons not to (money, COVID, student debt) they have come to university. They have enrolled, they have engaged, and according to the HEPI data, they are studying harder and longer than their pre-pandemic predecessors.

## The system, not the student

When I hear people describe students as disengaged or entitled, I want to ask: have you looked at what they’re dealing with? A student who misses a 9am lecture may have finished a warehouse shift at 2am. A student who submits work that is below their ability may be doing so on three hours of sleep, with £12 left in their bank account until their next loan instalment. I heard a student recently describe their desire to become a teacher. When told about the long hours teachers face, they responded by saying they currently have to wake up at 3am to work. Being a teacher comparatively a goal to achieve work live balance!

The Sutton Trust found that 49% of undergraduate students have missed classes specifically to do paid work. My experience reflects this (from experience, I would not be surprised if the number is higher). The NUS found that among those reporting a negative impact on their studies from employment, the most common reasons were tiredness, juggling commitments, and simply having less time for studying. 

If students are struggling, the explanation lies not in their character but in the structural conditions we have created for them.

## A concluding thought

Every generation has been called lazy by the one before it. But the difference now is that we have data, and the data tells a clear story. Today’s students are working longer hours, under greater financial pressure, with poorer mental health, and after more disrupted schooling than any comparable cohort in recent memory. They are not entitled. They are not fragile. They are, by any reasonable measure, remarkably resilient and hard working.

The question is not what is wrong with our students. It is what is wrong with the system that expects so much of them and provides so little in return.

Anyway, I should really finish my PhD now, rant over.

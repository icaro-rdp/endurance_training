# The sodium paper that electrolyte companies don't want you to read

<abstract lang="en">
What the best synthesis of the sodium evidence says about when electrolytes matter, and when they don't
</abstract>

The electrolyte industry has had a very good few years. Sachets in every gym bag, a 1000 mg sodium hit sold as the baseline dose, and a marketing line that has quietly become orthodoxy: that plain water doesn’t really hydrate you, and that you need salt, lots of it, to stay on top of your training.

It is a tidy story, and a profitable one. Interestingly like so much, it’s origins lay in the low carb/keto movement (this is something I’ll discuss another time, but it’s interesting and means I’ve been seeing electrolytes pushed for almost a decade!), but it’s a classic case of marketing hype getting ahead of science, so let’s discuss the science of sodium (and electrolytes).

The problem is that the science underneath it is much less certain than the marketing suggests. In 2025, Alan McCubbin published a review in _Performance Nutrition_ called [“Sodium intake for athletes before, during and after exercise: review and recommendations.”](<https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s44410-025-00011-9>) McCubbin did his PhD on exactly this question, sodium in endurance athletes, and the paper is the most careful synthesis of the evidence we have. It’s open access and avaliable here, if you’re into reading papers, it’s worth your time to read it - but if not, I will be summarising it below.

Its conclusions are awkward for a product category built on the premise that everyone needs a lot of sodium, all the time, and I suspect very few of the people selling you electrolytes have read it. If they have, they’ve done a good job hiding it!

So here’s what the McCubbin paper outlined:

## The reframe that changes everything

The central argument is deceptively simple. Sodium is not a standalone need that you top up like a fuel tank. What matters physiologically is the relationship between sodium and water, not the absolute amount of sodium you lose or replace. Your body does not regulate total sodium so much as it regulates the concentration of sodium in your blood, and that concentration is a ratio of sodium to water.

This single point puts a lot of what the marketing world tells us about electrolytes in doubt. I’m sure you’re familier with the electrolye pitch: you lose X milligrams of sodium in sweat, therefore you must replace X milligrams. McCubbin’s review makes clear that this is the wrong model. Sodium losses on their own tell you very little. What matters is whether sodium and fluid are moving together in a way that keeps your blood concentration stable. A fixed milligram target, divorced from how much you are actually drinking, is solving a problem you may well not have.

## You don’t need more sodium day to day

Start with the simplest claim. There is no good evidence that athletes need a higher daily dietary sodium intake than anyone else, even if you’re sweating more sodium out than the average sedentary person who’s not exercising. Your kidneys and your sweat glands between them are very good at conserving sodium when you need to and dumping it when you don’t. The notion that you must replace every milligram you sweat out, every day, with a supplement, does not survive basic scrutiny with renel physiology. Most athletes already eat more sodium than they need from food alone, something I discuss at length - we already consume too much sodium. The recent trend of daily electrolyte supplementation, taken regardless of whether you have trained, looks far more like a marketing success than a physiological requirement.

## Loading before a session: mostly pointless

If you are already well hydrated, drinking a salty solution before a normal training session does little for you. Pre-loading sodium has a narrow rationale in very specific hyperhydration protocols before long events in the heat, but for the everyday athlete heading out for a run or a ride, it is unnecessary. You start euhydrated, you do not need to manipulate your blood volume with salt to get there.

## During exercise: the claim with the least evidence

This is the part the industry would least like you to dwell on.

When McCubbin went looking for studies on whether taking sodium during exercise actually improves endurance performance, they found five across the entire literature, that’s not very many. The doses ranged widely, none of the studies were conducted in hot conditions, and not one of them matched sodium intake to an individual athlete’s measured losses. Of those five, a single study reported a benefit, and that result was so large, an improvement of around twenty-six minutes in a half-distance triathlon, that it is very hard to credit to sodium rather than to the messiness of testing performance in a real race. Strip that one outlier out and what we see is a very small body of not overly convincing research.

So the two pillars usually wheeled out to justify sodium during exercise are cramp and hyponatraemia. Both are not as clear cut as you may think.

Cramp first. The old story is that cramp is your muscles screaming for electrolytes. The better-supported explanation, built over more than two decades of work led by Schwellnus, is that exercise-associated cramp is a problem of altered neuromuscular control. It comes from muscle fatigue and disordered signalling between nerve and muscle, not from a salt deficit. Prospective studies of athletes who cramp do not show the electrolyte or hydration differences the depletion theory predicts. Chasing cramp with salt tablets is, for most people, treating the wrong thing.

Then hyponatraemia, the genuinely dangerous condition where blood sodium falls too low. It sounds like the perfect argument for salt. It is not. The consensus position is that exercise-associated hyponatraemia is overwhelmingly a problem of drinking too much, a relative excess of water diluting the blood, rather than a problem of losing too much sodium. And here is the uncomfortable implication: the modest amount of sodium in your sports drink or sachet is nowhere near enough to offset the dilution from serious overdrinking. The fix for hyponatraemia is to not overdrink. Salt is not your insurance policy against bad fluid decisions.

If you’re exercising for prolonged periods, sodium is absolutely important, but the amount you’re getting in your sports drink will in most cases, suffice.

## The sweat test you probably don’t need

One of the cleverest parts of the review is what it does to the personalised sweat-testing concept. This is a really popular movement right now, and one that I do find interesting. The pitch is very good: get your unique sweat sodium concentration measured, then replace your personal losses precisely. McCubbin lays out the idea and arrives at an inconvenient conclusion for many. For almost everyone, outside genuine ultra-endurance, sweat composition testing is unnecessary and adds no useful information to your strategy. Field sweat tests are also methodologically fiddly and prone to inaccuracy, so you may be paying for a precise-looking number that is not even reliable. For the vast majority of athletes, a simple rule of thumb does the job that an expensive test claims to.

## When sodium during exercise genuinely matters

This is not a piece arguing that electrolytes are a scam. They are not. They are being sold to the wrong people for the wrong situations.

Sodium during exercise earns its place in a specific remit: when you are exercising for a long time, replacing a large proportion of substantial fluid losses, especially if that fluid is plain water. Think long durations, several hours and up, high sweat rates, hot conditions, multi-day efforts, and people who genuinely lose a lot of salt in their sweat. In those scenarios, taking on sodium alongside your fluid helps keep your blood concentration stable and reduces the risk of dilutional hyponatraemia. If you are an ultra-runner or a long-course triathlete drinking heavily across many hours, sodium is part of your plan. If you are doing a forty-five minute tempo run, it is not.

## After exercise: the one clear win

If there is a single use of sodium that the evidence fully supports, it is recovery. When you rehydrate with plain water after a sweaty session, you dilute your blood, switch off your thirst, and send a chunk of what you just drank straight to your bladder. Add sodium to that fluid and you hold onto more of it, restore your blood volume more effectively, and rehydrate properly rather than just topping up and peeing it out. This is the part of the electrolyte story that is real and well established. It just happens to be the part the marketing talks about least, because “useful for post-session rehydration in some situations” sells fewer sachets than “you are dangerously under-salted right now.” So if you’re in peak training weeks, adding sodium to your drink after a tough session may be useful, and in fact, it’s likely more useful than sodium consumed during a session.

## Key takeaways for endurance athletes

  1. Stop thinking in milligrams of sodium. Think about the ratio of sodium to the fluid it travels with. Sodium losses on their own do not tell you what to do.

  2. For sessions under roughly an hour to ninety minutes, you almost certainly do not need added sodium. Water is fine.

  3. You do not need to load sodium before a normal session if you start well hydrated.

  4. Cramp is not a salt deficiency. It is a neuromuscular fatigue problem. Salt tablets are not the fix.

  5. Hyponatraemia is an overdrinking problem. The sodium in a sachet will not protect you if you drink too much. Match your drinking to your thirst.

  6. You probably do not need a sweat test unless you are doing ultra-distance work.

  7. The clearest evidence-based use of sodium is after exercise, with fluid, to rehydrate properly. This is the win most people overlook. But again, after a 45 minute jog it’s probably making minimal difference.

  8. Sodium during exercise genuinely matters when you are going long, in the heat, with high sweat rates, replacing a lot of fluid over several hours. If that is you, plan for it. If it is not, it is mostly noise.

## The honest summary

McCubbin’s review is not a hit piece on electrolytes and neither is this. But this is an arguement for sticking to research and not getting carried away by marketing.

Sodium is useful in a narrow set of situations that most recreational and even most competitive endurance athletes rarely find themselves in, and that the industry has taken a real but limited physiological tool and sold it as a universal daily need. The science did not change. The marketing simply ran a long way ahead of it.

There is one group for whom this picture genuinely shifts, and that is low-carbohydrate and ketogenic athletes, whose sodium and fluid handling really is altered. That is a different conversation, and one worth its own post, because it is also where a lot of the electrolyte narrative quietly came from in the first place.

For everyone else: drink to thirst, salt your food, save the sachets for the long hot days and the recovery afterwards, and keep your money for race entries.

Puresport are advertising mushroom codyceps in the following way:

It combines organic Cordyceps Sinensis mycelium extract with key bioactive compounds to enhance VO2 max, ventilatory threshold, and metabolic efficiency. With Informed Sport certification, it's safe for athletes seeking marginal gains in performance.”_

And thankfully, they actually cite the supporting evidence for this. They cite the following two studies:

  * Cordyceps and VO2 Max: "Effects of Cordyceps on VO2 Max and Endurance Performance.  
Authors: Yi, Xi-Zhen, and Jia-Shi  
Published in: Chinese Journal of Integrative Medicine (2004).  
Outcome: 37 healthy elderly adults showed a 7% increase in VO2 max after 6 weeks of Cordyceps supplementation.

  * Cardiovascular Improvements with Cordyceps: "Cordyceps Militaris and its effects on VO2 Max.  
Authors: Hirsch, K.R., et al.  
Published in: Journal of Dietary Supplements (2016).  
Outcome: A 10.9% improvement in VO2 max among younger adults consuming 4g of Cordyceps Militaris for 3 week

So since I want to focus on evidence base for this newsletter, I dug into these papers to see whether Mushroom Cordyceps are all that they seem. If they really do improve VO2max as these studies suggest then they would absolutely be worth using to enhance performance 

To cover it briefly before we discuss the mushrooms, VO2max is the maximum rate at which the heart, lungs, and muscles can effectively use oxygen during exercise, and is used as a way of measuring a person's individual aerobic capacity. Your muscles need oxygen to create energy (ATP) to allow the working muscles to work. The more oxygen you can get to the working muscle, the more energy you can generate, so an increase in VO2max is definitely something we should be interested in... Let’s dive in.

## Mushroom Cordyceps - the verdict

To put it simply, I am not convinced. And that is putting it diplomatically.

Looking at the studies they cite, Hirsch et al. (2016) does show an increase in VO2max over 3 weeks, but the study methodology leaves me with a lot of questions. This newsletter will be a bit of a rant above research methodology, I hope this helps equip you to read research in the future, or just to feel my anger as i has to read these. So, the Hirch paper…

They had 28 participants split into a mushroom group and a placebo group. After one week there was a significant increase in VO2max…. in the placebo group. Yes, you read that correctly. The placebo group increased their VO2max in a week, yet no change in the mushroom group. This leaves me with a lot of questions around the original VO2max testing (it should be very hard to improve VO2max in a week if you did a proper test and then took nothing but a placebo. 

But them a ‘subset’ of participants continued on the placebo or the mushrooms (4 participants on placebo, 6 on mushrooms), and after 3 weeks, they showed significant improvements in their mushroom group. But there is no clarity on the previous VO2max of these participants, or why they carried on. And these groups are far too small to draw meaningful conclusions from.

In short, I’m not a fan of this research, and if I’m going to spend £30 on something, I need better evidence. 

**But lets see if the second study can win me over.**

Now the Xi-Zhen, and Jia-Shi does seem at least, a slightly more well controlled study, however it’s now 20 years old, and without wanting to bore the you with more discussion on research methodology and statistical analysis, the analysis conducted here seems slightly sketchy. Take this analysis:

_“VO2peak was increased by 5.5% in CM (p=0.003), but by only 2.2% in P (NS). Exercise time to VO2peak was longer in CM (+5.4%, p=0.047), but no change in P. TMW was reduced by 29 sec in CM (p=0.05), but slightly increased in P (+19 sec, NS). WO was increased by 2.8% in CM (p=0.033), but fell in P (-5.6%, NS). RER was reduced by 2.1% in CM (p=0.018), but no change in P. Diastolic blood pressure was reduced by 3.2% in CM (p=0.045), but no change in P.”_

The first thing I see here is lots of P values very slightly under 0.05 (and one that is 0.05 exaclty!). 0.05 is the cut off for ‘statistical significance. This can happen, but can also be a result of ‘p-hacking’ - this being the **the manipulation of data analysis until it produces statistically significant results. We see a 0.05, 0.047, 0.045, 0.033 - lots of values at, or just below the cutoff. This doesn’t pass the sniff test. And (I say, trying to suppress my rage), it gets worse…**

On top of this, the researchers compare VO2max at the 12 week point to the starting point. This is not the correct method of analysis. They should be comparing changes in VO2max between the placebo group and the mushroom group. We can see that both groups increased their VO2max, and my bet is that if they compared groups against eachother (the correct statistical practise), there would be no effect observed. But just comparing to timepoint 0 has given them a significant result.

Basically, if I put their study into a table, they did this:

Placebo week 0 —> placebo week 12

Mushroom week 0 —> mushroom week 12

When conducting research like this, you are comparing mushroom to placebo, so when analysing your result, you compare mushroom week 12 to placebo week 12 to see whether there has been a sigincantly greater change in one group comapred to the other. This helps prevent order having an effect (aka, people may just be fitter 12 weeks after the start of a study, maybe they’re training for a marathon, maybe they’re motivated because they’re doing a study, etc). 

But even though they have a plaecbo group, the authors focused on analysing this bit: 

Mushroom week 0 —> mushroom week 12

With no factoring in of the placebo.

**This is bad science 101** (ps, if you want another book recommendation, [Bad Science by Ben Goldacre](<https://www.worldofbooks.com/en-gb/products/bad-science-book-ben-goldacre-9780007284870?gad_source=1&gclid=CjwKCAiA-Oi7BhA1EiwA2rIu2yTMCMfNY22g3cU-p0iY6k-MDi2WwGNkHQ-5KO1DT7T_atT0uyRtsRoCyUkQAvD_BwE&sku=GOR001643095>) is a classic).

This does not mean that Mushroom cordyceps do not enhance performance, but I hope this blog shows that there is not any rigorous evidence to support their benefit. Unfortunately, research conducted to date is of poor quality with questionable methods and analysis practices.

Sorry for the short statistics rant, but unfortunately these sorts of questionable practises are very common, and it’s really important to understand these to prevent misinterpretation of findings. 

Doesn’t mean they don’t improve VO2max, but I’m not holding out any hope. With the research as it is, **don’t waste your money.**

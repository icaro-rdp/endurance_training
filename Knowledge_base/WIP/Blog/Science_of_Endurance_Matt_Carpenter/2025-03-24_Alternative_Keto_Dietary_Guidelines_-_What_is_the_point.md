Now this presents a number of questions. One is regarding the healthfulness of this way of eating. As someone who researches ketogenic diets I see them as a potentially healthy method of eating, but of course this does not mean all carbohydrate restricted diets are created equal. To me, this outline presented above is scientifically illiterate. These are the reasons

  * You can restrict carbohydrate without being meat and dairy heavy

  * To have low sugar fruits so high on the list is ludicrous. 

  * There is no one way of eating a low carbohydrate diet

  * There are minimal plant-based options. This reads more like a ‘meat-based diet’, rather than ketogenic.

  * Who is this for? Athletes can consume more carbohydrate - there is a lack of specificity.

Anyway, I won’t rant about this anymore, though I would be interested to hear any comments.

Rather than evaluating the health impacts of this, what really drew my attention to this article was the overwhelming urge to say ‘what is the point’. Even if this article was a clear outline of how to eat a healthy ketogenic diet (which I don’t think it necessarily is), why does an alternative eatwell guide need to be published like this by a number of ‘Independent authors’. I don’t really see the value in the eatwell plate at the best of times, but when I read a paper like this, I just want to say ‘can’t this be a blog post’.

I see no value in this in the scientific literature, and it highlights something I see becoming more and more prominent, the idea of science as clickbait. As someone who’s slaved away over a PhD for 8 years, looking at these sorts of papers baffles me. It’s attractive to a journal as they know it’ll get clicks (not the most useful of metrics to focus on mind), but I don’t see how this adds anything more than a simple blog on ‘what to eat on a ketogenic diet’. There are so many ways to formulate a low carbohydrate diet, making this guide essentially useless, but it’s also not science.

I understand the role of opinion pieces in science, but I’m not even sure this would come under this. There is no unique perspective offered. It’s pure clickbait.

They cover myths about ‘carbohydrates being essential’, to be clear, this ‘myth’ was busted in the 1960’s with the Cahill studies, and not the idea of carbohydrates not being physiologically essential is a phrase drastically overused to try and prove the benefits of a ketogenic diet. It doesn’t prove that in the slightest, and is a great example of a poor scientifically illiterate argument. It’s the sort of misleading I expect from politicians, not scientists.

**To be clear, just because something isn’t essential, doesn’t mean it isn’t beneficial.**

So that was a short rant about this paper. You can read for yourself [here](<https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/17/6/1047>). I would love to hear your thoughts on it, agreement and disagreement are always welcome!

## Preface

I never wanted to write a book. I am not a writer. I don't have any interest in selling anything. All I was trying to do is share what was working for me in a small corner of the internet. Most people were not all that interested in what I wrote. I didn't invent a new way of training. All I did was explain why I thought that a validated cycling training technique may work for running. Many people said it wouldn't work. It resonated with a few people. Those people watched my progress and had progress of their own. In the small corners of the internet where people share ideas, training techniques that work tend to get shared. The success people started to have using this training approach combined with the unexpected race results I had at the age of 41 have led to more attention than I expected. Most of the methods and techniques I am going to describe in this book are already out there on the internet. Unfortunately, the information is divided among countless forum posts, Strava responses and Reddit wikis. For some people that is enough. For others I put everything down here.

This book will not be a running guide. It is not an autobiography. It is a book explaining the rationale behind a training system that has worked to get me from barely breaking 30 minutes in a 5k to 2:24 marathon over the course of 4 years. I will explain the rationale through stories, as they will be easier to recall when you conceptualize the key principles for yourself. As I have said, I am not here to sell you on anything. I only hope to provide you with some key concepts that will allow you to train consistently and effectively to your physiologic potential.

If you are reading this because the book was recommended, it is probably because it worked for someone. No training plan is generalizable to everyone, but my hope is that in the words here you will be able to find some useful concepts. With those concepts you will be able to start stacking your training together in a way that allows you to stay healthy and perform at your best. I will never advocate that this is the best training system; I just know it will work for most people and maximize the training load for most hobby joggers over an extended time horizon.

Lastly, while I always try to respond to queries and have done a few podcasts to try and clarify this system, I have a limited number of hours each week. I work full time teaching the plasterers of tomorrow and still need to train. In an attempt to create a resource to communicate my ideas in the way they need to be communicated, I have decided that a book is the best medium to inform the most people. I will still answer questions as much as I can on Strava, but I hope this book will provide a good starting point for those interested in training this way. With any luck you will find something useful in these pages. In the worst case scenario, it was a fun exercise among friends.

## Prologue

Why write a book? It’s a question I grapple with as I type these words. This was not part of the plan when I started running. But over time, I have realized that there are insights worth sharing with a wider running community. These insights are surprisingly absent from most resources. The insights are for those who, like me, treat running as a passionate hobby and want to maximize their potential with limited time.

This book is not for complete beginners nor elite runners in the top 1%. It is for the rest of us. The dedicated hobbyists who log hours each week. The ones that scour running forums or pick up books to improve their times. If you’re expecting quick fixes or miracles, you might want to put this down and call a wizard instead. What I lay out is a sensible, structured framework based on my own running journey. A framework you can adapt to your own needs. Whether you’re chasing a 22-minute 5k or a sub-3-hour marathon, this book will empower you with the knowledge and skills to train effectively long-term, without relying on a coach.

The principles here have worked for a wide range of runners, ranging from masters rolling back the years, to those posting personal bests beyond their expectations. I've connected with many of these runners through quirky corners of the internet. They can tell you this isn’t about overnight transformations but about building consistent, sustainable progress toward your running goals.

My own journey started elsewhere. I was first a time-trial cyclist. I won some local races, crashed often, and eventually retired. Sometime after, I turned to running. I progressed well, hitting a sub-19-minute 5k but then slammed into the proverbial brick wall. Googling “sweet spot running” led me to the wild world of LetsRun.com. Initially I lurked. Eventually I posted, thinking I had something to contribute. That moment changed my life. I cannot tell you, even to this day, if that's a good or bad thing.

The ideas I posted gained traction and the thread swelled to frankly ludicrous levels (410 pages and 8000 replies at the writing of this book). At the same time my running progressed. By implementing the ideas I have written about, I’ve clocked a 15:01 5k, a 30:41 10k, and—against all my earlier vows—a 2:24:08 and 55th place marathon debut at London in April 2025. Not the obvious trajectory for a stagnated 41-year-old 18minute 5k runner using a cycling training plan! The combination of heretical training ideas combined with my better-than-expected performance gains have led to somewhat of a viral response in a niche part of the internet.

## Foreword

When I first heard about James Copeland's work, I was intrigued. Here was a former time trial cyclist, a hobby jogger in his early forties, who had taken something similar to what we developed in Norway and reimagined the principles for a completely different audience. What fascinated me was that James had already discovered many of these concepts independently through cycling. I knew that time trialists, particularly with the use of power meters, had long understood the value of sustained subthreshold work, training just below functional threshold power, accumulating time in that zone without the fatigue penalties of harder efforts.

He was bringing that cycling wisdom, refined through years in the saddle, into the running world: His cycling background gave him the analytical tools and disciplined approach to recognize what mattered most in the Norwegian method and what could be adapted.

When I developed the double threshold approach and the Norwegian method of distance running years ago, I discovered the extraordinary potency of subthreshold training. Working systematically just below lactate threshold proved far more effective than the traditional mix of (too fast) easy runs and hard intervals that dominated the sport. The Ingebrigtsen brothers later demonstrated this approach could produce world champions. But it is very intensive and demanding: two threshold sessions daily, constant lactate monitoring, and a commitment level beyond what most runners could sustain. It comes with a very large upside but also with a risk. It was never designed for the parent juggling work and family, the masters runner returning to the sport, or the time-crunched amateur seeking sustainable improvement.

James saw this gap and did something remarkable: he simplified without diluting.

The Norwegian Singles Approach preserves what matters most in my view: controlled subthreshold work that builds lactate clearance and metabolic efficiency, easy running that truly stays easy, and the discipline to avoid the "grey zones" that destroy so many runners at all levels. By removing the second daily session, eliminating the need for lactate meters, tuning down the overall intensity and creating clear guidelines around training load, James has given runners a plan they can actually sustain year after year, regardless of their level within the framework of elite performance.

What makes subthreshold training so potent is its precision and repeatability. Too easy, and you're leaving adaptation on the table. Too hard, and you accumulate fatigue faster than you can recover. But when you hit that sweet spot consistently, the zone cyclists learned to target with power meters and runners can find through disciplined pacing, the body responds with remarkable gains in efficiency. Your threshold rises. Your race paces become more sustainable. The effort that once felt difficult starts to feel manageable.

In addition, by focusing on (and defending) a system that avoids the common mistakes of rapid change and too fast progress into higher intensity work in the training cycle, the NSA guides runners to dramatically increase the chance of staying injury-free and therefore higher consistency over time. This patience and the willingness to progress gradually and disciplined, is perhaps the hardest lesson for ambitious runners to learn. I personally believe this is underestimated, now a hobby runner myself. James has remained humble throughout, actively engaging with the community, answering questions, and continuing to learn. This is how science progresses: Someone identifies a powerful principle, proves it works at the elite level, and then others find ways to democratize it.

James has done that brilliantly.

## Backstory

### Beginnings

Let us revisit the beginning. Why did I start running? What frustrations with traditional approaches led me to develop a more unconventional way of thinking? As you read on, you will discover that my approach is not as unique as some claim. However, the approach is rather unique in its presentation of a practical structure and sensible way for many runners to optimize their time. This guide is not intended to be a piece of academic research, nor is it likely to meet the approval of academics or coaches—I can already imagine them rolling their eyes. Instead, it’s a helpful resource for everyday runners like me. This is the guide I wish I had on my first day of running. Even if just a few readers find it valuable, it will have been worthwhile.

My journey began long ago with cycling. I can’t quite recall why I started, other than as a practical means to commute. Over time, those commutes evolved into leisurely rides lasting an hour or two. Then a friend convinced me to compete in a time trial. The UK time trial scene is peculiar, yet strangely addictive once you are immersed. The clothing is unconventional, the bikes resemble futuristic concepts from Blade Runner, and the helmets look like they belong in a circus. Yet, there is remarkable creativity in the training approaches. These training breakthroughs are often driven by middle-aged, balding enthusiasts who embrace the sport with an unmatched passion.

Time trial cycling events are categorized into 10, 25, and 50-mile distances, with some mad men tackling the frankly ridiculous 100-mile challenge. A useful parallel to note is that the durations of the 10, 25, and 50-mile time trials roughly correspond to the aerobic demands of the 5k, 10k, and half-marathon in running. While not exact, these similarities are critical and worth considering as we progress through this book. Time trialing differs markedly from cycling road racing where performance is often dictated by short, high intensity efforts that break from the peloton. From a racing perspective, time trialing shares closer similarities with running, where athletes aim to maintain a consistent effort over a set distance at a relatively even pace.

A year after starting, I was fully immersed. I invested in my first power meter, bought a dedicated time trial bike, and was actively seeking out knowledge from online forums and resources. I purchased Training and Racing with a Power Meter by Andrew Coggan, consuming as much information as possible. Like many who discover a new passion, I was a sponge, eagerly soaking up every detail. This is common across my hobbies—whether it was darts, where I aspired to emulate beer drinking heroes of the 1990s, or snooker, where I dreamed of replicating the genius of Ronnie O’Sullivan (incidentally, an impressive runner with a sub-17-minute 5k, something I was lucky enough to chat to him about when I played him in an exhibition), I'm in it to reach my potential and cycling was no exception.

Anyone becoming familiar with cycling training will quickly learn about the concept of training load. This topic dominates discussions within the time trial community. Training load quantifies the effort of a ride by applying a mathematical formula based on power output and time. It serves as an estimate of how demanding a given activity is relative to an athlete’s capabilities. Regardless of the mathematical model used, it’s critical to emphasize that the training load is just an estimate. While it may not perfectly capture an athlete’s performance or potential, it provides a valuable framework for understanding and managing training efforts.

My initial foray into understanding training load was basic but eye-opening. In simple terms, Andrew Coggan’s book explains that training load quantifies the cumulative training stress required to build fitness, improve power output, and enhance race performance (Coggan & Allen, 2019). The load must be appropriately balanced with recovery to avoid overtraining. This approach yielded improvements at first, but without a structured training plan, I soon hit a plateau. It was my first encounter with the concept of stagnation. Reflecting on it now, I realize I fell into the classic trap of riding hard without any kind of plan. This left me perpetually fatigued and unable to progress. Sound familiar, runners?

Luckily, I stumbled upon the Time Trial Forum. This was a goldmine of training insights. It was there that I first encountered real world use of "sweet spot" training, a major buzzword and trend at the time. Clueless about what it meant, I dove into researching the concept. I found what remains to this day the clearest explanation I have come across:

“Sweet spot is a specific intensity zone for building endurance and improving power output whilst having the ability to recover in a short time frame”

Training Load

By riding hard all of the time, I had already completed a significant amount of "pure threshold" work, which refers to training at or above the second lactate threshold (LT2). At this intensity, lactate begins to accumulate rapidly in the blood, marking the upper limit of a sustainable effort. Runners will likely be familiar with this intensity. It feels manageable at first but becomes grueling quickly, especially during training. Importantly, going over threshold will also have a drastic impact on how fatigued you are the next day, which directly influences how much work you can do in the coming days/weeks. In terms of training load, threshold work produces a substantial score. For instance, training at your threshold for an hour generates a Training Stress Score (TSS) or fitness score of 100.

TSS = duration (hours) × Intensity Factor (IF)² × 100
Intensity Factor (IF): Relative intensity compared to threshold intensity. At threshold effort, IF = 1.0
TSS = 1hour × (1.0) 2 × 100 = 100

I refer to this as training load, but the original standard was outlined by Andrew Coggan and is Training Stress Score (TSS). Moving forward, we will use the term "fitness score," which follows (for as good as we need to know) the same equation and is the metric employed by the excellent free platform Intervals.icu. In order to represent your fitness score across time, most platforms will use a time weighted average to give you a single number. This time weighted average typically weights the most recent workouts the highest and will average them over a given time period. Performance Management Charts (PMCs) like Intervals.icu and TrainingPeaks typically follow a 42-day weighted average because it closely represents the fitness gains and decay windows seen physiologically. In addition to being based off of physiologic responses, it also allows for smoothing out short term fluctuations. I highly recommend exploring Intervals.icu once you have a solid understanding of the core principles and are ready to apply them to your own training.

![Intervals.icu](image_rsrc51C.jpg)

Intervals.icu

While threshold training provided a good amount of training stimulus, it was believed that it didn’t provide a “good bang for the buck” or “more juice from the squeeze” like sweet spot training did. What did this really mean? Why does everyone have to talk in training riddles? It was like trying to decode the meaning from Nirvana or The Doors. Just like the lyrics of Jim Morrison or Kurt Cobain, the training lingo was intriguing but often left me puzzled, searching for clarity amidst the mystery.

We don’t need to delve deeply into the specifics of cycling training here, but some concepts transcend sport type and will prove relevant later. The ultimate claim is that three weekly sessions of sweet spot training will provide a greater fitness stimulus than two weekly sessions of pure threshold work. Sweet spot training is defined as approximately 87–94% of Functional Threshold Power (FTP – which is the power sustainable for roughly an hour), balancing high training stress with manageable recovery (Allen & Coggan, 2019). A typical threshold session for a cyclist might involve two 20-minute intervals at FTP. A typical sweet spot session might involve three 15-minute intervals at 90% of FTP with 5-minute recoveries between efforts, or four 10minute intervals at 92% of FTP with 3-minute recoveries.

These formats allow cyclists to sustain high workloads while keeping fatigue manageable. While you might only typically fit in two threshold sessions a week, you could comfortably fit in three sweet spot sessions in the same 7 days because they are easier to recover from. Sweet spot training was appealing because it allowed me to accumulate training load more rapidly. The slightly lower intensity still generates a high training stimulus while allowing for more frequent sessions. Additionally, recovering from three sweet spot sessions feels significantly easier than recovering from two threshold sessions per week. This is likely because the lower intensity reduces overall physiological stress and fatigue, enabling quicker recovery between workouts. This approach became my training pattern for months on end.

Over time, I got curious. What other ways could you train to generate the same fitness score?

I discovered that achieving a fitness score of, say 80, consistently yielded similar improvements in FTP and performance. This was achievable regardless of how I reached that training score. Amongst the cyclists who experimented with different approaches to increasing load, we found that the specific composition of training was less critical than the overall load. I suspect that approximately 90% of the training effect stems from the cumulative impact of the load, rather than the structure of the sessions themselves. For example, a study by Sanders et al. (2019) found that in 15 well-trained cyclists there were strong correlations between cumulative training load (measured via Training Stress Score, session-RPE, and TRIMP) and improvements in cycling performance, specifically power output at 2 and 4 mmol·L ⁻¹. Studies like this support the real-world observations, showing that total training load was the predominant driver of fitness gains, with the structure of sessions playing a less critical role (Sanders et al., 2019). For myself and others, running follows a similar principle.

The fitness score proxy was tested during my cycling days when an injury sidelined me to easy indoor cycling at a low to moderate effort. With time off work, I rode up to 15 hours a week, often while watching Breaking Bad. I was pedaling easily and fueling myself with candy while Walt and Jesse pedaled their own blue candy. I amassed one of my highest-ever fitness score peaks doing this. Remarkably, within a week of returning to regular cycling, I set a 20-minute peak power personal best. I was able to do this despite only training at easy to moderate intensity. Though this was an inefficient and time-intensive way to accumulate training load, it highlights that the total load, rather than the training’s composition, is the dominant factor in driving fitness gains. This likely explains why marathon runners frequently achieve personal bests during their training blocks. The cumulative effect of the training load elevates their fitness to new heights. (If they survive it! More on that later.)

It is crucial to emphasize that your fitness score is entirely personal and not a reliable metric for comparing performance across individuals. A fitness score is not an indicator of who is faster or stronger in a direct comparison. For example, Person A with a fitness score of 40 could easily outperform Person B with a score of 60 in cycling or running, as the score reflects individual training load rather than relative ability. This distinction is vital to understand before discussing or comparing scores with others. Your training load holds no real world bearing on competitive performance between individuals. Instead, the fitness score is a tool for tracking your own training load. It signals when you’re increasing your efforts or need to push further volume to achieve your personal goals.

The true value of sweet spot training lies in its efficiency. It maximizes the impact of training load while minimizing time and fatigue. Unlike my inefficient approach of slowly grinding out 15 hours a week on an indoor bike in front of the TV, I discovered I could accumulate the same fitness score in roughly 10 hours with sweet spot training. I did this by incorporating three (sometimes four or even five) sweet spot sessions per week. Complemented by easy rides, I could recover effectively and build a substantial training load with the best use of time. This experience solidified a principle I continue to support: the overall training load is the dominant driver of fitness gains, far outweighing the specific composition of the workouts.

As mentioned, you can achieve similar fitness outcomes and roughly the same FTP by training either 10 hours per week with sweet spot rides or 15 hours a week with only easy to moderate efforts. However, we live in a busy world with responsibilities like work, family, or just life. You are also spending time with loved ones, watching your favorite sports team disappoint you yet again, or enjoying an evening at the pub. Very few of us have the time for extensive training schedules. Training isn’t our job, nor should we treat it as one. For me, the choice was clear: sweet spot training was the most efficient approach, and I was eager to see how far it could take me.

I achieved considerable success in cycling by dedicating several years to progressively building my fitness and steadily increasing my training load to boost my power output. This approach was largely linear: the more I trained, particularly with consistent sweet spot sessions, the fitter I became. For approximately four years, I stacked sweet spot training month after month, continually adding intensity and volume (as well as stacking marginal aerodynamic gains, I could write another book on that!). It is important to note again that training load only serves as an estimate of the effort exerted during an activity relative to an athlete’s capacity. While not intended to be a precise measure of long-term physiological changes, it effectively reveals valuable patterns and trends in an individual’s training. This is especially true when considering the methods we will discuss, where our training approaches and behaviors remain relatively consistent from week to week, month to month, and year to year. This consistency makes it easy to determine when more is more, when more is not more, and when you might need to adjust by incorporating other training methods.

Sirpoc's Sidebar: Training Load Score

This will be the first of a few sidebars throughout the book, with the purpose of digging further into some of the ideas that have shaped how this training looks. Whilst they aren’t essential learning to make the most of this training, they offer the reader the chance to dive under the hood in more depth, if they choose to do so. They might even be something you come back to at a later date.

In this one, we will discuss Training Load Score (TLS). In order to accurately depict load over time, we need to establish a way by which we can find the load imparted by each individual training session. This is commonly done using the below method. We find a running equivalent of "Intensity Factor" by dividing the speed of the run by the speed of our lactate threshold, and then multiply that by the time, and 100 to simplify the value from a decimal to a whole number.

![Training Load Score](image_rsrc51E.jpg)

There are many ways to establish a training load score. Some ways include rate of perceived exertion (RPE), excess post exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC), and, as previously mentioned, TRIMP. Each of these approaches works in a different way, and will yield different analyses of your current fitness and fatigue. This will be discussed later. TRIMP, for example, works by assigning a weighing factor to a variety of zones (e.g. Z1 might have a weighing factor of 1, Z2 is 1.5, Z3 might be 2.5, etc.), multiplying the amount of time spent in each zone by the weighing factor, and finding the sum.

Getting Started with Tracking Training Load

Tracking load by pace made the most sense for me. The cool, flat and temperate climate I live in minimizes external variables compared to heart rate monitoring. You have to remember that heart rate can be influenced by numerous factors such as time of day, hydration, caffeine intake, and temperature (among others). My recommendation is to select either pace or heart rate for tracking training load and maintain consistency with it throughout your training going forward. This ensures reliable and comparable data tailored to your own progress. The goal is not to compare training load with others but to monitor your individual development from point A to point B over time. If you opt for heart rate, it’s critical to accurately determine your lactate threshold heart rate. To date, the most effective method I've ever seen for this remains the Friel test.

Although I chose pace as my primary metric for tracking training load, I recognized the value of getting serious about my training and decided to conduct the Friel Lactate Threshold Heart Rate (LTHR) test. The Friel LTHR test helps you to determine your lactate threshold heart rate on a practical level and it is something that I think makes sense for everyone to do. I strongly advise against relying on simplistic formulas, such as the outdated 220-minus-age calculation for maximum heart rate. Instead, take the time to accurately assess your LTHR for practical and effective training purposes.

Lactate Threshold Heart Rate (LTHR) Test

This test is also referred to as the Friel Lactate Threshold HR Test for the coach who popularized it. You are going to want to do this test in ideal temperature conditions and find a course that is relatively flat and with minimal wind. You are going to warm up for roughly 15 minutes. You are then going to run a 30-minute time trial on your own. The main goals are to 1) run as fast as you can over those 30 minutes and 2) keep the pace relatively constant throughout. During your 30-minute run, hit the lap button on your watch after 10 minutes and at the end of the 30 minutes. After you pick yourself up from the ground and catch your breath, you will review your average heart rate over the last 20 minutes of the run. That average heart rate over the last 20 minutes will approximate your LTHR.

For the purposes of this discussion, the focus remains on keeping training as practical and applicable as possible. Your lactate threshold heart rate (LTHR) represents the effort level you can sustain for approximately 40 to 60 minutes in a race setting. This will depend in part on your fitness level and race conditions. The pace or heart rate you maintain during the final 20 minutes of a solo 30-minute time trial serves as a reliable indicator of your LTHR intensity. Research by Gavin et al. (1998) supports this, concluding that heart rate during a 20-minute field test in runners correlates highly with laboratory-determined lactate threshold, validated through blood lactate measurements (Gavin, 1998).

This HR closely aligns with the effort you could sustain in a longer race, such as a 10k or half marathon, when factoring in the benefits of tapering, adrenaline, and other race-day dynamics. In my experience, this correlation has consistently proven accurate. If you are being extra cautious and want to give yourself a safer buffer zone, I recommend using 96-98% of the last 20 minutes from the Friel test. For example, my LTHR is sometimes 98% of my average HR in the last 20 mins of a 10k. In future training sessions, we are looking to never exceed LTHR outside of racing. For that reason, there is no harm in a slightly more cautious estimation when starting out. It is preferable to run every run under LTHR, rather than over.

A critical point to emphasize is that I recommend using a chest strap heart rate monitor for accurate results. Having tested various options, I have found wrist-based monitors to be inconsistent and unreliable. This is often due to factors such as poor skin contact, motion artifacts from wrist movement, or variations in blood flow in my, quite frankly, pathetically skinny wrists. When they function correctly, they provide the needed data but inaccuracies could compromise the entire test. A test like this isn't anything you want to have to repeat. An alternative to chest-based HR monitors is an optical armband monitor, which offers improved reliability over wrist-based devices. Some people (myself included) find that they can be more intrusive to wear or lead to arm rashes. The arm’s softer tissue provides a better surface for optical sensors to read blood flow and heart rate, though it may still exhibit slight lag compared to a chest strap. Nevertheless, an armband is a suitable option for this purpose if you have specific reasons for avoiding a chest strap.

Sirpoc's Sidebar: Fitness, Fatigue, and Form

The origins of load tracking can be traced back to the 1970s, where Banister proposed the idea for an Impulse-Response Model. The model was later refined in the 1990s. The revised model applied a training effect score to two different time periods to attempt to account for the acute effects of fatigue and the long-term fitness gains. These were called a negative and positive training effect, respectively. The positive training effect represents the training load accumulated over a longer period of time 𝜏1 that contributes to your fitness. The negative training effec t represents the training load accumulated over a shorter period of time 𝜏2, that contributes more to the fatigue you feel on any given day. These are put into an equation that represents performance on day t. You begin with your performance on day 0, add the cumulative positive training effect (weighted using an exponential expression), and subtract the cumulative negative training effect (weighted similarly, but with different constants). The constant k acts as a weighing constant, e is another mathematical constant, t represents the current day you are on, and n is the term that indexes through the previous days.

![Pt](image_rsrc51J.jpg)

This concept is refined for the purpose of tracking individual fitness and fatigue values. Below are the equations used in their simplified form. The Training Load Score (TLS) from days closest to the present day is weighted most heavily in the equation. For example, when i = 0 (the current day), the 41/42 term is taken to the power of 0, which is equal to 1. When i = 1, the 41/42 term is taken to the power of 1, and just equals itself. With each subsequent increase in i, TLS is multiplied by a new, lower factor, so each day further in the past is weighted less heavily. The same goes for fatigue, only on a 7-day cycle instead of 42. Once the cumulative sum is found, it is averaged over a period of 42 or 7 days, to find fitness or fatigue, respectively. Then there is form, which is simply the difference between your fitness and fatigue.

### Plateau

By late 2022 I had shaved some more seconds off my personal best, but my running journey was hitting a rough patch. This is probably relatable to many. I increased my weekly training volume through the winter, but the process was becoming increasingly challenging. There was a pattern: I could sustain solid training for two to three weeks, but fatigue forced a letting up by the third or fourth week. This took the form of an extra rest day, a shortened easy run, or left me feeling dreadful by Tuesday despite a day off. Experimenting with a true recovery run on rest days didn’t help. I hit a plateau—a frustrating brick wall. This boom-and-bust cycle is textbook: a few weeks of increased training load yields gains, only for a forced down week to erode much of that progress. I was left treading water. This cycle is a common pitfall in standard training plans, especially for recreational runners. These training plans often mimic the intensity and structure of elite athletes, who rely on a massive aerobic base to absorb such demands. For us mortals, that foundation is difficult to build. I was painfully learning the limits of my resilience.

When considered in isolation, a traditional training approach isn’t overly demanding. For instance, a workout like 5-6 x 1k at 5k pace is something most runners could tackle tomorrow. Similarly, a steady tempo run, perhaps rated around 6-7 out of 10 on the RPE scale, isn’t a race effort. However, over time, the cumulative impact of these sessions takes a toll on the body. This raises some important questions. How necessary is this constant state of fatigue? Could we be capturing aerobic gains without it?

Even the easy or moderate runs, which I’d rate around 3-4 out of 10 on the RPE scale, prompt skepticism. Is there truly a meaningful benefit to these compared to very easy runs which prioritize recovery at a 2 out of 10 effort? The more I examined the logic of this approach, the less it made sense. It became increasingly difficult to justify this paradigm of training.

My suspicions were confirmed when I reviewed my fitness score. While it is not the definitive measure people assume I treat it as, it generally provides a reliable snapshot of my current ability. This is particularly true when my training follows a consistent pattern week after week. By the first couple of months of 2023, my score remained nearly unchanged from late summer, despite intense training and feeling the cumulative impact of these demanding sessions on my body. Many runners likely share this experience at some point. A troubling thought arises: How is this going to be sustainable long term? Especially when requiring more frequent days off in order to recover.

Two questions nagged at me. First, had I reached the peak of my athletic potential? While possible, I found this unlikely. It seemed improbable that someone could fully exhaust their potential with just five hours of weekly training. Second, how am I going to progress if I am barely recovering from this training load? I struggled to see how I could progress further using the standard training framework most recreational runners follow—two quality sessions, easy/steady mileage, and a semi quality long run. These approaches left me feeling constantly fatigued and quite frankly, trashed, both physically and mentally.

I ran a 5k in 18:50 again. At this point, I had exhausted all my physical energy and willpower just trying to improve. This was peak frustration. To add insult to injury I was passed and then distanced in the parkrun by someone dressed as a leprechaun. I seriously contemplated quitting. What would I take up next? Perhaps cross-country skiing? The lack of snow on the south coast of England would pose a challenge. I also enjoy darts and snooker, but the endorphins from aerobic exercise are too important to me. The best mental states I have experienced in life are linked with hobbies that make me sweat and allow me to let off steam. This is probably the reason most of us enjoy running.

### Sweet Spot Running

It was at this point that a serendipitous conversation with an old friend shifted my perspective. He was working as a cycling coach and asked for advice on helping a client improve their time trialing. I suggested a basic, medium-term, stable program of sweet spot training to gradually push the client’s FTP up from below. ### Sub Threshold, LT1 and LT2

I dove back into Bakken’s writings and revisited posts from some brilliant minds on the cycling time trial forum (timetriallingforum.co.uk). As we touched on before, there is a strong consensus that sweet spot training is highly effective for several reasons. Outside of the aspects already mentioned, sweet spot training is common when using lactate guided training. Those testing lactate levels found that this intensity range keeps the body in a subthreshold state, where lactate production is elevated but remains below the second lactate turning point. It is a point at which the body can still clear lactate efficiently, avoiding the rapid accumulation that leads to fatigue. This “state” is critical. There is no precise pace or power. This state varies slightly depending on the manipulation of intensity, duration, and rest. These hobby cyclists experimented on themselves like lycra-clad mad scientists. They argued that achieving 90% of the benefits of a threshold workout three times a week with quicker recovery is a smarter long-term strategy than two full-threshold workouts delivering 100% of the benefits but leaving you with significant residual fatigue. That is where the name sweet spot comes from – it is the sweet spot between stimulus and recovery. Cyclists aren’t bound by the mechanical stresses of running and can often manage sweet spot sessions more than three times a week and still recover.

Lactate Thresholds

The first lactate threshold (LT1) and the second lactate threshold (LT2) are physiological markers that indicate shifts in exercise intensity and energy metabolism during aerobic activities. Lactate is a fuel and byproduct of carbohydrate metabolism that rises during intense exercise. The concentration of lactate rises in the blood when production exceeds clearance. Lactate can also serve as an energy source for muscles and other tissues (Brooks, 2012). “Lactate levels” refer to blood lactate concentration, measured in millimoles per liter (mmol/L). It reflects metabolic stress. Higher lactate levels indicate greater metabolic stress and physiological cost to the body’s energy systems (Faude et al., 2009). Below I am going to explore these terms in more detail. To do that, it is necessary to simplify some of these concepts to better communicate. I am knowingly sacrificing some accuracy in language and nuance in order to bring as many readers along in the discussion of a complex topic.

Lactate Threshold 1 (LT1), has many phrases to describe it: the aerobic threshold, first lactate turnpoint, or first ventilatory threshold (VT1). This threshold is the point at which blood lactate levels begin to rise above baseline or the resting lactate concentration. Typical ranges are 0.5–1.0 mmol/L above baseline (Faude et al., 2009). This threshold or turning point signals a shift from predominantly fat-based energy metabolism to increased carbohydrate utilization. At this intensity, aerobic activities start to feel noticeably less easy, and fatigue begins to slowly accumulate, though it remains manageable. Depending on the zone system people are talking about, LT1 marks the threshold between Zone 2 and Zone 3 in a 5-zone model or Zone 1 (easy) and Zone 2 (moderate) in a three-zone model.

Lactate Threshold 2 (LT2) has many names and approximations as well: the anaerobic threshold, onset of blood lactate accumulation (OBLA), second lactate turning point or maximal lactate steady state (MLSS). This threshold marks the intensity at which lactate accumulation accelerates significantly, and the body’s ability to clear lactate is limited (Faude et al., 2009). At this point, carbohydrate metabolism dominates, and fat oxidation—the process of breaking down fats to produce energy—decreases sharply. This is due to the body’s preference for faster carbohydrate-based energy production. Aerobic activities above LT2 feel challenging, and fatigue accumulates rapidly. A historical marker for LT2 is a blood lactate concentration around 4.0 mmol/L, though this can vary (typically 3.5–5.0 mmol/L) based on individual physiology. For that reason, absolute numbers have largely been abandoned (Faude et al., 2009).

For moving through this book, understanding these thresholds is key to optimizing training. While understanding the concepts is critical, knowing exactly where your thresholds land or at what lactate they occur is not necessary. By keeping easier aerobic sessions below LT1, we can minimize fatigue and support recovery (Seiler, 2010). Training in our sweet spot, just below LT2, enhances endurance while still managing fatigue effectively. In the training approach laid out in this book, we are essentially looking to promote long-term progress through a sustained, gradual increase in training load. Our understanding of these somewhat arbitrary markers can help us shape our training, assess its impact, and better interpret the physiological demands we place on our bodies. As George A. Brooks notes, “The lactate threshold is not a fixed point but rather a dynamic response, varying with training state, exercise mode, and measurement protocol” (Brooks, 2012), highlighting the context-dependent nature of these thresholds.

![Identifying the Range of Training Intensity Based on a Lactate Curve](image_rsrc51T.jpg)

Description: LT1 represents a point at which your body’s demand for energy begins to outpace the capacity it has to produce energy exclusively through “aerobic” means. This results in a lactate level that becomes elevated above baseline. LT2 serves as a mathematical estimate for maximal lactate steady state, the fastest pace you can run (above LT1) where, given that intensity remains constant, lactate will remain roughly stable (with very little upward drift). Beyond LT2, you are far exceeding your body’s ability to recycle the lactate being exported from the muscle cells, and, in most cases, will be incurring a significantly greater metabolic strain.

One last concept that is important to understand is that this curve can shift. In fact, when you exercise, you are trying to “shift” your curve to the right. Practically, what you are trying to do is increase the speed or pace at which your LT1 and/or LT2 occur. This causes a shift to the right when the x axis is a measure of speed, and hence where the term “shift” comes from. For example, if your LT2 initially occurs at a pace of 4:00 min/km and you train diligently, your LT2 at some later time point may now occur at a pace of 3:55 min/km. Your body has adapted and now can sustain a faster speed at the same physiologic cost. That is how we get faster. You can shift your LT2 pace up by training below, at, or above that actual speed of LT2. The physiologic costs of training below, at, or above LT2 are not the same and are non-linear. This means that the cost of training above LT2 carries a higher recovery cost and injury risk when compared to training below.

Sirpoc's Sidebar: Lactate Step Tests

If you have access to lactate testing, it can be a very good way to hone in on what effort corresponds with various lactate levels. While not necessary, it can be helpful and insightful for some. Some people already have a good sense of effort and find success jumping right into testing sessions without a step test. However, a lactate step test can be helpful in establishing your individual numbers, since not everyone will have a maximal lactate steady state that occurs at 4 mmol/L. Some people would struggle to train at 3 mmol/L every other day for sub-T sessions, and could be overtraining if they aim for it as an arbitrary number.

To perform a lactate step test, begin with a thorough warm up. This will help prevent any anomalous readings where your lactate is elevated the first couple steps before coming down. Then perform steps of roughly 5 minutes in length, starting at or a bit faster than your current easy run pace. This pace should certainly be below LT1. Then progress by ~0.4-1 km/hr steps for another 5 minutes. Smaller steps are more precise, but you don't want to progress so slowly that the run becomes long and you end up overly fatigued for the final steps. Ensure there is no sweat in each sample, as this can result in an elevated lactate value.

There are a variety of methods you can use to determine your LT1 and LT2. LT1 is sometimes called the aerobic threshold, and is the first step at which lactate becomes significantly (maybe ~0.5 mmol/L) elevated above its minimum value. Some people pin LT1 at the first rise from the low point, and others pin it at 0.5mmol/L above the low point. It just depends on how conservative you want to be about determining where your LT1 or first lactate turning point is.

Lactate threshold 2 can be determined via a variety of methods (see below), but a common method to use is Dmax. For Dmax, you fit a polynomial to your data, and draw a straight line from the minimum lactate value to the maximum lactate value. You then find the point along the curve that is furthest (perpendicularly) from that straight line, and use it as your LT2. You could also simply eye the point at which the curve begins to really spike, and aim to remain below that value in your sessions. If you have trouble interpreting your curve, the Strava or Subreddit groups have many knowledgeable and helpful individuals. When testing during sessions, you should find that your lactate after 10-15 min is about the same as your lactate after 30 min. If there is a significant increase, you have overshot the correct effort. It can take some time to really hone in, but can be useful once you do.

The main challenge I faced when I started this process was that I did not know where my lactate threshold (LT2) was. I had no idea where to start. Fortunately, I had the opportunity to experiment on myself for a couple of months. The intention was to test my lactate in order to create guidelines so I could later drop the lactate testing. As odd as that sounds, my primary focus was to invest some time tinkering with this now, so that down the line I didn't have to think about it.

By determining my LT2, I could reverse-engineer the paces I needed to run for specific durations. These durations, combined with appropriate rests, will generate just enough lactate to elevate my second lactate threshold from below without exceeding the threshold. This approach avoids the excessive fatigue that comes with crossing that line. In essence, the goal is to maximize the aggregate workload, pushing as close to the edge as possible without tipping over. This needs to be done in a sustainable and repeatable way, using pace as a proxy.

This is what many refer to as the “Norwegian Singles Method” or at least how it’s come to be understood. In addition to naming the system, the internet has also supposedly decided I was the creator. While I already explained that this training method is common in cycling, I am at best someone popularizing it in running. In essence the system is consistently training at the brink of maximal manageable workouts without ever plummeting into the abyss of overtraining. Ideally doing this week after week, month after month, perhaps even year after year. All I have done is provide a framework and structure for people to work from, building the foundations from the ground up, opposed to scaled down elite training programs less suited to hobby runners.

I had several unsuccessful attempts at a lactate step test, during which bystanders might have mistaken me for someone in medical distress or, more likely, a class A drug user attempting to emulate John Travolta’s iconic portrayal of Vincent Vega in Pulp Fiction. Eventually, I was able to complete a test successfully and concluded that my results were in line with the old textbook standards with a LT2 of 4.0 mmol/L. For those unfamiliar, a lactate step test involves incrementally increasing exercise intensity while measuring blood lactate levels to determine the point at which lactate accumulates faster than it can be cleared, indicating the lactate threshold (LT2). While 4.0 mmol/L is so average it used to be the standard, the spread of LT2 can vary significantly, with values ranging from 3.0 mmol/L to 4.5 mmol/L. This finding was particularly useful because 4.0 mmol/L is a standard reference point in exercise physiology literature, making it easier to compare my results with established studies. A question remained: what should my starting point be?

A structure of 3, 6, and 10-minute intervals appeared to be the most logical approach. These durations aligned with discussions around double sessions and lined up well with my cycling training experience. I aimed to follow a sustainable 75/25 split of easy versus subthreshold intensity. For consistency, we will refer to this as subthreshold intensity for running moving forward. My weekly training volume totaled around 5 hours. Using 8x3-minute, 4x6-minute, or 3x10-minute subthreshold intervals effectively recreated the 75/25 easy to subthreshold split. The 10-minute intervals, being slightly longer but less intense, offered a format that retained the benefits of a traditional tempo session while breaking it into more manageable and recoverable segments. At the time, easy running was still not my primary focus; that could be addressed later. My main objective was identifying a pace proxy that could consistently maintain lactate levels below 4.0 mmol/L and determining if this was repeatable.

In theory, I could run 6-minute repetitions three times a week at the same intensity. However, varying rep lengths keeps sessions dynamic, challenges adaptability, and provides the body with the mechanical stimulus and sensation of different paces. It does this, all while targeting the same subthreshold levels.

## The Method

### Determining the Pace

I started with 3-minute intervals, targeting my 10k pace, for no other reason than “why not?” This process wasn’t based on data but rather a trial-and-error approach. I was aiming to match the intensity of cycling FTP which seemed realistic for 3-minute intervals. My approach drew hope from Kristoffer Ingebrigtsen, the older brother of Jakob, who trained effectively with limited hours compared to his professional athlete siblings. However, I found Kristoffer’s easy day intensity a touch too fast given his HR data. I also thought his subthreshold intensity was slightly conservative, as he wasn’t running doubles. With only three workouts a week, I could afford to push subthreshold intensity a touch more by decreasing the easy days intensity. This strategy maximizes the benefits of limited training time.

I still vividly recall the first experimental workout to this day. I felt a surge of excitement, almost like race-day nerves, though I’m unsure why. It was just me, the necessary gear, and ideal running conditions. Another point worth mentioning is that I deliberately chose average and favorable conditions for testing. This was not only to replicate real-world testing conditions but also to establish a “normal” baseline, allowing me to adjust paces based on variations in less favorable conditions or terrain in the future.

The first experimental run was somewhat chaotic, akin to Diana Ross’ missed penalty at the 1994 World Cup opening ceremony. A dog chased me, I forgot to lap my Garmin twice, and my heart rate suggested I was above my target lactate by the final repetition. My lactate measured around 4.9 mmol/L, far from the sweet spot or subthreshold effort I had hoped for. This confirmed that even my 10k pace, around 3:53/km at the time, was quite demanding. This also highlights the potential recovery challenges and cumulative fatigue from consistently running at 5k pace or harder week after week. In hindsight, this experience clarified why I had been pushing myself into a performance rut which halted my progression.

From there, the process became more straightforward. At the time, “Tinman” (ironically notably known from LetsRun discussions two decades earlier) provided a calculator with equivalent paces, scaling up paces every 2–3 km. I used this to guide my next attempt, targeting a slower 12k pace. This session went far more smoothly. It felt noticeably easier and by the final repetition, my lactate level was approximately 3.5 mmol/L. I experienced an invigorating sensation, reminiscent of successful sweet spot cycling sessions—challenging yet manageable, leaving me with the desire to do more. If forced at gunpoint to choose between listening to a full Maroon 5 album or doing yet another additional rep, I could likely manage one more without too much difficulty, perhaps two. This session sparked a strong sense of confidence that I was on the verge of solving the larger puzzle of the training approach.

I conducted further testing later that week. I experimented with repetitions at 15k pace, which produced even lower lactate levels of around 2.5 mmol/L. In another session, I repeated the 15k pace, yielding lactate levels of 3.5 mmol/L, comparable to my initial 12k pace. Finally, I tested the 12k pace again, which resulted in lactate levels in the middle of the initial 12k and second 15k range. All of these tests fell within a lactate range of 2.5–3.5 mmol/L. My heart rate didn’t perfectly correlate with lactate levels, but the pattern was undeniable. My first repetitions finished 10–15 bpm below LTHR, and by my last repetitions, my HR was close to, but still below, LTHR. This HR pattern has remained reasonably consistent over time. This was an incredibly rewarding realization, as I had pieced together something deceptively valuable. I could now confidently perform 3-minute repetitions at a targeted pace without needing to measure lactate. I now knew at what pace I was likely within the desired lactate range.

It’s worth noting that I experimented with shorter, distance-based repetitions early in my training, specifically 20x400m reps targeting my 10k pace. While these reps fell within the 2.5–3.5 mmol/L lactate range, the cumulative fatigue carried into my easy days became noticeable. As a result, these shorter reps incurred higher fatigue costs, making them less appropriate for my long-term goals. I haven’t included reps at or near this pace in a long time. I suspect the physical stress of running at faster speeds, even with lactate levels in the 2.5–3.5 mmol/L range, contributes asymmetrically to fatigue. I simply decided that the risk-reward ratio wasn’t justified. That said, I’m not suggesting others avoid this approach—this is merely my experience and perspective.

I followed a similar process with the 10-minute repetitions. By this point, the approach was manageable. I selected a starting pace, experimented for about a week, and reached a similar conclusion. In my testing, pace seemed to correlate better with lactate than HR. I was able to consistently achieve a lactate range of 2.5–3.5 mmol/L across multiple runs using a theoretical 25–30 km pace. This reinforced my confidence in targeting this lactate band without needing exact HR-to-lactate patterns.

Six-minute repetitions were performed at a pace roughly equivalent to half-marathon (HM) effort, targeting a lactate range of approximately 2.5–3.5 mmol/L. Again, there was no direct correlation to heart rate, other than remaining below LTHR by the last repetition. Heart rate is not without its uses. HR can be valuable for tracking long-term patterns like a decreased heart rate at a given speed. This trend often indicates improved performance and readiness to race a new PB. Outside of that, HR provides limited insight within the context of a single session.

Guidelines work broadly, but these pace bands may not be suitable for everyone. If a pace feels excessively challenging, it is likely too intense. You are the one performing the workouts, so it’s crucial to be honest with yourself. I cannot overemphasize the importance of being honest with yourself about your fitness and efforts. When starting with the pace guides listed below, begin at the slower end of the spectrum. Some individuals have reported that even these paces feel too demanding, so do not hesitate to scale back further if needed. It is far better to exercise caution, particularly when you are still finding your footing, than to push too hard and risk accumulating excessive workout fatigue. Excessive fatigue is the opposite of our goal.

Being honest with yourself means not only avoiding the temptation to stubbornly push too hard but also accurately assessing your current fitness level. I strongly recommend using current fitness by running a race or time trial if you don't know where you are at, as covered earlier when discussing setting threshold pace in Intervals.icu. For practical reasons, a 5k is often the best option. Avoid that critical mistake of assuming your current fitness matches a personal best set nine months ago.

Lastly, I want to emphasize that you do not need a lactate meter to train using this method. The whole reason I created these guidelines was so I didn't need to test lactate again. The overwhelming majority of my fitness gains have come from subthreshold sessions where I never checked lactate. That being said, don't let that stop you from getting one if you think having a lactate meter might be helpful. Most people who I know that test lactate have confirmed that these pacing guidelines put them in the appropriate subthreshold range.

Rest Between Reps

The rest intervals between repetitions were kept relatively consistent throughout testing: 1 minute for the shortest rest and up to 90 seconds for the longest. In my experience, the precise duration of rest is not critical. I experimented with varying rest periods, and it does not warrant extensive discussion. The key point about rest intervals is their role in enabling us to run at higher speed while maintaining lower lactate levels. These speeds often exceed traditional threshold paces, but are manageable because of the rests. A study by Seiler and Sjursen found that 60–90-second rest intervals allowed runners to sustain high-intensity efforts with manageable lactate levels, supporting this approach (Seiler, S., & Sjursen, J. E., 2004). The brief rest allows us to quickly return to the desired intensity when resuming running, yet it is sufficient to prevent lactate accumulation from becoming excessive by the end of the set. As with all aspects of training using this method, it’s about striking a delicate balance, one that is essential to understand and apply effectively. Think about it this way: If your first rep is a little too fast, the rest gives your body a chance to reset, clear metabolic byproducts and enables you to complete the remainder of the workout without incurring too much fatigue. In a long tempo, if you over-pace, those metabolic byproducts and lactate accumulate, leaving you drastically more fatigued by the end of the same session. A similar 30 minutes of work with rests has a safety mechanism in place to allow you to complete these sessions 3 times a week, week in week out.

In both cases, the athlete works a bit too hard from minutes 5-10, and lactate begins to rise above the goal for the session (~2.5 mmol/L). In both cases, the athlete slows down in an attempt to salvage the workout. The athlete running the continuous tempo manages to keep lactate from getting too crazy, but lactate is drifting upward, and they will almost certainly be experiencing a greater strain from the work and struggle more to recover in the following days. In the latter case, by splitting things up into intervals, the athlete allows lactate to come down significantly, before then returning to sub-T intensity, this time at the correct effort/pace. The short rest pauses maintain a similar stimulus while also making it easier for the athlete to reign in intensity when accidentally exceeding the target intensity. Many athletes also find that the pauses help to give the muscles a break and reduce the time needed to recover in the following days.

Since my initial testing, I have adjusted the rest intervals between repetitions in most of my running over the past couple of years. The chart shared (Table 1.3) outlines the updated rest periods alongside the corresponding pace bands. I generally prefer the longer rest durations among those I’ve tested, finding them suitable in nearly all situations. How you choose to take the rest is your decision. Personally, I prefer to stop completely for a moment, then walk and perhaps jog lightly to build back up to speed when my watch signals the start of the next set of repetitions are due to start. Ultimately, the rest period is yours to use as you see fit. Don’t overthink it, and do what feels right for you. You don't get any meaningful extra fitness for cutting them short or jogging. Additionally, for those concerned that you won't be able to hold paces for longer distances like the half-marathon, rest assured. Taking these breaks does not lead to people having issues with stamina in racing.

Session Warm Up

Another point worth briefly addressing is my warm-up routine for training sessions. I keep it simple: a 10-minute easy jog to get the legs moving and ease into the session. No strides or additional elements. The cool-down follows a similar approach: a 7–10-minute jog at whatever pace feels natural, which I’d describe as “jogging it in.” The lack of strides may seem unconventional, adding another unique aspect to this training method, but it has consistently worked for me and others. As with the rest intervals, the warm-up and cool-down are yours to customize. If you prefer a different approach, feel free to do so. I don’t consider these elements essential or worth overthinking.

I also find that keeping the warm-up low effort helps me avoid starting too aggressively, allowing me to ease into the workout and ensuring there’s no risk of overdoing the first repetition. Typically, I aim for 1–2% below target pace on the first rep of the day, particularly with shorter reps. On longer reps like 10 minutes, you are already warmed up by the final 5 minutes, so you can aim for appropriate pace by the second half of the first rep. Remember, you don't gain extra fitness if you overcook your session and can't sustain the consistency.

Heart Rate Guardrails

Another point to revisit is heart rate. If you’ve completed the Friel Lactate Threshold Heart Rate (LTHR) test you will have a helpful reference point for correlation. Assuming you have accurate HR measurement, you should not regularly exceed your LTHR during these workouts. Occasionally, your HR might spike unexpectedly on the final rep, which can happen. If you consistently exceed LTHR, it’s a strong indication that the paces are too fast. This has occurred for some athletes, and the solution is to dial back the pace slightly for a few weeks—or longer, if needed. As noted, if your HR is approximately 10–15 beats per minute below LTHR on the first rep, it’s typically a good sign that you’ll stay under LTHR by the final rep. This buffer accounts for cardiac drift, a natural phenomenon during workouts where HR gradually increases over time. This can happen even at a consistent pace due to factors like rising body temperature, dehydration and fatigue. Starting 10–15 beats below LTHR by the end of the early repetitions helps ensure that you maintain your target intensity and your HR remains below LTHR despite the drift. While it’s worth checking your HR occasionally, adjusting pace in the moment based on HR can be challenging due to the sometimes-erratic nature. The only time I would consider adjusting pace is if my RPE is higher than expected and my HR is over LTHR.

Adjustments

A final point regarding paces is the importance of considering how you feel before a workout. I recommend further reducing the pace if you are: recovering from a minor illness, haven’t slept well, are dealing with high stress from work or personal life, or simply don’t feel at your best. I never blindly adhere to prescribed paces if they don’t align with my body’s condition. In such cases, I adjust by dropping to the next set of pace parameters. For example, I might run 3-minute reps at the pace intended for 6-minute reps, or the longest reps might be performed at as slow as marathon pace. This common-sense approach helps manage fatigue effectively. Opting for lower intensity is preferable if it prevents exacerbating illness or accumulating excessive fatigue.

Some individuals have reported that they have noted correlations with Heart Rate Variability (HRV) and subthreshold session lactate levels. HRV is a measure of the variability between heart beats. If you have a heart rate of 60 beats per minute, it is unlikely that each beat lands on the same tick of a second hand. This variability is normal and can be indicative of the balance of the sympathetic and parasympathetic input your heart is experiencing. More details are beyond the scope of this book, but HRV is becoming more frequently measured and used to assess the body's readiness to train. Generally speaking, when HRV declines it can be a sign that the body is under stress or not fully recovered. Many smartwatches measure HRV and people have described using it as one metric to help them assess their readiness for a session. Others have noted higher lactate readings despite running at their normal subthreshold paces when they have a low HRV. All of this is to say, if your watch measures a low HRV and you feel a little more fatigued than normal, it may be prudent to aim for slower paces in your subthreshold session.

Thinking About Distances

Some of you may note, “But sirpoc runs distances, not times!” This is true, but I have reached a point where the distances I run for my reps closely align with the original time-based targets. For example, my 1k target time aligns close with the original 3 minutes. Although I’m incorporating slightly more total volume with additional reps, I’m still largely adhering to the original rep times I established. Additionally, I’ve spot-tested lactate levels. Even as I’ve gotten faster the pace ranges continue to hold up effectively. As the saying goes, it never gets easier—you just get faster.

I enjoy distance repetitions for several reasons. First, it’s an excellent way to practice pacing, especially when running familiar routes. It provides a clear visualization of traveling from point A to point B. It is also practical for planning road routes, allowing you to avoid crossing roads. It is particularly useful on the track or regular out and back loops you are going to set up. However, when incorporating distance into your training, consider scaling it to your current fitness level. For example, I can now cover 1 km in just over 3 minutes, which suits my current ability. When I first started using distance, my 3-minute repetitions were closer to 800 meters, and a 1 km effort would have taken nearer to 4 minutes. Choose a distance that aligns with your capabilities, especially if you’re early in your running journey. For instance, if you can cover 600 meters in approximately 3 minutes, that’s a great starting point. Some runners prefer to train by time rather than distance, and both approaches are valid. There’s no right or wrong way as we are all fundamentally working toward the same goal.

My guideline for fixed-distance training is as follows: aim to stay within ±30 seconds for short repetitions (intended to take around 3 minutes), and within ±1 minute for medium and longer repetitions (intended for approximately 6 and 10 minutes, respectively). For example, I currently perform many 1600-meter repeats, which is a more convenient road distance for me than 2 km. These take just over 5 minutes, which remains well within the acceptable range for medium-length repetitions.

### Paces and effect of conditions

If you are going to run using a running system that is based on pace, it is important that you understand the environmental factors that affect pace. There are factors that can pose significant challenges during outdoor workouts: hills, wind, altitude, winter conditions and heat. Despite having large impacts on performance, these factors are often overlooked in classic running texts. I will discuss these in detail and share some intriguing insights I’ve gathered over the past couple of years. All of these elements are crucial to understand when you are using the paces we’ve established as a proxy for lactate testing and intensity control. It may not be obvious but they can impact pacing in a variety of ways—sometimes dramatically, other times with barely noticeable effects. Before heading out for a run, I always check weather apps to prepare accordingly. If you do not adjust your paces based on these factors, you will find yourself above threshold and the fundamentals of the system (intensity control) fall apart.

Hills

When it comes to hills, maintaining consistent subthreshold pacing is challenging. I live in a remarkably flat region and I've found that when traveling to hilly areas on holidays, you must account for elevation changes as best as you can. The most effective solution is to locate a track or a flat park if a treadmill is unavailable. If these options aren’t feasible, my best advice is to choose an out-and-back loop and strive to keep it as flat as possible.

For hilly routes, I’ve found that relying solely on Grade Adjusted Pace (GAP) from a watch is often unreliable due to inaccurate elevation data. Instead, I recommend planning your route online beforehand using more precise elevation data (I find [onthegomap.com](https://onthegomap.com/) very useful). Based on my experience, for every 10 meters of elevation gain and loss per kilometer, at a pace of approximately 4:00 per kilometer, I gain about 7 seconds per kilometer on descents and lose about 13 seconds on ascents. Consequently, for each 10 meters of elevation gain and loss within a kilometer, your overall pace will be approximately 6 seconds slower. This is not ideal, but in good conditions, a 4:00 per kilometer target pace becomes 4:06 for a 10-meter gain and loss on an out-and-back route. The out-and-back format simplifies pace calculations, and this approach is grounded in real-world data I’ve collected over the years. Practicing pacing on rolling terrain, returning to your starting point, can also provide valuable experience for future races.

It’s far more effective to avoid structuring workouts where you run uphill for one repetition, rest, and then run entirely downhill for the next, as this leads to significantly inconsistent paces. For practicality, if you scale elevation changes to a 5-meter gain and loss per kilometer for our 4:00 runner, you lose approximately 3 seconds per kilometer. For a 15-meter gain and loss per kilometer, expect a loss of about 8 seconds. On a very hilly course with a 20-meter net gain and loss per kilometer, a runner targeting a 4:00 per kilometer pace could lose around 10 seconds per kilometer. Beyond this level of elevation change, using pace as a metric becomes highly unreliable for workouts. These adjustments can also be successfully applied to racing and should be used for planning.

Naturally, pacing adjustments for elevation changes must be scaled based on a runner’s speed. Faster runners cover the same distance in less time, reducing the relative impact of elevation. Slower runners spend more time on inclines for longer slope exposure, with fatigue amplifying the effect. A 2010 study found that slower runners lose more time on an incline because they spend longer on the uphill, using more energy and tiring faster (Townshend et al., 2010). For instance, a runner maintaining a 3:00 per kilometer pace might lose only about 4 seconds per kilometer for a 10-meter net elevation gain and loss per kilometer. Conversely, a 5:00 per kilometer runner will typically lose approximately 7 seconds per kilometer, while a 6:00 per kilometer runner could lose as much as 9 seconds per kilometer. These estimates apply to a net elevation gain and loss of 10 meters per kilometer.

Below is a practical range of adjustments for runners, covering paces from 3:00 per kilometer to 6:00 per kilometer, which likely encompasses most runners during workouts. Simply round your target pace to the nearest full minute for simplicity. For example, if your target pace is 3:48 per kilometer, use the adjustment for a 4:00 per kilometer runner. Similarly, if you run at 5:20 per kilometer, apply the adjustment for a 5:00 per kilometer runner. This approach offers a rough but effective real-world guide for running regular loops or out-and-back routes. Lastly, depending on the elevation gain and loss, even net downhill race courses may not necessarily be faster than a flat course.

Wind

Wind significantly impacts runners due to aerodynamic drag and energy expenditure. A headwind increases air resistance, forcing runners to exert more energy to maintain speed. This is similar to running uphill. This extra effort tires you out faster. A 2024 study showed that a very strong headwind made runners at a 4:10 min/km pace work ~50% harder, burning more energy (Mesquita et al., 2024). A tailwind offers a smaller benefit because it doesn't fully make up for the energy lost against a headwind and you lose the thermal benefits of sweat evaporation. A 2025 study found a 30 km/hr tailwind saved only 9% energy, while a headwind cost 37% more, so tailwinds don’t balance the loss (Legramandi & Cavagna, 2025). Aerodynamic drag grows sharply with wind speed. Even moderate headwinds can slow you down a lot. A 1980 study showed even an 18 km/hr (11 mi/hr) headwind increased energy use, slowing runners noticeably (Davies, 1980). Crosswinds also disrupt forward momentum by creating lateral forces, requiring stabilizing muscle effort that further reduces efficiency. A 2019 report noted that crosswinds raised energy use, making running less efficient (Stryd, 2019).

On an out-and-back or looped track, these effects result in a net performance loss, as the energy deficit from headwinds and crosswinds outweighs tailwind gains. A 1980 study found a 10 mi/hr headwind slowed runners by ~12 seconds per mile, while a tailwind helped by only ~6 seconds, causing a net slowdown (Davies, 1980). Whilst you really don't need to know the exact science behind all of this, from a sheer practical point of view, we need to understand roughly how much the wind is slowing us down.

These observations are based on a lot of my data collected over time during runs fully exposed to the elements. Those familiar with my running know that I train on the seafront along England’s south coast, in a place where there are no buildings, trees, or any form of cover. In areas with some tree cover or light buildings, the wind’s impact can likely be reduced by approximately 20%. A 2021 study found that trees or sparse buildings slow wind by 15–25%, supporting this estimate (Gromke & Ruck, 2021). This reduction occurs because trees and structures act as partial barriers, disrupting airflow and creating pockets of lower wind speed through resistance and deflection. In urban environments or locations with dense tree cover, an additional 20% reduction may apply, totaling a 40% decrease. Research shows dense buildings or vegetation can cut wind speed by 30–50%, backing this claim (Ng et al., 2012). Dense vegetation or closely spaced buildings further impede wind flow, absorbing or redirecting its force. This significantly weakens the aerodynamic drag and lateral forces experienced by the runner, thereby lessening the overall impact on performance. You will have to use your own judgement here though. Use a best guess in terms of identifying what type of environment your route fits into before doing any calculations.

For wind speeds up to 5 km/hr (3 mi/hr), the impact on running is minimal and nearly imperceptible, making it negligible for practical purposes. This assumes an out-and-back running route or track loops. However, beyond ~7 km/hr (4 mi/hr), the effect of wind becomes more pronounced. Wind hinders progress on the outbound leg significantly more than it aids on the return. Furthermore, the time lost running into the wind is exacerbated, as this segment takes longer to complete due to the increased resistance. Notably, slower runners experience a greater relative penalty from wind, as they spend more time running into the headwind, amplifying its impact.

These calculations assume the wind is primarily a headwind or tailwind, with minor real-world variations such as crosswind components or shifting directions accounted for in the charts. This variability highlights the limitations of calculators and tools like Garmin wrist-based power metrics. These tools often assume constant wind speed and direction, leading to inaccuracies comparable to rough estimates.

Crosswinds

Crosswinds can significantly impede running performance, often more than expected. For this reason, the guide referenced above is designed to apply to calculations for an entire track loop or an out-and-back route, where the wind is primarily a headwind or tailwind, returning to the same starting point. Even if portions of the route involve crosswinds, their notable slowing effect is factored into the guide, ensuring the overall calculation remains practical and reasonably accurate, despite some crosswinds.

Consequently, in a race or workout with a pure crosswind throughout an out-and-back route, the impact on running performance is more significant than might be expected. Unlike scenarios with headwinds and tailwinds, a pure crosswind offers no compensatory tailwind section, resulting in consistent slowing in both directions. As previously mentioned from the 1980 study, a 20 km/hr crosswind slows runners in both directions of an out-and-back route, with no tailwind relief (Davies, 1980). This continuous resistance, without the benefit of a tailwind respite, amplifies the overall time loss.

A pure crosswind creates significant challenges for runners, impacting performance through increased drag, even when the wind is perpendicular to the direction of travel. Maintaining proper running form and consistent forward motion becomes more difficult. The paper states that for every 1% increase in horizontal (wind, for our purposes) impeding force applied, there is a 6.13% increase in metabolic cost. Second, some runners might think that a headwind and tailwind “cancel out” so to speak, and that if you run 1 km out and back with a headwind and tailwind your pace will be similar to what it might be with no wind. Just when you think you have gotten to the bottom of it, you peel back another layer to deal with. Furthermore, a runner’s performance may already be compromised by feeling off-form, meaning the target pace could be slower than usual even before accounting for wind, terrain, or the next factor we are about to come to, heat.

Heat

Heat is something I only worry about a few months out of the year. I am lucky in that I don't have to deal with it very often. It can be layered and complex, but I will give you a simple guide that comes from my own data and the literature.

Depending on where you live, heat may be a relatively infrequent challenge compared to wind. That being said, the impact of heat combined with dew point can be significant. Dew point, a measure of humidity, affects running by making it harder for sweat to evaporate. This reduces the body’s ability to cool itself and increases perceived effort, especially in warmer conditions. A 2015 study found that dew points above 20°C (68°F) raise body temperature and slow pace by ~3–7% (Sawka et al., 2015). Higher dew points exacerbate heat stress, leading to faster fatigue and slower paces. A 2016 study showed 70–80% humidity at 30°C (86° F) raises heart rate and slows runners by ~5% (Che Muhamed et al., 2016). A 2012 study found hotter, humid conditions slow marathon times by ~6–12 s/km at 25°C (77°F), especially for non-elite runners (El Helou et al., 2012). The magnitude of this effect is large. Below is a simplified guide, derived from my own data and experience, to adjust pace for heat and dew point. You should prioritize scheduling summer workouts during the coolest parts of the day with the lowest temperature and dew point, as these two factors combine to significantly impact performance. A 2010 study showed morning or evening runs reduce heat-related pace slowdowns by 15–20%, saving ~2 s/km for a 4:00 min/km runner (Casa et al., 2010). The table below offers examples for runners across a range of paces, using a 4:00 per kilometer runner as a reference. Running Surface

Running on dirt or semi-compacted gravel can slow your pace by 2–3% compared to smooth tarmac. This is a factor worth considering when planning workouts or races. I don’t often train on these surfaces, but data I’ve gathered suggests this range is consistent. At a local parkrun, where about 50% of the course is a compacted gravel path, I’ve analyzed my own times and those of other runners across various distances. After cropping the data to just the gravel sections, I found that an average 2–3% slowdown holds true for myself and others over a large proportion of runs. As an example for myself, in one race I managed 3:05 min/km on the tarmac section, but only 3:09 min/km along the back stretch of gravel. A friend of mine who is around a 20-minute flat 5k runner as well as being good at pacing, was managing around 3:58 min/km on the tarmac section but 4:04 min/km along the same gravel section. This has a notable impact on performance.

The reasons for this slowdown stem from the nature of gravel and dirt surfaces. Tarmac is hard and stable, providing excellent energy return that allows for efficient push-off with each stride. Compacted gravel or dirt absorbs more energy, reducing the “spring” in your step and requiring greater effort to maintain pace. Additionally, even compacted gravel has small variations in texture, forcing stabilizing muscles like the glutes, calves, and ankles to work harder to maintain balance. This extra effort can impact fatigue and reduce efficiency of a runner's stride. Runners also tend to take shorter, more cautious steps on gravel to avoid slipping. This adaptation disrupts the longer, confident strides possible on tarmac and contributes to slowing overall turnover. Even on compacted gravel, subtle slippage during push-off can reduce forward momentum, adding up over the course of a run. For example, a runner averaging 3:30/km on tarmac might see their pace drop to 3:34–3:36/km on compacted gravel, a difference of 4–6 seconds per kilometer. Runners at paces of 5:00/km, could experience a 6–9 second/km slowdown, as the absolute time lost scales with pace.

The slowdown is even more significant on really loose, uneven gravel paths, such as freshly laid stones or deeply rutted trails. This can potentially exceed 5%. For a 3:30/km runner this energy loss can translate to a mean pace of 3:40–3:51/km, or 10–21 seconds slower per kilometer. When planning runs on gravel, it’s helpful to adjust expectations for these surfaces. For a 5k, a 3:30/km runner might finish 20–30 seconds slower on compacted gravel or up to a minute slower on loose terrain. Testing your pace on both tarmac and gravel at the same effort level, such as matching heart rate, can provide a clearer picture of how these surfaces affect you personally.

Time of Day

It's worth noting that the time of day significantly affects my performance in workouts or races. I consistently perform better during evening sessions, with a noticeable ~1% drop in performance in the morning. This is especially true during very early workouts between 5–7 AM. While motivation can play a part (who really loves rolling out of bed for a workout?) this dip persists even when I’m mentally prepared. This is frustrating when races are scheduled early in the morning. Interestingly, some people experience the opposite, performing better in the morning while struggling later in the day.

Physiological factors likely explain these differences. Circadian rhythms, which regulate body temperature, hormones, and muscle function also influence athletic performance. For instance, a 2016 study found that strength, power, and anaerobic performance often peak in the late afternoon or early evening due to higher body temperature and testosterone levels (Chtourou & Souissi, 2012). This aligns with my experience of feeling more energized in the evening. My consistent ~1% performance drop in the morning might reflect an “evening chronotype,” as people with this profile often perform worse in the morning. A 2020 study noted that evening chronotypes show reduced physical output in the morning compared to afternoon or evening (Facer-Childs et al., 2015). Conversely, those with a “morning chronotype” are wired to thrive earlier, with better performance in the morning due to heightened alertness and muscle readiness, as shown in a 2019 study (Vitale et al., 2019). I suspect my body is geared for evening energy, while others shine at dawn, making early races a challenge for me but ideal for them. Whilst this may seem minor, it’s yet another thing to account for when targeting a specific pace range.

Treadmill

There tends to be a lot of questions about the use of treadmills for this method. While I don't use a treadmill for my training, I will try and address the commonly raised questions and key factors to consider. Most people are substituting treadmill runs for outdoor runs due to either unsuitably hot or cold outdoor conditions. If the alternative is to avoid running, then the treadmill offers a way for people to consistently build up training load throughout an otherwise un-runnable season. The other added benefits of treadmill running are that it offers both environment and pace control. This allows for week-on-week consistency when training. It is important to note, however, that treadmill calibration is likely to be off from real outdoor paces and I would not assume a 1:1 pace equivalency. This is important for both the return to outdoor running as well as for using different treadmills in a gym. For this reason, it may be wise to use LTHR rather than pace if you are unable to consistently use the same machine at your gym. If you have your own treadmill, some people have had success calibrating their treadmill with the Runn sensor from North Pole Engineering which measures the actual belt speed and allows pace to be transmitted to your watch or a smart phone app. Alternatively, you can also chalk up your treadmill, measure the belt length and then count the revolutions per minute at your subthreshold pace. With the known revolutions per minute and belt length, you can convert that to actual belt speed and compare your treadmill’s reported speed to your measured belt speed.

One last question that is often discussed is the need to set the treadmill elevation to 1% to offset the energy costs of running outdoors. While this might make a difference in some instances, it is more likely that the magnitude of difference is not significant to most hobby runners. If you are trying to offset wind resistance, it is probably only going to make a difference at those running faster paces. Additionally, some people have cited data suggesting the 1% grade over time can increase the load on the Achilles. With all of that out there, I am not sure it really makes a difference, and the choice is up to you. Typically, when a topic lacks a consensus agreement it is usually because a correct answer is not clear.

Limitations of Pace as Primary Training Load Metric

A key observation some of you may have noted is that using pace as the primary metric for training load can alter the intended load when slower paces are required due to environmental conditions. This is accurate. You have two options: manually adjust the training load (in Intervals.icu) per run or do nothing. I typically choose the latter. Over time—across months, years, and seasons—conditions tend to balance out, rendering minor variations insignificant with the assumption you are consistently training the same way. If you choose the first option, you can calculate the training load based on the adjusted pace. For example, my easy runs typically yield a score of 0.85–0.9 per minute on Intervals.icu. A workout, including up to 10 minutes of recovery (standing still) and 15–20 minutes of cooling down, generates approximately a 1.2–1.25 score per minute. If desired, you could recalibrate or seed your score to align with your normal range by manually adjusting it. Personally, I don’t bother with this approach, but it’s worth mentioning for those seeking precision. With the need for continuous adjustments to training paces, this may be an instance where heart rate (HR) may provide a more effective primary metric for accurately tracking your workload.

Considering these factors, using pace alone as a proxy for lactate levels doesn’t tell the whole story. For instance, a runner completing 1km reps with a net elevation gain and loss of 10 meters, in 20 km/hr wind and 21°C heat, may see their 4:00/km pace slow to 4:14/km. This is a significant difference, but it determines whether the workout is completed with normal recovery or becomes so demanding that it’s either unachievable or requires additional recovery days. Unless you are checking lactate (which we are trying to avoid) you will never know.

Takeaways

* Be honest with yourself about your current fitness level.
* Use the pace tables provided to determine your subthreshold paces.
* Use rests to avoid overshooting your session.
* Stay below your LTHR and aim to only approach it near the end of a session.
* Certain conditions need to be accounted for: Hills, wind, heat, and surface.
* An equal elevation gain and loss still leads to a slower average pace.
* Headwinds and tailwinds do not cancel each other out on an out and back loop.
* Even pure crosswinds lead to slower paces.
* You will need to account for environmental variables in racing or subthreshold sessions to perform at your best.

### Easy Paces and the Long Run

Easy Pace

Given that at least 75% of weekly running volume consists of easy runs, why don’t more books address specifics on this topic? Despite comprising the majority of your weekly training, easy runs are straightforward to explain and calculate compared to subthreshold efforts.

Percentage of HRMax

Our primary objectives for easy running are twofold. First, we seek to gently stimulate LT1 from below, similar to the approach of raising LT2 from below in subthreshold sessions. This establishes a foundation to accommodate progressively higher training volumes. Training below LT1 primarily enhances aerobic efficiency, improving endurance and metabolic function. Many studies, including a 2014 study, find that low-intensity running boosts endurance and supports higher training volumes (Seiler, 2014). Second, while providing a mild physiological stimulus, easy running acts as a recovery buffer between subthreshold sessions. Easy running is designed to minimize additional fatigue from the previous day. The literature shows that low-intensity runs aid recovery, reducing fatigue within 24 hours (Buchheit & Laursen, 2018). That means that even after completing up to an hour of easy running, your fatigue should diminish within 24 hours. This ensures recovery for subsequent workouts and prepares you effectively for the next subthreshold session. As little as 20–30 minutes a day of easy running can provide aerobic benefits, including improved fat oxidation and capillary development to deliver oxygen to the muscles (called capillarization). A 2021 study found that 20–30 minutes of low-intensity running increased mitochondrial growth by 15–20% and capillarization by 10–15%. This enhances fat oxidation (the body’s ability to use fat for energy) which boosts endurance (Bishop et al., 2021). Sessions shorter than 20 minutes likely offer more limited aerobic adaptations.

These benefits underscore the importance of refraining from pushing too hard or becoming overly ambitious on easy days. In the broader context, such overrunning provides negligible benefits while incrementally increasing fatigue. This can be emphasized if you look at the difference in training load contribution between a true easy run and a marginally faster easy run. You get negligible extra load at the cost of not being recovered enough for your next subthreshold session. In my early training, I undoubtedly fell into this trap. Does running too intensely on an easy day lead to extreme exhaustion or physical breakdown? Certainly not. A single session is unlikely to have a dramatic impact in isolation. However, consistently overexerting on easy days over weeks to months is a leading contributor to the boom-and-bust cycle of training blocks. Fatigue accumulates like water in a tank. Each small excess contributes. Eventually, even a minor addition can cause overflow when capacity is maxed. The real skill here is managing this high wire act of training balance.

If keeping easy days truly easy wasn’t essential, wouldn’t elite runners perform much of their training at a moderate intensity? They don’t. Moderate intensity running provides minimal long-term benefits but creates cumulative fatigue. I’ve always been puzzled about adapting training plans from elite athletes to recreational runners. They fail to accurately reflect the critical balance of easy running and intensity. These plans frequently prescribe intervals that recreational runners struggle to recover from, while exacerbating the issue by setting “easy” paces that are excessively intense. Research on elite distance runners highlights that world-class track runners (5,000m and 10,000m) and marathon specialists perform ≥80% of their total running volume at low intensity throughout the training year. This is typically at 55–75% of maximum heart rate (HRmax) and is considered easy running that supports aerobic base development and recovery without excessive fatigue (Haugen et al., 2022). This low-intensity running is often described as comfortable and conversational. The high proportion of this type of running allows elite runners to sustain high training volumes while minimizing fatigue. This is a principle often overlooked in recreational training plans.

How easy should “easy” running be? This is a complex question, and I cannot provide an individualized answer. However, when examining data and establishing broad guidelines suitable for a large group, we must clarify our objectives. This is where using a percentage of maximum heart rate serves as a simple, practical tool for ease of application.

Personally, I wasn’t certain of my maximum heart rate. I had no interest in subjecting myself to a max HR test. For practical purposes, let us assume an all-out 5k effort— where you’re gasping for air and picking up your lungs off the floor—yields a heart rate within one or two beats of your maximum. That’s it. This approach prioritizes practicality, usability, and real-world conditions. Would a scientist approve? Likely not. However, for practical application, let’s consider this your running max HR, or at least the highest you’re likely to encounter in real-world scenarios. You are always free to update this number if you reach a new high, provided you trust the measurement is accurate. For example, if your highest known MHR is 189 BPM but then suddenly after a year you notch up 202, the only logical explanation here is bad data.

What percentage of maximum heart rate should you aim for? I’ve settled on 70% as the upper limit for the average heart rate during an easy run. This is for runs lasting even an hour or longer with minimal to no cardiac drift after the first 10 minutes. This aligns with the idea of easy, aerobic running (60–70% of max heart rate) which helps burn fat and build endurance below the first lactate threshold (LT1) (Esteve-Lanao et al., 2007). Cardiac drift, where your heart rate slowly rises despite a steady pace, often occurs in the first 10 minutes because your body is warming up to exercise from rest. During this time, your heart works harder to supply oxygen and energy for running, even at an easy pace. Your heart rate will go up in response to things like warming muscles, a slight rise in body temperature, and stress hormones (like adrenaline). A 2008 study found that heart rate usually levels off after about 10 minutes of easy exercise as your body settles into a steady state, as long as you keep the effort below LT1 (Borresen & Lambert, 2008). After about 10 minutes of your body adapting to the workload it reaches a steady state and heart rate typically stabilizes, provided the effort remains below LT1.

It’s worth remembering this isn’t a target but more of a speed limit to respect. Often, my runs fall at 65% of max heart rate or lower. In over two years of training with this method, not a single easy run has averaged above 70% of max heart rate across the entire session.

I’ve settled on 70% as the upper limit for the average heart rate during an easy run.

The range for LT1 varies widely among individuals. From a physiological perspective, LT1 represents the upper boundary of what we consider "easy" running. However, this threshold can range from 70% of maximum heart rate (MHR) to as high as the upper 80% for others. Even without knowing your precise LT1, setting a cap at 70% MHR ensures that you are safeguarding yourself by staying well within the easy zone. If everyone followed this rule, the vast majority of runners would be running below their LT1.

Although Dr. Stephen Seiler’s work has generated some controversy among physiologists, I strongly endorse his view that when operating safely below LT1, you should experience minimal cardiac drift under optimal conditions. During an interview with The Physical Performance Show in 2019 Seiler states, “If you’re clearly below LT1 and you’re reasonably well-trained, then you should have a flat heart rate at least over an hour… If it’s drifting up already in 30 minutes, it’s too high in intensity.” Extreme heat may pose challenges to this, but excluding such conditions, my experience supports this as a dependable guideline. During the initial 10-15 minutes you will typically see a gentle rise that occurs before your HR levels off. A truly easy run should yield a relatively stable and flat HR graph following that initial rise.

If you are running in normal conditions and notice a 10–15 BPM rise in heart rate during the second half of your “easy” run, you have likely added unnecessary fatigue to your system even if it felt easy. Truthfully, it probably wasn’t easy. This type of run isn’t one we want to make a habit of, as it undermines the principles of consistency and sustainability in training.

When maintaining a heart rate below 70% of maximum HR, many people find it surprising how remarkably slow running feels. This pace is considerably easier— potentially by a significant margin—than the “easy” runs you may have undertaken previously. Personally, this was the most difficult aspect for me to embrace. Nonetheless, adopting this approach is essential to promoting the sustainability and consistency that we are seeking.

One of the most effective pieces of advice I can provide is to remove pace from your watch display, showing only elapsed time and heart rate. This reduces the psychological burden of running at a slower pace. The speed of your easy runs is not the key factor in achieving your training objectives. I’ve lost track of how often a local runner, capable of finishing a 5k 3–4 minutes behind me, overtakes me on their Strava-labeled “easy” days, running at an intensity reminiscent of peak Steve Cram. If you’re preoccupied with how your training appears on Strava, you may find it challenging. That being said, joining the “Norwegian Singles Approach” Strava or Reddit groups could offer support with runners who share this philosophy and have likely navigated the same challenges as those new to this approach. For an 18-minute 5k runner, a pace of 4:30/km (7:15/mi) does not constitute an easy day. Unfortunately, we see this all the time.

When I was in 18:50 5k shape and first began training this way, the pace not only felt slower but actually was slower. Over time, the gap between perceived effort and actual pace has narrowed, though perhaps not as dramatically as one might expect. One anecdote is that I live in a flat area, but steep road and railway bridges occasionally interrupt my routes. Early on, I found myself walking up these inclines or, at best, managing a crawling jog to keep my heart rate low. This approach reflects a key insight from Stephen Seiler’s research on elite endurance athletes. Seiler observed, “Specifically for people who find their heart rate increases very quickly while running, there is no shame walking up a hill! That was one of the first observations I made in Norway that started me on this - I watched a female athlete who I knew had a VO2max of 65mmol/kg, which is good, walk up a steep hill during a run session. I learnt that she was doing a low intensity session and she was disciplined. She walked fast up the hill, kept her heart rate stable and kept going.”

Of course, nothing catastrophic happens if you run up these hills. Your legs don't fall off, but strictly adhering to prescribed effort levels is a critical piece of the larger puzzle. The discipline reinforces trust in the process, fostering not only improvement as a runner but also a deeper understanding of the journey. Embracing this mindset while committing to the principles and the bigger picture will get you most of the way to your goals.

Percentage of Maximum Aerobic Speed

Some of you may be familiar with my original posts, while others may not. Initially, I outlined two training options for easy days: one based on heart rate and the other on 65% of Maximal Aerobic Speed (MAS). MAS is the fastest pace you can sustain for approximately six minutes, reflecting your aerobic capacity at the point where oxygen consumption plateaus (often near VO2 max). It is a practical metric for setting training intensities, particularly for interval or threshold workouts. The easiest way to estimate MAS is not through a time trial—unless you’re eager to perform one—but by using your most up to date race time and using one of the many online calculators that give you equivalent performances. From there, identify the race distance closest to six minutes, use that pace as your MAS benchmark and take 65% of that pace. The best way to do this is to convert the pace to seconds and divide it by.65. For example, if the pace you have identified is 4:00/km for the 6 minutes, 240 seconds divided by.65 will give you 370 seconds rounded. Divide by 60 to convert back to pace/km and this will roughly equal around 6:10/km.

However, using 65% of MAS as a guide has limitations. I’ve noticed that many runners can run at 65% of MAS, but their average HR is often too high for the run to be truly “easy”. This is especially true when sandwiched between two subthreshold training days. An additional problem with MAS is that people see it as a target rather than a speed limit. People seem much more inclined to push to the maximum limit with pace as a guide on these easy days, as opposed to HR.

While HR on subthreshold days can vary slightly and lag in real-time response, I have found that HR becomes a reliable and consistent metric during easy runs. At lower intensities, HR serves as an effective guide for effort. Ideally, you’re using a chest strap, as discussed earlier, or at least an optical arm strap. This provides reasonably accurate HR data at these lower efforts. In my experience, HR is one of the best tools we have for gauging easy runs.

Through training this way for years and seeing the pitfalls that others have fallen into, I believe using a percentage of maximum heart rate is the better approach for guiding easy runs compared to 65% of MAS. A key drawback of pace-based training is the need to adjust for variables like terrain, wind, and heat, which we have already been through in detail. In contrast, adhering to an HR-based guide allows you to run freely. It affords runners the ability to explore diverse routes and maintain the intended effort on four out of seven weekly running days. This flexibility is reason enough to prioritize using HR over MAS. It enhances enjoyment by offering variety in where and how you choose to run.

Respiration Rate

A fourth option for easy run intensity control is respiration rate (RR). Similarly to how we can observe how many times our heart beats per minute to gauge a given effort, we can also observe how many breaths per minute we take. Respiration rate serves as an excellent proxy for RPE since the two are closely related in a part of the brain called the medulla oblongata ($5, £5, other currencies are available, if you can say that 5 times fast, I can’t even say it once). In order to give a clear and helpful explanation, I am going to need to simplify a very complex system – so bear with me.

When exercise intensity increases early on, there will be little to no lactate accumulation. Through this process your body will increase your air exchange by increasing your minute ventilation (VE) which is a measure of the total air moved in a minute. The body increases your minute ventilation in the initial stages of exercise by first increasing your tidal volume (volume of air per breath) then later by increasing your respiration rate (breaths per minute). Generally speaking, people don't need to increase their RR significantly until after LT1 which is why the “talk test” guideline holds up surprisingly well. However, at some point you will reach an effort level that exceeds your first lactate threshold, and your body will respond to the increased energy demand and byproduct buildup by increasing your minute ventilation. This is the point where your body begins to use lactate to help shuttle hydrogen ions out of the muscle to prevent a decrease in muscle pH. It does this because low muscle pH would slow the rate of energy production and cause you to slow down. Once you reach this intensity, your blood lactate levels will become slightly elevated, but more importantly, there will be additional H+ in your blood that the body will seek to buffer. It does this by using bicarbonate (yes, that same stuff that you can take exogenously and gives you some toilet trouble before your races), and the buffering reaction produces water and carbon dioxide (CO2) which is then exhaled.

As a result, you will begin to notice your breathing becomes more labored above this first threshold. By this point, your tidal volume will no longer be able to contribute to the release of CO2. With tidal volume unable to compensate, your respiration rate will increase to ensure all that CO2 is exhaled and blood pH remains as stable as possible. This means that we can use respiration rate as a proxy for when we are beginning to drift above our first lactate threshold. More importantly, we can use it as a tertiary metric for ensuring our intensity remains at a manageable level, especially on easy days.

Pace and heart rate each have their own benefits, but they also have flaws. Tracking respiration rate seems to be a reliable way to keep honest about how “easy” a run is. It helps turn the “conversational pace” guideline into a trackable metric. Finding your RR proxy can be challenging. Ideally the easy run RR would be determined with lactate measurements to determine exactly what your respiration rate is at LT1. That being said, it is possible to find your easy run RR with some effort.

You have a few options for measuring your respiration rate. You can buy one of many devices on the market (which have varying accuracy) or just manually count your breaths over the course of a minute. The idea is to experiment over time to find a value that is sustainable for every easy run you do. You want to ensure you are staying comfortably under that first threshold. You also want a value for which there is no drift in RR over the course of the run. For example, if you try to run at 28 BrPM (breaths per minute) and find it increases to 30-32 over the course of your 1-hour easy run, then the run probably isn’t as easy as you think. Repeat that run over several weeks and you will probably find you are not recovering between runs. In this example you probably need to run at a pace with 26 BrPM. Another option is to do a progressive run where you measure your respiration rate at increasing intensities from very easy, aiming up towards threshold pace and make note of an inflection point where it begins to increase more rapidly. This likely aligns with your first lactate threshold. Once you know that inflection point, you can take that value and ensure you are always several breaths per minute below it during easy efforts. Like HR, RR is very individual, and you should only compare your numbers to yourself. No 220 minus your age shenanigans.

By observing RR day-to-day you can begin to put data together on what RR correlates with a truly easy effort. You can then use that data later on to check yourself on days where other data will be less accurate. For example, if you have a good idea of what your easy RR is during the spring when temps are mild and your HR is nice and stable, then you can take that value and apply it on hot summer days where you would otherwise see cardiac drift regardless of the effort. I recommend recording it in a journal or Strava hidden notes. This way you’re not left wondering if you’re running too hard or left frustrated that your HR is drifting too high. You can instead just focus on your breathing. A check-in every 10 minutes or so ensures your RR is not climbing above the value you want it at. Eventually you will naturally regulate your pace based on this RR.

Respiration rate is just another metric that can act as a backup when your others are “failing”. For example, when your HR and RPE are misaligned. A big benefit of respiration rate is that it tends to be a bit more accurate than heart rate as an intensity gauge in the heat. In the heat you will see HR increase more rapidly than usual as your body tries to keep itself cool. Another scenario is when you don’t quite have the aerobic base built and your HR climbs quickly; you can instead use RR as a guide. This can be especially beneficial for newer runners, where, without walking, their HR might drift over what is considered an easy HR. Respiration rate can be a useful backup tool in ensuring your easy runs are truly easy, and that you can keep stacking those bricks over time. Ultimately, err on the side of too easy.

The Long Run

The long run is a simple extension of an easy run, devoid of the intricate twists found in a Coen Brothers film. The primary goal is to promote adaptation to prolonged time on your feet, delivering a gentle aerobic stimulus without becoming a full-fledged workout. Unlike the other easy runs, this will add a small but important increase in training load. However, care must be taken to avoid overdoing it. Extending the duration or intensity beyond a reasonable limit can disproportionately increase fatigue, disrupting the delicate balance of training stress we strive to manage.

My training formula (outside of marathon preparation) remains straightforward: multiply the duration of your easy run in minutes by approximately 1.7. For instance, if your easy runs on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday last 40 minutes each, your Sunday "long" run would round up to roughly 70 minutes. I have come to find this offers a great starting point for understanding how to balance the time spent on your long run compared to an easy run. Below is a very simple guide to get you started.

One exception I made was to slightly extend the long run to replicate the time spent on my feet during a half marathon. For me, this meant increasing it to approximately 80 minutes, even when my weekday easy runs were only 40 minutes. While I wouldn’t push to extremes, extending the long run to 80–90 minutes is reasonable if it aligns with your projected half marathon duration. When your daily easy runs reach about 45 minutes, your long run will naturally approach 80 minutes anyway. For those at a slower pace, it’s acceptable to push these boundaries a few times, perhaps 3–6 weeks before a half marathon goal, to get as close to projected time on foot as possible.

Consider a cap of 95-100 minutes even if that is still less time on foot than your race goal. For my first half marathon, I relied solely on this approach and achieved a time consistent with my 5k and 10k fitness. It is worth reiterating that the general training principles laid out are tailored for 5k to half marathon distances, enabling you to confidently enter any of these races at any time, well-prepared.

I strongly recommend avoiding anything overly complex or ambitious within the long run. If you’re committed to three subthreshold runs each week, maintaining an easy pace for the long run is essential. There is little room in the system to handle additional intensity. The only exception might be if you’re restricted to six running days and choose to merge the third workout with the long run. However, I consider this suboptimal and favor the consistency of a seven-day training week whenever possible. All else being equal, you are able to fit more load into 7 days than 6 and avoid the risk of trying to cram and overload the 6 days.

If six days is your only option here is what I would recommend: Consider dropping one midweek easy run, reducing the intensity of the long run slightly while extending its duration by about 10 minutes. For everything else I recommend adhering to the outlined guidelines. Among various adaptations, this approach has proven to be the most sustainable for condensing training into six days. Even for those, like myself, who previously found a seven-day schedule unsustainable or at least very hard, adopting the philosophy of this book has made training every day manageable. Unlike other training plans, this system was designed to allow for enough recovery in 7 days of training.

The chart (Table 2.4) provided offers a clear guide for balancing easy runs and long runs. For most runners, unless you’re logging over seven hours of weekly training or preparing for a marathon, there is little need to exceed 90 minutes for long runs. Research shows that key physiological benefits like increased mitochondrial density and capillary growth, are nearly maximized between 60 and 90 minutes of moderate aerobic running, with diminishing additional aerobic gains beyond this point (Holloszy & Coyle, 1984; Andersen & Henriksson, 1977). Mitochondria, the powerhouse of cells, produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to fuel running, while capillaries deliver oxygen-rich blood to muscles, enhancing endurance (Brooks et al., 2005)

Extending runs past two hours, especially beyond three hours, can increase injury risk due to form breakdown, muscle fatigue, and overuse, particularly for beginner runners (Fokkema et al., 2019). Long runs can also raise stress hormone levels, which can slow recovery by breaking down muscle tissue for energy (Hackney, 2006). Outside of marathon focused training, pushing beyond 1 hour 45 minutes yields little benefit and often introduces fatigue that disrupts easy days. Our goal is to accumulate time on our feet, lay a strong foundation, and move on with the day. Many have noted the stability of my heart rate during these runs, even beyond 90 minutes. This consistency comes from keeping long runs as intended. These are just an extended easy run and I maintain a heart rate below 70% of maximum, often averaging 65-67%.

One final note. You may know your LT1 threshold or be able to run at 75% of max heart rate while maintaining a stable heart rate profile. I’m not saying you must avoid this, but consider what you’re aiming to achieve by doing so. Is there really any benefit to running easy runs at 75% of MHR compared to 60–70%? The fitness returns are likely marginal, and fatigue is significantly increased (Esteve-Lanao et al., 2007). The Esteve-Lanao study found that low-intensity training at 60–70% MHR minimized cumulative fatigue while improving aerobic performance, supporting the idea that higher intensities like 75% MHR increase recovery demands unnecessarily. My emphasis is that easy days require as much caution as workout days when viewed through a long-term lens. You should avoid thinking of easy runs as isolated runs removed from the cumulative effects of training.

### Takeaways

* Pace is the ideal metric for guiding your subthreshold sessions and can be determined based on your current fitness level.
* Breaking the session into repetitions with rests helps you avoid incurring too much fatigue on a workout day.
* The subthreshold pace needed for a workout will depend on the duration of the repetitions.
* Use your LTHR as a guardrail for staying within the desired zone during training.
* Paces will need to be adjusted to accommodate environmental factors such as wind, hills, heat and surface. Use the tables provided.
* Easy pace is important to get right in order to avoid stacking fatigue and allow for adequate recovery between subthreshold sessions.
* The best way to gauge an easy run is 70% of maxHR as an absolute limit.
* The long run is an extension of the Easy run (roughly 1.7 times the duration) that allows for increased stimulus and training load without adding significant fatigue.

## The Results

### Does It Work?

After stagnating on traditional training plans and experimenting with this new training approach, I decided this was it. I fully committed to this system. I followed the sample week laid out: three easy runs, three subthreshold sessions, and one long run. That was it. I set aside all distractions, laced up my shoes each day and completed each run with its intended purpose. I adhered to the established guidelines and moved on with my day. I avoided speedwork, strides, and hills. I did nothing outside of the set parameters. My primary focus was to isolate the key factors driving long-term progress and eliminate the noise or temptation of overcomplication.

I quickly realized this approach was sustainable. While I won’t call it exciting, I will say that within just a couple of weeks I no longer felt mentally or physically burned out. The psychological stress of running had dissipated. Ironically, the monotony of this training structure became the primary challenge. It is a simple plan, but not easy. It is not physically exhausting, but the repetitive nature can feel mentally tedious and draining at times. This is likely the biggest barrier to long term success with the plan. The sentiment appears common, as many share similar experiences, particularly after extended periods on the system. Those who maintain this training for six months or even beyond a full year often describe the same mental fatigue opposed to physical but find they are happy with this trade when results improve beyond expectations. To reinforce the point previously made, the flexibility of using HR for your guide on easy days will allow you to do a lot of social running at conversational and easy paces to help mitigate some of this. It will be the workout days that are the challenge, as likely these will make you a lone runner, unless you are lucky enough to have a training partner around your own ability.

After a month, I grew curious about my progress and decided to test myself at a parkrun the following weekend. The conditions were favorable, and I felt energized going in, but I only managed 18:48. This result was deflating. I didn’t feel strong during the run, my legs felt unresponsive and my performance had declined. Disheartened, I returned home and seriously contemplated giving up while sipping my morning coffee with some chocolate digestives. Having now seen many people start this training, this initial setback is common among runners training this way for the first 4–8 weeks. Many people anticipate a significant breakthrough that doesn’t materialize straight away. Runners are notorious for their lack of patience when it comes to their training. I certainly include myself in this bracket.

I decided to give it one more month. If no progress materialized, I was done with running. Perhaps I had truly reached my peak. I was consumed by the thought that the weeks of experimentation and training might have been wasted. Including those experimental weeks testing paces, I was likely well beyond two months into this process.

Then, like the slow buildup in Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, where dialogue drags on until everything sparks into life with an unexpected flamethrower, my breakthrough arrived. I showed up to another parkrun, feeling dreadful after a lethargic 3km jog from home. Even in the first 500 meters of the race, my legs felt unresponsive. At the first turn, just before the 1km mark, I still felt off but noticed my split. I worried I had started too fast. Yet my kilometer splits improved, my legs began to respond, and I felt stronger as the race progressed. I finished the 5k in 18:06, powering through windy conditions. I was stunned. It didn’t feel much faster, but the result was undeniable. This was the breakthrough, and the floodgates opened. In retrospect, I realized my training load had initially decreased with this approach. It took time—much like the tortoise outpacing the hare—for the benefits to manifest. I should note that during years of training like this, there have been other periods where nothing happened for a number of months and then the flood gates open to progress and personal bests.

Through the steady progression of this training system, I reached my highest-ever training load. This translated into my best-ever performance. Eventually, I broke 18 minutes multiple times. And yes, I “won” the parkrun I had targeted! Around this time, I began posting on LetsRun thinking the approach could benefit others. Initially I posted as a guest without registering. But the community engaged, questions flowed, and before I knew it the LetsRun mega thread took on a life of its own.

By this point, I was fully committed and increasingly curious about how far this approach could take me. On reflection, the LetsRun posts were psychologically beneficial. They helped me stay accountable and motivated to demonstrate that consistent, straightforward training could drive long-term improvement. It was encouraging to connect with a few individuals via email who were genuinely interested. Some of them had experienced success with this method or believed it was the most effective approach for maximizing training value. Several of these runners have become close friends to this day. It’s remarkable how running fosters such meaningful connections.

Within seven months, I was training nearly every day. I broke 36 minutes in my first 10k and ran a 5k in 17:10. At this point, I finally felt like a true runner. That being said, I still grapple with imposter syndrome. Even winning regional races against competitors half my age, I struggle to fully embrace my identity as a runner. While my paces improved, my training remained unchanged: three easy runs, three subthreshold sessions, and one long easy run.

By ten months, I completed my first half marathon in 1:15 and ran a mid-16-minute 5k. In the early days I raced every four to six weeks, which is highly beneficial for several reasons. First, races provide an excellent measure of fitness progress. This complements indicators from workouts, like declining heart rate trends at the same pace. In the UK (and now beyond), parkruns offer a free, accessible way to test fitness. Second, racing introduces a small dose of high-intensity stimulus absent from easy and subthreshold running. That said, I’ve gone months without this extra stimulus (and race a lot less these days) and still improved while maintaining the ability to lock into 5k pace. If you race a 5k, simply substitute it for your third subthreshold workout of the week and keep the long run as usual. This approach works well, provided you don’t race too frequently. If someone told me they planned to run two or three parkruns all-out each month, I’d caution that this may be excessive.

Over these months, I gradually increased my training volume. For example, I progressed from 75 minutes of weekly subthreshold running to 80 minutes and beyond. I applied the same approach to easy runs, incrementally adding time when my training load stabilized. Without progressively increasing the load, you risk stagnating, which was the problem in the first place.

### Increasing Load

Rather than increasing intensity, gradually increasing training load is the key. In the first 9–12 months, you likely have significant room to improve simply by running consistently, day after day and week after week. Breaking the stop-start pattern and boom-and-bust cycle of past training will lead to improvements. For every minute I added to weekly subthreshold running, I aimed to add four minutes of easy running, striving to maintain a 75/25% easy-to-subthreshold ratio as closely as possible.

However, as weekly training hours increase, the proportion of easy running inevitably grows. This is because higher volumes place greater demands on recovery. This makes it harder to sustain a high percentage of intense subthreshold work without risking fatigue, overtraining, or injury. For this reason, elite runners often adopt an 85/15% or even a 90/10% split, especially at very high mileage. With their substantial volume, filling 25% of their training with workouts (as I recommend here) becomes nearly impossible. Conversely, at lower weekly hours, you can allocate a higher percentage—closer to 25%—to subthreshold work and adopt a more aggressive approach.

I think you will find my training load progression interesting. Despite rapid performance gains, my progress followed a strikingly linear pattern. Each increase in training time or load directly translated to improved performance. Much of these early gains stemmed from successfully extending my training time well beyond previous levels, capitalizing on untapped potential. However, progress became more elusive entering my second and third years. The low-hanging fruit of aerobic gains had been exhausted, making further improvements challenging. Does this call for a new approach? Not necessarily. Gains remain attainable, but they require greater effort. Eventually extending training time, as opposed to increasing intensity, becomes significantly more demanding compared to the first 9–12 months. The substantial early rewards from increasing training time from 5 to 7.5 hours are harder to come by.

### What Don’t I Do?

Specificity

While I am not opposed to adding specificity if a clear purpose demands it, I advise against it. Some exceptions may apply. If there is a rationale that is absolutely compelling or an undeniable glaring weakness that needs to be addressed. For most runners (particularly older ones like myself), I believe specificity is overvalued and can detract from the long-term gains still achievable. This is true even years into training. Incorporating faster workouts tilts the risk-reward ratio too far toward potential injury. Especially when you compare it to the sustained benefits of a consistent, progressive and measured training approach.

For runners who respond well to training, substantial aerobic gains are achievable with 7–8 hours of weekly training. They may need to push harder to unlock them, but it is important to remember that the 5k is predominantly an aerobic event. Research indicates that approximately 85–90% of the energy demands of a 5k rely on aerobic metabolism. For most amateur runners, the primary limiter is not a lack of speed but underdeveloped aerobic capacity. Training studies, such as those by Billat et al. (2003), demonstrate that consistent aerobic volume enhances VO2 max and running economy, leading to faster times without the need for high-intensity speed work.

Of course, it would be unwise to recommend this approach to professional or aspiring professional athletes, as this training framework was never designed for them. Elite runners, having already maximized their easy aerobic gains, rely on fine-tuned, event-specific details to unlock the last of their potential. For example, in a tightly contested 5k where the final 200 meters comes down to a final kick in a slow race, raw speed can often separate a medalist from the pack. Such specificity is critical for elites, as it can determine whether they secure an Olympic medal, make the final, or even earn a spot on their national team. However, as someone running a near-flat 15-minute 5k like me, I still don’t see specificity as a priority. Ask yourself: should I be overly concerned about it? For a 13–14-minute 5k runner who has exhausted other avenues, focusing on speed and that final edge is logical. But for an 18-minute 5k runner? The factors limiting performance are rarely a 400m closing lap or 200m sprint speed. The evidence points to aerobic fitness still as the key bottleneck. Building a stronger aerobic base remains the most effective and lowest-risk path to faster times, deferring speed-focused training until much later in the progression.

Strides, Plyometrics, Stretching, Bicarb, Strength Work

I am going to cover a list of things I don’t do, mostly because they are frequently asked about. The list of things I don’t do: strides, plyometrics, focus on stretching, strength work, and although I’ve tried bicarbonate (bicarb) for over a month I noticed no benefits.

What does this mean for you? Not much. Sometimes, certain practices are unavoidable, as I’ve mentioned before. For instance, if you have a specific injury, you might need targeted stretching or strength work tailored to your situation. The same applies to strength training. If a physiotherapist or sports professional has recommended something specific, don’t suddenly drop it based on my approach. Just because I don’t do these things doesn’t mean you shouldn’t. However, I take a comprehensive approach, weighing the risks and rewards of what I include in my training. You can assume that these elements—strides, plyometrics, stretching, strength work—are probably not essential or at the top of the priority list in the grand scheme of things. If you have only an hour a day to dedicate to training, outside of regular life responsibilities, I’d suggest focusing that entire hour on running. Running should always be the priority in my view, as it’s the most direct way to improve your performance.

Regarding strides, I used to incorporate them early in my training, but they made little difference to my overall performance. There is no universal consensus that strides are essential for all runners. Some studies suggest they improve running economy and speed, while others show minimal impact for non-elite runners. As someone who has torn hamstrings playing soccer in my youth, I am sensitive to hamstring injuries. The only time I have felt twinges in my hamstrings while running was when incorporating strides into easy runs or warm-ups on subthreshold days.

Given that the benefits of strides are debated, the risk of aggravating my hamstrings is far too high for me to consider them worth worrying about. With sensible race plans and pacing, I still perform well in 5k races despite not incorporating strides. My racing isn’t limited by leg turnover or the ability to surge, but rather by my aerobic capacity.

As for stretching, while it can be useful, research shows it’s primarily beneficial when addressing specific issues. A 2013 study by Simic and colleagues showed just this. They found stretching to be helpful for things like tight hip flexors that restrict stride length. Many runners perform well without dedicated stretching routines, relying instead on dynamic warm-ups or general mobility. Unless you have a specific problem, extensive stretching “just because” may not be necessary.

As far as taking bicarbonate, I have had little success. I tested it for over a month but noticed no improvement in performance. Research on sodium bicarbonate shows mixed results. It can act as a buffer for acidity during high-intensity efforts (like 1–10minute races), potentially improving performance, but the effectiveness varies widely. Some studies, such as Burke (2013), suggest benefits for specific contexts, while others note that side effects like stomach upset can outweigh advantages for many runners, which aligns with my experience. I understand people are taking it for longer efforts, but for me it has not proved beneficial.

As for strength work or plyometrics, I won’t insult your intelligence by pretending to guide you on these topics. While I don’t prioritize them, I’ve read Strength and Conditioning for Endurance Running by Richard Blagrove, which I highly recommend. I have used a few of the suggestions to overcome a few minor injuries, such as targeted glute work that might have been creating imbalances in the body. It’s not only an excellent starting point but also the gold standard for all the information you’re likely to need on these subjects.

## The Plans

### Example Weeks

So, what might a training week look like? I’m not here to provide countless variations, but moving forward, I’ll outline a basic framework for a week based on your available hours. Note that the workout day encompasses more than just the workout itself; it includes warm-ups, cool-downs, and rest periods, as discussed earlier.

The intention is to maintain minimal variation, progressing gradually from one structured framework to the next. This provides a clear guide for your training. You should aim to ramp up slowly, avoiding weekly jumps in volume. To minimize risk, consolidate your current training range for at least a month before advancing. As previously mentioned, you might add one minute of workout time for every four minutes of easy running. As you progress, maintaining the original ratio becomes unsustainable, and by the 7-hour weekly training mark, the split shifts to approximately a 78/22% easy-to-workout ratio, as noted earlier. At the upper end, this aligns more closely with the classic 80/20 approach, but with a distinctly different perspective on controlling intensity. If you’re familiar with literature on the subject of 80/20 training, you will recognize that the intensity of the “20” is often way beyond the intensity suggested in this book.

As a simple example, before progressing from the 5–5.5-hour weekly training range to 6 hours, consider increasing from 8 x 3-minute intervals to 9 x 3-minute intervals while adding 12 minutes of easy running distributed across the week’s easy runs. Consolidate this adjustment for an additional two weeks beyond the initial month before fully transitioning to the 6-hour structure. This means committing to at least four weeks at the current level, followed by another 2–3 weeks of this interim increase before moving to the next training structure outlined in the subsequent chart. This approach underscores the importance of long-term thinking. If you’re seeking shortterm miracles, this may be the moment to recognize that this framework prioritizes manageable, progressive, and sustainable structures for medium- and long-term gains.

Within Intervals.icu, ‘ramp’ is a key metric, simply reflecting the weekly increase in your weighted training load. Traditional training methods often advocate a ramp rate of 3–6, whereas my ramp rate has rarely exceeded 2 and is frequently below 1. This underscores the long-term perspective of this approach. A slower ramp rate can, over time, surpass the gains of a more aggressive one.

Joe Friel notes: “A reasonable ramp rate is one that you maintain for a few weeks before taking a break for a few days. While it depends greatly on the athlete, I’ve found that an increase in CTL (fitness score) of about 5 to 8 points per week is about right for most.”

While there’s nothing inherently wrong with this statement in theory, the quote reveals a flaw: it describes the boom-and-bust cycle. The first issue is that such a ramp rate is often sustainable for only a few weeks—sometimes as little as two, in my experience. The second issue is not just the need for a few days off, but potentially an entire recovery week. This encapsulates the boom-and-bust cycle and illustrates why my approach, with its gradual progression, leads to a better outcome in the long run. By maintaining a slow, steady ramp rate, you can consistently outpace the cumulative gains of a high ramp rate without the setbacks of mandatory recovery periods that cause progress to crash.

Tip: The key to progression is increasing load through more work in the easy and subthreshold domains, not increasing pace before you are ready. When people increase pace to try and add “more intensity” they enter into the threshold range and then the three-sessions-a-week system fails to be sustainable.

## Racing

### Tapering

To explain my views on tapering, we must revisit my cycling days. Everything I know comes from a group of data-obsessed, Lycra-clad, middle-aged geeks who meticulously studied their data and performances. A group I then ironically joined. Their insights informed how I plan tapers for my races. They taught me the importance of leveraging data from platforms like Intervals.icu to assess training load and fatigue. In its simplest form, tapering is about trading some of your hard-earned fitness for freshness. The amount of fitness you sacrifice depends on your starting fitness level and the fatigue you have accumulated. Let me explain this through an example.

Imagine a runner with a high fitness score, logging over 10 hours—potentially 13–14 hours—of training per week. This runner has substantial fitness to “cash in” and significant fatigue to shed. Their fitness has likely reached the point of diminishing returns, allowing them to trade more fitness for freshness with minimal long-term detriment. For the race, the benefits of reducing fatigue outweigh any small fitness loss. Conversely, a runner with a lower fitness score likely hasn’t hit the point of diminishing returns. This means that the same taper duration, while still exchanging fitness for freshness, may erode future long term fitness gains. This is because the athlete is still on a steeper improvement curve. In addition to this, lower training volumes/loads mean less fatigue, making a taper less important.

Remember, tapering is about shedding fatigue. The more you train, the longer the taper you likely need and can afford. For those training less, they carry less fatigue, and a shorter, sharper taper is more effective. Also consider that their training volume is more precious to give away at lower weekly hours. The system laid out in this book is designed to carry minimal fatigue. That means for the intended distances of 5k to half-marathon, tapers are less critical. This is especially true for non-A races where the long term goal of getting fitter is the primary objective.

The literature supports this sentiment. For example, Busso et al. (2005) found that cyclists with high training volumes benefited from a 14-day taper with reduced volume, improving time trial performance by 2.5%. Cyclists with moderate training loads, however, saw better results with a 7-day taper, as longer tapers risked performance declines due to fitness loss.

In Intervals.icu, fitness reflects training load, while form represents fatigue. If you train week to week and month to month in the same way, your form typically lingers in the grey zone on the chart. This indicates you are neither fully recovered nor deeply fatigued. It doesn’t take long to return to full freshness and race-ready condition. Typically, in this system, we hover around a low single-digit negative form score. A positive blue number signals that you’re coming into form and feeling fresh. Keep in mind that coming into form comes at the cost of trading in some of your training load.

If you remember nothing else about how form should look on the charts week over week, remember this rhyme: “Green too keen, grey OK”.

The data shared by my nerdy lycra-clad cycling group put these theories to the test, providing real-world insights into tapering strategies. I am fortunate to still have access to much of the data shared by our cycling group. The group amassed all of our collective training and racing data. The data is overwhelmingly consistent in showing that the higher the training load, the more you could cash in that load while still retaining fitness. That is until, inevitably, the balance tips and you can’t hold onto your fitness any longer. For a fitness score of 100, the optimal approach was to plan a taper targeting a form score in the +20 range. This reduced the fitness score to the 80s but allowed cyclists to retain enough fitness, bolstered by positive form, to maintain performance and gain a small race-day boost of 1–2%.

These findings are further supported by Morton et al. (1994). They showed that athletes training 12–14 hours per week gained a 2% performance boost after a 14-day taper with 60% volume reduction. Those training 4–6 hours per week, however, performed best with a 5-day taper, as longer tapers reduced fitness and hurt performance.

In our group data, for someone with a fitness score around 60, tapering into the +12 form range was typically the limit before performance declined in spite of the taper. This reinforces the idea that you can’t afford to sacrifice much of your gains. The trade-off between fitness and positive form is proportional to your initial fitness level. A rider with the lowest consistent training load—around a fitness score of 40 and 4–5 hours per week found they could only push form to about +5 or +6 before performance risked being worse than with no taper at all. When tapers were optimized to place everyone in their ideal form range, a 1–2% boost in power output was typically observed. This makes tapering well worth it, and my own running experience suggests these principles transfer effectively. I've been using and encouraging other hobby runners to use exactly the same principles.

Given the training volume typical of hobby runners, we are often at the lower end of the fitness load spectrum. In simplistic terms, based on this theory, our tapers should be shorter rather than longer. One of the biggest mistakes people make is opting for a week-long (or longer) taper when their training load is relatively light to begin with.

There is also a psychological factor at play. When you train consistently over a long period, an abrupt and aggressive week-long shutdown can signal to your body that it’s on a complete break. This makes it challenging to retain sharpness on race day. Physiologically, extended tapers in athletes with lower training loads can lead to reduced muscle glycogen stores and diminished neuromuscular activation. Studies suggest that excessive rest may impair muscle readiness and VO2 max efficiency, potentially reducing 5km running performance by 1.5% (Mujika & Padilla, 2003; Mujika et al., 2000).

Even when you have carried out a taper correctly, there comes a point after the race where it all settles back down. The freshness and form then become overshadowed by the decay in fitness. You will have to start to build back up again. There is a defined lag to this, but ultimately it means you may take a step back in fitness after a taper, even if it was a successful race. I found this after my London Marathon. There was a ‘goldilocks’ period for 2-3 weeks post marathon where despite the vast reduction in load, I held onto this peak fitness. Unfortunately, there was then a clear drop off point and I was no longer ‘holding on’ to the performance benefits of the huge peak in training load. So, when you do choose to taper, make sure you choose wisely, you can only cheat the system for so long!

What Our Running Taper Can Look Like

I will share my approach to tapering, which has proven effective in achieving a noticeable performance boost on race day. This tapering method enables a swift return to training without too much loss in fitness. It also helps avoid the boom-and-bust cycle found in traditional training programs, which sometimes prescribe one to two weeks of significantly reduced activity post-race. To me, this seems excessive and risks losing valuable fitness. While full recovery is essential (particularly for the unique challenge of a marathon), the races we are discussing don’t require extended downtime. Unless it’s a high-priority race, for a 5k or parkrun I typically skip tapering altogether. I don’t reduce training before or after the event—just continue as usual. I’ve found that unless it’s a key race, tapering makes little difference for the 5k. However, I’ve noticed that tapering becomes increasingly beneficial as race distances increase.

### The Warm Up

My preference on warming up for a race is to keep it simple yet effective. For shorter races, I opt for a longer and slightly more intense warm-up. For longer races, I prefer a shorter and less intense warm-up. Before a 5k, I warm up with about 5 kilometers of easy running with the addition of two 400-meter reps at roughly half-marathon effort, separated by 60 seconds of recovery. This approach gets me to the start line with a light sweat and ready to race, but not so energized that I risk starting too fast. For a 10k, I follow a similar routine but reduce it to one 400-meter rep at half-marathon effort and a total of 3–4 kilometers. For a half-marathon the race distance allows for a gradual build, so I warm up with just 2–3 kilometers at an easy pace, then hit the start line and race. Research supports this approach. Studies indicate that a thorough warm-up incorporating jogging and dynamic efforts like strides enhances performance by increasing muscle temperature and improving oxygen delivery for shorter races like the 5k. This prepares the body for high-intensity efforts from the start. A shorter warm-up conserves energy while still priming the body for longer races like the half-marathon. (Bishop, 2003) I find that the literature and my real world experiences align here. I hope this gives you something to work with that is practical and stress free.

### Pacing

Here is what most people don’t tell you about pacing: Poor pacing can significantly hinder performance, but good pacing can lead to essentially free immediate gains. Mastering pacing is both a science and an art form. It feels intuitive in many ways, yet it can be practiced and refined over time. My background in time trial cycling gives me a distinct advantage. Time trial pacing is a direct battle against the clock. Riders typically start at one-minute intervals and occasionally pass others on the course. In cycling’s time trial circles, pacing is a fundamental skill. Unfortunately, I am not convinced it holds such a high priority in running communities. Cyclists have power meters that provide a significant advantage by allowing riders to set a target power output, unaffected by variables like wind and hills. The strategy is straightforward: start slightly below target power, maintain goal power through the middle, and empty the tank in the final stretch. This simple approach is highly effective due to the reliability of power data. Unfortunately, running lacks a similarly accurate and consistent power metric. Despite efforts from tools like Stryd (which I’ve used and still own) the data isn’t reliable enough to trust. It is especially not something I would want to risk relying on in an important race.

Pacing in running can be straightforward. In order to pace appropriately you need to have:

1. Completed the appropriate training.
2. Know your fitness level – something runners struggle to assess realistically.
3. Have the energy reserves to reach the finish line.

Think of it like driving a car with a full tank of fuel and a 200-mile range. If you drive efficiently by maintaining a steady speed, avoiding sudden acceleration, and keeping the engine in the right gear, you can cover the full 200 miles. But if you floor the accelerator, over-rev the engine, or weave recklessly through traffic, you will burn through fuel faster and end up stranded. Running a race is much the same. If you start too fast or push too hard early on, you will fade before the finish line. Pace wisely and you will instead cross the line strong, just emptying the tank at the finish.

For most of us, running is a glorified time trial. This is particularly true of the local and regional 5ks or 10ks. Even for those vying for the podium, where gaps of minutes can separate the top runners, this is still true. We are ultimately each running our own personal time trial within the confines of a mass-start race.

While highly competitive races may involve tactical elements like drafting, most hobby runners benefit from treating the race as an individual effort against the clock. Studies show that a negative-split pacing strategy—running the second half faster than the first—optimizes performance by conserving energy early and preventing premature fatigue (Abbiss, 2008). Adopting this negative split time-trial mindset makes it easier to stick to your personal race plan. This allows you to maintain discipline and resist the urge to follow others’ paces. While weather conditions can influence performance, the negative-split strategy mentioned will typically lead to the fastest times.

Let’s take a step back to discuss how to determine goal pace. While most runners race the 5k distance more frequently, I will address that pacing later. I find it easiest to explain a 10k race pace. The consistency of repeating the same workouts week after week, while seemingly monotonous, offers significant advantages for pacing. This is particularly true at the 10k or half-marathon distances. Workouts like 3-minute and 10-minute repetitions can directly inform your goal pace by providing a reliable framework for setting realistic targets.

In favorable conditions, I have found that my final few subthreshold workouts of 3-minute repetitions typically are run at 95–96% of my upcoming 10k race pace. Similarly, my 10-minute repetitions align closely with 95–96% of my upcoming half-marathon pace. This method has proven remarkably accurate. It provides a reliable starting point for setting goal paces, especially for distances I may not race as frequently as a 5k. For example, if I have recently averaged 3:12 per kilometer across multiple 3-minute or 1-kilometer repetitions in the weeks leading up to a race, I can expect to run approximately 4% faster than that pace. This assumes that the race conditions are similar to my training. That would put me around 3:05 per kilometer—resulting in a 10k time close to 31:00. This adjustment accounts for a road course likely running long and a short taper. I might jump to the more aggressive range of 5% faster if I am coming into the race feeling really fresh.

To calculate 10k pace, convert the workout pace to total seconds per kilometer, e.g., 3:12 per kilometer equals 192 seconds per km. Multiply by 0.96 to account for a 4% faster race pace, yielding 184 seconds rounded. Then, convert back to minutes per kilometer taking into account the rounding range, resulting in approximately 3:04–3:05 per kilometer.

On a flat course with decent conditions, this prediction has proven overwhelmingly reliable for myself and others. For those new to this approach or lacking extensive data, I recommend starting conservatively at maybe only 3-4% faster than your regular workout paces and adjusting your strategy from there.

This approach makes pacing a race relatively straightforward. In adverse conditions, you can refer to the pacing charts I shared earlier to adjust accordingly. Under favorable conditions, the race should feel much like a time trial. The strategy is simple: start conservatively, maintain a steady target pace through the middle, then go ape shit for the finish. That’s all there is to it. Simple, right? About as easy as following the first rule of Fight Club without breaking it.

Tip: The most effective way to execute this pacing strategy is to set up your watch correctly. On a Garmin, I configure it to display lap pace (1km splits), heart rate (mostly ignored, maybe a brief eye kept on it), and total average pace. Nothing more. This keeps things simple and avoids overcomplication.

10k Pacing

Let me put this strategy into a practical example. For a 10k race where I am targeting a 31:00 10k at an average pace of 3:05 per kilometer, I ensure the first kilometer is slightly conservative, around 3:08 or 3:09 per kilometer. I do this no matter what. If other runners surge ahead, I let them go—they’re either faster or they’ll fade and I’ll catch them later. A recent race illustrates this perfectly. I believed I had a shot at winning, but another runner went out at 2:58 for the first kilometer. I stuck to my 3:09 pace. He was either going to shatter the course record or had started too fast. I won the race in 31:09, catching him just before the 2km mark, while he faded to a 34-something finish, placing fifth. A similar scenario unfolded in a recent half-marathon. It is surprising how often runners undermine their hard-earned training by starting too aggressively. Overaggressive starts result in times far slower than what better pacing could achieve. Beyond wasting potential, suboptimal strategies make it difficult to accurately assess the effectiveness of your training because your race time doesn’t reflect your true capability.

I observe this pattern at the front of the field in at least 90% of races. No matter the level, at least one runner always seems to start too fast. Even when I was a relatively slower runner, I consistently passed dozens of competitors in the second half by adhering to these pacing principles. This holds true wherever you might be in the pack. Runners around you will often surge ahead at unsustainable paces, only to fade as the race progresses. The key for you is to avoid the same trap.

Returning to the example of a 10k race, after starting conservatively with a first kilometer at around 3:08 or 3:09 per kilometer, I aimed to settle into splits of approximately 3:06-3:07 per kilometer for the next couple of kilometers. As the race progresses, I gradually increase the pace to hit my target of 3:05 per kilometer. It's up to you if you choose to set a target to give you alerts on your watch, or simply keep a close eye on it yourself. With two kilometers remaining, I begin to push beyond this target pace, and in the final kilometer, I give it everything, completely emptying the tank. The technical term for this is the “go nuts” “go mental” or “go apeshit” phase of the race. This negative-split strategy typically yields excellent results, particularly once you master the approach. Reviewing data from actual race efforts reveals a consistent, effective ramp-up in pace. This ensures you don't overload your system with metabolic byproducts until the very end of the race, as opposed to a positive split race.

While heart rate as an isolated metric or number doesn’t provide a complete picture, analyzing your data post-race should reveal a clear pattern of progression. In races like a 10k or half-marathon, you should observe a distinct start, middle, and end, with HR reflecting a linear increase over the course of the event. For a 5k, as we’ll discuss later, HR typically shows a steady ramp-up, reaching a point where you’re either completely spent or feel like you’re pushing death by the end. It can genuinely feel that intense. This controlled progression reflects the efficient distribution of energy and effort.

Half Marathon Pacing

Setting realistic expectations based on your current fitness level is critical for a successful half marathon. An unrealistic goal pace can be as detrimental as starting the first 2km 10 seconds per kilometer too fast. During a half marathon, I occasionally monitor my heart rate to ensure it remains safely below my LTHR for at least the first 10 km. This confirms sustainable pacing and reduces the risk of burning out, ensuring my target pace is appropriate. For example, a 2017 Strava study analyzing over 40,000 half marathon performances found that runners who kept their heart rate 5–10 bpm below LTHR for the first 10 km were less likely to slow significantly in the second half compared to those who started at or above LTHR (Vickers & Vertosick, 2017).

For pacing a half marathon, the strategy is similar to the 10k just scaled for a longer distance. Use the 4-5% faster (3% if being cautious) of your 10 minute repetition pace as a guide. Start the first 2–3 km at a slightly conservative pace, then gradually increase the effort. With 3–4 km remaining, you can push harder and exceed LTHR as the shorter duration makes lactate accumulation sustainable. Even professionals do this. During the 2019 Vitality Big Half in London, Mo Farah maintained a controlled pace below his LTHR for the first 10–12 km before surging in the final 5 km, likely exceeding LTHR, to finish with a strong negative split (BBC Sport, 2019). In the final kilometer, you should fully commit to emptying the tank and enjoy the “go nuts” or as Emil Zapotek might say, the “dying a little” phase.

The most common mistake in a half marathon is starting too aggressively. Early on, HR often appears lower than expected. This is partly due to the effects of tapering and the initial effort feels quite easy. The low early HR creates a false sense of security that tempts you to push harder than your goal pace. This is a critical error. The lower-than-expected HR that occurs early on does not indicate that the effort is sustainable. Going out too hard inflicts damage and fatigue that can be hard to recover from. You may not feel it early in the race, but it accumulates and will significantly impact performance later.

When you exceed your sustainable pace early in a race, you rapidly deplete glycogen stores and accumulate excessive lactate, along with H+ (hydrogen ions, which lower muscle pH and cause acidosis), Pi (inorganic phosphate, a byproduct reducing muscle force), and ADP (adenosine diphosphate, signaling energy depletion), which cannot be fully cleared or replenished during the event (Allen et al., 2008). Thermoregulation (regulation of body temperature) is also optimally managed with a negative split rather than a positive split. If you start too fast and increase your core body temperature too high, your body will not perform at its peak. These factors lead to a pronounced slowdown in the second half, as your body struggles to maintain output in a toxic environment with depleted energy reserves. This results in a suboptimal race time. Similar to battling hills or headwinds, an overly fast start results in greater time losses than the time you banked once you inevitably slow down. Additionally, there is a psychological impact to consider. Slowing down in the second half after an overly fast start feels demoralizing as others pass you. This erodes confidence and the drive to push. Conversely, pacing wisely provides a mental boost as you steadily overtake others in the latter stages. This fosters a sense of control and enhances your overall race experience.

There’s no escaping this. You cannot maintain the benefits of an aggressive start if your energy reserves are depleted before the end of the race. For example, a runner targeting a 1:45 half marathon (5:00 min/km pace) might gain 40 seconds over goal time by running the first 4 km at 4:50 min/km (10 seconds/km too fast) but, due to fatigue, slow to 5:30 min/km for the final 4 km, losing 120 seconds compared to their goal pace, resulting in a net loss of 80 seconds and a slower overall time. Also, remember that because they will be spending more time running at a slower pace, they will be going further into a deficit. This is very basic but incredibly common. The irony is that it is actually easier to race with the pacing I use. It’s not complex, but it is hard for people to execute.

5k Pacing

Despite being shorter than a half marathon or 10k, the 5k remains a demanding aerobic event. Typically, a negative split strategy is still optimal. If you compete in 5k races frequently, you can use your prior performances to gauge current fitness and guide pacing. With consistent progress, I am often achieving personal bests. Because I am always slowly increasing my fitness, I know that I can start about one second per kilometer slower than my most recent 5k race. I can build into the race and likely set a new PB. Patterns in your own training can also help assess your 5k fitness. The recent 5k example I’ll share below demonstrates how 5ks require a controlled, steady build — akin to a slow burn. This allows you to ramp up the pace and finish exceptionally strong. Aim to cross the finish line fully spent. Expect to feel like death and questioning your life choice to even start running. If you aren’t running 5ks on a regular basis to guide where you are at, you can also use workouts to shape where to pitch your next 5k. Whilst the relationship isn’t quite as clean as with 10k-HMs, I have found that typically my last set of 3-minute/1k subthreshold repeats have been run at around 93% per kilometer of my next 5k. There are also many online calculators you can use to enter a recent race distance to give you a reasonable equivalent time and target for a 5k.

In a 5k race, the initial pace can be incredibly fast as runners jockey for position. Sometimes to secure a clear run or a favorable spot, you may need to briefly surge for the first 5 to 10 seconds. This short burst, often 3-6% faster than your goal pace, allows you to navigate early congestion without getting boxed in. This is particularly important on narrow courses. Research from the University of New Hampshire supports this approach, showing that moderately trained runners achieved faster 5k times when starting slightly above their baseline pace, then settling into goal pace (Gosztyla et al., 2006). Physiologically, this brief effort relies on the phosphagen system, which fuels high-intensity bursts of 5-10 seconds, preserving glycogen stores for the race’s duration. However, pacing discipline remains critical beyond this brief initial surge. Extending this effort beyond roughly 12 seconds risks creating a deficit you cannot return from. In the example I’ll share, I pushed hard off the line, running well above my goal pace for about 5-10 seconds to reach the front and lead through a narrow section. This brief sprint utilized energy similar to that in short strides, protecting my aerobic reserves for a sustainable race pace thereafter.

With about 400-500 meters remaining in a 5k, pacing discipline often gives way to an all-out effort as you empty the tank to maximize your finish. Thus, aside from the initial 5-10 seconds of a fast surge to secure position and the final 400-500 meters of all-out running, optimal pacing principles remain critical throughout the race. A pacing disaster, or "blowing up," in a 5k has relatively limited consequences compared to longer races. For instance, a blow-up might cost you 10 seconds in kilometer 4 and 15 seconds in the final kilometer, totaling a loss of about 25 seconds. In contrast, a similar miscalculation in a 10k, occurring at 60-70% of the distance, could lead to 3-4 suboptimal kilometers, potentially costing a minute or more. In a half-marathon or, worse, a marathon, a blow-up could result in losses of 10 minutes or more. In the longer races, glycogen depletion and compounding fatigue slow you significantly over the remaining distance, with potentially catastrophic impacts on your overall time.

Sirpoc's Sidebar: The Speed-Duration Curve

You may be familiar with the concept of a power-duration curve, or a critical speed curve. What these seek to do is illustrate the relationship between an athlete’s capabilities at races that last increasing greater periods of time. Below is the model for what a curve might look like, formed by the men’s and women’s world record performances across a variety of events.

![Chart](image_rsrc52C.jpg)

What you see is that the athlete’s maximum reachable speed for 1 second is very similar to their speed for 10 seconds, which lines up with what you might expect. These race durations often rely on a lot of contribution from the ATP-PC system, a way of producing energy rapidly over short periods of time without some of the byproducts that might come from anaerobic glycolysis. Once you begin to get into race distances that are between 1 minute (60 seconds) and 20 minutes (1200 seconds) in duration, the speed continues to decrease, but begins to look like it will plateau around what is typically considered to be critical speed. Critical speed represents a delineation between unsustainable and sustainable exercise. However, just because we are functioning in a “metabolic steady state,” doesn’t mean we can sustain such an intensity indefinitely, as might be theoretically possible. In reality, we run into issues with localized muscle glycogen depletion, central nervous system fatigue, dehydration, and potentially even issues with a “central governor” that might cap our ability to put out power long term. As a result, what is often observed is a continued decline in race speed sustainable as race duration increases beyond this point, which is why you see the continued gradual (but only slight) decline in speed as the graph begins to go past 30 minutes (1800 seconds).

As we move into this new era of increased fueling and hydration over longer distances, we can often make this relationship between our critical speed and our longer duration race performances tighter. That is, there is less of a decline in performance as duration increases, provided we are adequately aerobically trained. But in all circumstances, pacing and an understanding of one’s individual physiology is crucial to ensuring effective race execution across all distances.

Adjustments

Clearly there are other factors that influence race performance. When planning your race, you need to account for the previously discussed variables like wind, hills, and heat. Consider a 10k out-and-back course with 100 meters of elevation gain and loss, 18 km/hr winds, and 22°C heat. A goal pace of 3:05 per kilometer becomes unrealistic under these conditions and most people fail to account for this. For example, the first kilometer, entirely uphill with a 10-meter climb, might slow you by at least 8 seconds. A tailwind could offset this by about 4 seconds, but the heat may cost an additional 4-5 seconds. This complexity requires adjusting the target for that first kilometer to approximately 3:13 per kilometer, and to avoid starting too fast, a conservative 3:17 per kilometer may be wiser. Not every course demands such detailed calculations and not all runners need to plan with this precision. However, I approach nearly every race this way to optimize my performance. This strategy relies on trusting the process, your watch, and the confidence that disciplined pacing will yield the best possible outcome. Even with this knowledge, it’s easy to get caught up in the moment and start too fast, following another runner’s pace or because we feel great. We all make this mistake, and I’m sure I will do it again too.

Over time, you may shift toward pacing by feel, relying more on instinct than rigid calculations or constant watch-checking. While this approach is less precise, it can be practiced and honed to an extent. The best pacing advice I have encountered comes from legendary British track and time trial cyclist Chris Boardman, talking about such matters on the soon to be missed and equally legendary ITV Tour de France coverage. He employs a continuous check-and-balance process during races. He ensures he doesn’t start too aggressively. By the first third of the race, he asks himself: “Can I maintain this pace to the finish?” If the answer is “no”, it’s likely to be a painful day leading to a blow-up. If the answer is “yes”, he may have started too conservatively, leaving time on the table. If the answer is “maybe,” he’s in the optimal zone. Continue this process throughout the race until you have the end in sight. He is balancing doubts about sustaining the intensity with confidence that he’ll manage. In the final 10% of a race, even if the answer leans toward no, sheer will and determination combined with proper training will often carry you through. That is the art of pacing.

Summary

Time trial cycling taught me the critical importance of pacing. You simply cannot run faster than your fitness level allows. Your consistent weekly workouts should realistically indicate your fitness. Use those workouts to gauge your current fitness, but also factor in the day’s conditions to set achievable expectations. Wind, hills, heat, or a combination of all three will demand adjustments to your pace. Refer to the pacing adjustment charts discussed earlier in the book to adapt accordingly. Understanding these conditions can help you find solace in comparing races: a slower time in Race A versus Race B might reflect a smarter, better-executed effort under tougher conditions.

Most runners struggle with pacing, often positive splitting (starting too fast and fading) because they overestimate their fitness. There’s no getting away from the facts: negative splitting leads to better performances. Yet runners don’t seem to like this truth. The goal of this chapter was to help you rethink and reshape your approach to achieve better post-race moods and post-race beers. To make this happen, set your watch up correctly and start conservatively. Many runners resist being tethered to their watches, but the unfortunate truth is that relying on one can significantly improve your race. Just remember the structure: start conservatively, settle into your goal pace for the middle segment, and go apeshit at the end. If that last sentence isn’t the most valuable in the book, then I’ll buy you a beer!

## Marathon Adaptations

I hesitated to include this section for several reasons. My primary concern is that someone might misinterpret it as a standalone marathon plan, which it absolutely is not. If you have been training for only three months, you lack the necessary base to utilize this plan effectively. Six months may even be insufficient. However, if you have been training consistently for longer and are approaching or exceeding the 7+ hour weekly training example outlined earlier, you might be ready to consider this adaptation. Keep in mind, though, that I was two years into my training when I implemented the plan I am about to lay out.

This marathon plan was intentionally designed as an extension of my own running journey. After training this way consistently for an extended period, I incorporated adaptations and changes that I believed would optimize my chances of running a strong marathon. Running a marathon was a goal I had always declared off-limits, much like an Oasis reunion. However, qualifying for London through a half-marathon time and facing a life-altering event like Noel’s costly divorce, can lead to unexpected decisions. While not my plan, it would have been a missed opportunity to not run the London Marathon. I had a guaranteed entry to the championship start line, and some people spend half a lifetime trying to secure a spot.

By Christmas, my entry was confirmed. Once committed to a goal, you cannot turn back or you will ruin your trust with yourself. Barring illness or injury, I was toeing the start line on April 27th. My goal was to break 2:30, a target I believed was within reach. To me, this would be a monumental lifetime achievement. Yet, a man can dream. Being ever ambitious, I had 2:25 in mind as the dream outcome.

On January 2nd, while at the pub watching Luke Littler claim his first World Darts Championship, I laid out the foundation. I carefully crafted the weeks of training that would give me the best chance to achieve what some might call an over-ambitious goal.

I nearly executed that exact plan. The only deviations were the addition of parkrun one week due to favorable weather (you have to seize opportunities for a personal best) and two sporadic Mondays where I slightly extended my easy run. Because my prior performances followed a consistent trend with my training load, I had a rough sense of the volume and training load needed to achieve my goal. My focus was to adhere to the plan, which I hoped would position me to run around 15 minutes for a 5k and break 31 minutes for a 10k during the buildup. Above all, I trusted that strategically increasing my training time would continue to yield results.

I reviewed other marathon training plans and examined the Strava profiles of runners I knew who had completed marathons in the 2:30–2:45 range. Frankly, I struggle to comprehend how some of them endured their training blocks, let alone arrived at the starting line fresh and prepared. For my own 2:25 marathon goal, I studied the Pfitzinger 18/85 plan, which seemed particularly daunting due to its relentless intensity and high mileage. For instance, a standard week around Week 10 included a 24-km medium-long run at a steady 3:53–4:12 min/km pace, a 10-km lactate threshold run at 3:12–3:15 min/km, and a 35-km long run with the final 6–10 km at marathon goal pace (3:26 min/km). The general aerobic runs, typically at 3:53–4:12 min/km (75–80% of max heart rate for me), felt too hard to be considered easy runs. This is an intensity range I was deliberately trying to avoid to prevent excessive fatigue.

The plan’s structure, with VO2 max intervals like 5 x 1200m combined with a rapid ramp-up to 112–128 km per week by Week 6 or 7, felt like a recipe for stacking too much fatigue. To me, this aggressive escalation seemed to risk overloading the body, potentially leading to burnout or breakdown well before the taper. If this level of intensity was required to achieve my marathon goal, I was in big trouble. Nevertheless, I decided to proceed. I shaped my own approach to meet the demands of marathon running. My primary concern was whether I had underestimated the challenge. Curiosity drove me to explore this concept of whether a marathon training block truly required pushing oneself to the brink, as I had often heard described.

Designed around the training philosophy in this book, I structured my marathon training into three mini-blocks followed by a taper. I aimed for a sustainable approach emphasizing consistency combined with sufficient intensity in the final block to replicate the unique challenge of a marathon — its extraordinary length. The distance was my overwhelming concern, and I suspect it’s the main worry for anyone signing up for the challenge.

Phases

Subthreshold Base Phase

The first mini-block was straightforward. Five weeks of consistent training focusing on building a solid foundation. Each day I prioritized getting out the door, completing the work, and accumulating minutes and mileage. I leaned on the approach of this book—three easy runs, three subthreshold sessions, and an easy long run each week. I employed a deliberate pattern for the long runs. Each week I increased the duration by approximately five minutes, aiming to reach a duration equivalent to my 2:25 marathon goal. That meant roughly 2 hours and 25 minutes on my feet achieved about a month before the race. The simplicity of this block was intentional. My sole objective for the long run was to acclimate to the time-on-feet required for a 2:25 marathon, even at an easy pace. Some observers noticed this approach in real time, as I shared my progress.

I had a hunch that time-on-feet could be as critical as intensity, a theory informed by my half-marathon experience. In my initial half-marathons, I hadn’t run the full 21.1 km in training but had accustomed myself to being on my feet for slightly longer than the goal race time, which proved effective. While easy long runs didn’t demand the intensive fueling required during a marathon, they provided an opportunity to gradually build my capacity to consume 100–120 grams of carbohydrates per hour via gels. Experts typically recommend 60–90 grams of carbohydrates per hour for marathon runners, though advanced runners may target up to 120 g/hour. This is supported by a 2014 review by Jeukendrup in Sports Medicine, which highlights that practicing carbohydrate intake trains the stomach to digest gels efficiently, enhances glycogen uptake to sustain energy, and prepares the body for race-day fueling demands all while reducing the risk of gastrointestinal distress. Recognizing that races of this distance demand a robust fueling strategy, I prioritized developing a sensible plan early in the process to meet the marathon’s unique and, quite frankly, inconvenient nutritional challenges.

A challenge arises for runners with slower goal times, such as 3:30 or 4:00 marathons. For these runners, I’d caution against exceeding 180 minutes on long runs, even if their target race time is longer, as the risk of injury outweighs the endurance benefits beyond this point. During this period, I realized I could have comfortably extended my long run by another 30–40 minutes past 2:25 if needed, but pushing beyond 3 hours would likely have been detrimental. Research supports this, with studies like van Gent et al. (2007) in British Journal of Sports Medicine indicating that prolonged running sessions exceeding 3 hours significantly increase the risk of overuse injuries due to cumulative biomechanical stress. By keeping long runs at an easy pace and maxing them out at 2:25, I aimed to mitigate this impact while building endurance, a strategy informed by my half-marathon training where time-on-feet proved more critical than distance. Ultimately, runners should aim to extend the long run to their anticipated time on feet for the race or to 180 mins, whichever is shorter.

Increasing Load Stage

The following five weeks marked a shift toward increasing training load, weekly training time, and mileage to meet the marathon’s demands. Building on the foundation of the previous five weeks, which established a consistent base, I adopted a slightly more aggressive ramp rate, with some weeks peaking at over 100 km. This approach allowed me to absorb the increased volume in a manageable way. I was deliberate about avoiding runs that are popular among other marathon training plans. Things like high-intensity sessions of 90 minutes at 90% of marathon pace (approximately 3:45 min/km) and other marathon specific sessions mentioned earlier. My view is that such runs pose too great a risk of physical stress due to their moderate intensity and high impact. I didn't want any setbacks like my early running days. While these classic marathon-specific workouts may suit runners with higher weekly volume, I believe they are less appropriate for my training level—and likely for those reading this book. This cautious strategy reflects a focus on consistency and minimizing injury risk while preparing for the marathon.

Knowing that injuries are common in marathon builds, I committed to minimizing injury risk as much as possible. I ran within the safe and familiar confines of controlled intervals to keep fatigue at bay. The foundation of this approach was 5km intervals. These were performed at 101-102% of my current estimated marathon pace – with adjustments for the conditions. That was approximately 3:22–3:23 min/km for a 2:25 marathon. My intention was to provide time at a slightly higher intensity than marathon pace while breaking up the effort into manageable segments. Run at a pace slower than our typical 10-minute efforts, these 5 km reps equate to 15–18 (3 min rest) minutes of running. Using time allows flexibility for others to adapt this approach rather than copying distance. If a 23-minute 5k runner strictly adheres to the 3x5k distance in training, they will accrue an additional 15 minutes of running at this sub-T pace – an unsustainable increase in effort when repeated in this block. The exposure to near-marathon-pace intensity without the prolonged impact of continuous runs, underscores the goal of balancing training stress and recovery during this five-week block.

I introduced extended 5 km intervals early in the week on a Tuesday, when I felt freshest. Tuesday follows an easy long run on Sunday and a standard easy day on Monday. This timing aligned with my prior strategy of scheduling slightly longer subthreshold workouts on Tuesdays. I placed intervals here rather than on Saturday which would feed into the ever-extending Sunday long run. The first 3x5 km at 101% of my estimated marathon pace felt challenging but not overly taxing. Comparable to a typical workout day. I finished the session encouraged, particularly since I had set a local parkrun course record three days earlier. Analysis of post-session data confirmed a controlled effort below my lactate threshold (LT2). I scheduled the next 3x5 km session on Saturday, followed by the extended easy long run on Sunday to test how this felt on fatigued legs. The long run felt strong, affirming the strategy of balancing intensity and recovery during this five-week period.

The next session, a 4x5 km interval workout, marked a significant step up. The volume forces one to confront the marathon’s vast demands as well as the challenge of sustaining effort despite mounting fatigue. This session will push you to embrace a degree of overload, testing your will and resolve. The fourth 5 km rep, run at almost 102% of estimated marathon pace was particularly grueling. This presented my first mental setback. Though only slightly faster than marathon effort, I was exhausted. By the end, I questioned how anyone could sustain running over twice that distance without 3-minute recovery breaks. The notion of race-day “magic,” felt ridiculous. On a practical note, this session introduced severe chafing for the first time. At least it provided a distraction from the reality I was actually going to have to run a marathon. It motivated me to spend several days researching the ideal running shorts with pockets suitable for carrying gels on race day. A small tip here is that trail shorts actually often provide the answer. They tend to be designed with long distance comfort in mind, but more importantly, come usually with a vast range and stash of pockets.

Consider using these longer subthreshold sessions to test equipment like kits, shorts, and race shoes. Practice how to carry gels and fueling strategies. My long run fueling strategy felt manageable, but at higher intensities consuming enough carbohydrates was challenging and less comfortable for my stomach. Additionally, mastering the logistics of retrieving, opening, and consuming a gel at race pace proved tricky. I started to practice this during workouts. A helpful tip I discovered was to cut the gel packet corner slightly more than the manufacturer’s perforation, making it easier to tear open. This avoided wrestling with the packet whilst running at pace. No one needs a demoralizing failure-to-open struggle at mile 20.

The plan was to maintain consistent effort but ease off slightly for a few days, as I had a 10k race approaching. This race was a big chance for me to run quickly at the regional level. Consequently, I scaled back to just 3 x 5 km opposed to the four. This allowed me to approach the race slightly less fatigued, marking the end of this five-week training block before increasing training efforts further. By this point, you will likely have completed more training volume than ever before, making a 10k an excellent opportunity to assess fitness and evaluate progress. Post-race, I intended to transition back into a standard training week to continue building momentum.

The 10k went better than I could have dreamed of, and the training clearly paid dividends. I felt remarkably good, almost floating through the race, even during the standard taper leading into the race. A small group pushed ahead early, but I was confident I had the reserves to reel them back in. Ultimately, I won the race in a surprising 30:41, a time that exceeded even my expectations. I had estimated that a 31-minute 10k would put a 2:25 marathon within reach, and this performance confirmed that goal was achievable. With this validation, it was time to enter the final training phase. Even increasing the long run duration posed no issues, signaling time for the next steps.

Special Phase

It was time to maximize the final aerobic gains while incorporating specific, challenging scenarios to push the comfort zone. The body has largely adapted to the prior endurance demands, with physiological improvements like oxygen uptake nearing their peak. However, the psychological advantages of this block are significant. Facing tough sessions builds mental resilience, boosts confidence for race-day challenges, and informs a realistic race plan. The sessions also allow enough time to remain fresh and avoid overtraining. A few substantial training weeks, followed by a half-marathon the next week, was appropriately timed as the final fitness assessment to shape the race strategy.

The half marathon course was mildly hilly, but I ran under 1:10 in less than ideal conditions. This indicated I was in 2:25 shape for the London Marathon. It felt effortless, as if gliding. I was well prepared and able to quickly overtake the leaders and surged to win in 1:08:51. That being said, I believe a half marathon aligns more closely with a 10k than a marathon. Similar to a prolonged cycling time trial, a marathon requires sustaining effort when fatigue sets in. This necessitates tapping into reserves that are hard to access. The 10 days following the half marathon were pivotal and included a critical “special” mini-block. This was potentially the final chance to absorb a training dose. Research, including work by Andrew Coggan, shows that the body typically recovers from training fatigue within 7–10 days, while fitness improvements, like better endurance, take 2–4 weeks to fully develop (Allen & Coggan, 2010). This approach differs from traditional marathon plans, which often peak 4–6 weeks out with more aggressive early training, building significant fatigue, then taper for 3–4 weeks to recover. Instead, I add controlled fatigue even at 2-3 weeks out, leveraging Coggan’s 7–10 day fatigue decay window to shed it in time for race day while still potentially gaining fitness. I think by pushing too hard early, many conventional plans risk peaking prematurely and taper too soon. They are potentially sacrificing late-stage endurance gains (which also provides a psychological boost) and can lead to a loss of that sharp edge come race day.

The big workout, planned for 5 days after the half marathon, was a significant challenge. With minimal tapering and fatigued legs, I aimed to cover over half the marathon distance at a slightly faster pace (101% MP). If completed, I was prepared to trust the advice of others that a marathon taper is unique and transformative. The workout consisted of 5 x 5 km intervals. It was, in a word, brutal. The first two or three repetitions were manageable. The fourth was grueling. The fifth repetition felt nearly as nasty as the 30 km mark in the marathon—though not as punishing as the final half hour of the race, which is unmatched in brutality.

Maintaining consistency in your training and completing a demanding workout like the 5x5km (or 15-18 minute equivalent) session without quitting establishes the resilience needed to endure a marathon with sensible pacing. I was already physically prepared aerobically, but this session provided the psychological edge. This wasn’t about sustaining a pace, but confronting the overwhelming desire to stop. It was about choosing to persevere. There is a huge value in workouts that simulate these mental and physical demands. However, incorporating them too frequently— sometimes weekly, as seen in some plans—can be detrimental. During my London Marathon preparation, I observed runners tackling these intense sessions (like continuous 60+ minutes near marathon pace) sometimes week after week. Some started as early as four months out. Unfortunately, some people didn’t reach the starting line. Others arrived far too fatigued. This undermined their potential by leaving them unable to perform at their best despite a significant taper.

Other workouts that provided valuable specificity included continuous runs without rest. The 8+8+8 km workout was a progressive 24km run, starting at 92% of goal pace, increasing to 93%, and finishing at 100% for the final segment. This session was challenging, but at this stage of training, I was intentionally overloaded. Completing it delivered another significant confidence boost. Successfully executing this workout again suggested that with a taper, race day would almost certainly be executable. There was also a long run incorporating a 10km segment at goal marathon pace in the middle. The block concluded with a long run one week before the race, featuring three 10-minute efforts of standard subthreshold pace.

I constructed the entire marathon training period to methodically progress by incorporating elements incrementally, culminating in an "intense block" designed to peak performance. This block was strategically structured to not only address the physical demands of the race but also to fortify the psychological resilience required for the marathon itself.

The approach proved highly effective for me. The block not only delivered my goal marathon performance but also lowered my personal bests across other distances. A question may arise: why not follow this plan continuously? It is simply not sustainable. This level of training intensity and volume isn’t designed for perpetual use, unlike the standard framework. Instead, it is carefully calibrated to build on top of a substantial training base, providing just enough additional stimulus to prepare for a marathon without risking burnout. The plan meets the race’s physical and psychological demands while adding in a sprinkling of late specificity. A growing number of runners have successfully adopted this marathon “top-up” approach, achieving notable success and personal bests. Initially I was not going to include this section, but the success of other runners suggests that when executed well, this block can deliver. While the significant achievements of other runners in distances from 5k to half-marathon are undeniable using the “vanilla” method, this marathon-specific strategy remains relatively uncharted territory. As with other sections of this book, the goal is not only to outline what to do but also to explain the “how and why” behind the approach. I hope it provides a clear framework for achieving your marathon success. If you find success with it, show a friend so we can get away from thinking injury and self-hate are normal in marathon builds.

Marathon Taper and Race Day

The taper itself is straightforward. I used a sharp, rapid reduction in training volume leading into race day rather than a prolonged, gradual one. Due to a careful buildup, I entered the taper less fatigued than runners who undertake more aggressive training cycles. That is the benefit of such a methodical approach. By the Thursday before race day, I felt an incredible sense of freshness and speed, confirming everything I’d heard about the effectiveness of a marathon taper—and then some. I could have raced that day and still had a strong performance, even though I just started the taper a few days earlier. By race day, I was eager to run. If I run another marathon, I will replicate this taper exactly.

Nothing prepared me for the night before the race. I barely slept, managing only about three hours. Many runners assured me this is common. Fortunately, I had slept well in the two to three days prior, so the lack of sleep didn’t significantly impact me. By this stage, my kit was well-tested, my fueling strategy was set, and I had planned a substantial breakfast. I carb-loaded in the three days leading up to the race. I consumed approximately 400g, 450g, and 500g of carbohydrates respectively, a mixture of food, liquid or just about anything I actually fancied the look of. This approach aims to maximize glycogen stores in the muscles and liver, which can enhance endurance performance by delaying fatigue during a marathon. Research suggests that consuming 7–12g of carbohydrates per kilogram of body weight daily in the 48–72 hours before a race is effective for most runners. They emphasize simple carbs like pasta, rice, and bread due to their digestibility (Burke et al., 2011; Sawka et al., 2007). On race morning, I took in 240g of carbs through a mix of foods. Coffee, a doughnut, porridge, a banana, a cookie, and the secret weapon — a Mars bar. I ate with the unapologetic enthusiasm of Brad Pitt in what feels like every movie scene he has ever been in. I didn’t overthink the specifics. Carbs are carbs, and my priority was avoiding under-fueling. While some might prefer a more textbook approach to the carbs consumed, this worked exceptionally well for me. There are no rigid rules dictating what you must eat to be a serious runner at a non-elite level. In fact, I even had a beer with dinner the day before — a detail you are unlikely to find in other running books!

The main challenge to an exceptional race is determining how to pace your marathon effectively. Honesty with oneself is critical. Pace according to your current ability, not your aspirations. Even if your fitness isn’t at the level you would have hoped. I have observed many runners attribute their marathon struggles to inadequate training or a flawed plan. In reality, the issue lies in setting an unrealistic pacing target based on their fitness and the day’s conditions. This approach inevitably leads to disappointment and, almost certainly, a performance collapse. It wastes a long training block.

Pace according to your current ability, not your aspirations.

As I stood in the queue at Greenwich Park, the heat struck me as significantly warmer than the forecast I used to make my race plan the previous night. Conditions were going to be more challenging than anticipated. Based on my recent half-marathon performance on a hilly course, I estimated I was in 2:22-2:23 marathon shape which was beyond my initial hopes. At the last moment, I recognized the heat’s impact and adjusted my target time by about 90 seconds. This decision was more important than most people realize. Though 90 seconds may seem minor, in the delicate balance of a marathon’s small margins, pursuing a 2:22 pace in those conditions would have almost certainly led to a performance collapse. Additionally, I noticed my heart rate was unusually high before the race. It was already 80–90 BPM while sitting on the train en route and spiked to 120 BPM at the start line. This underscored the immense pressure and adrenaline of a marathon, far exceeding what I’d experienced in other races. It highlighted the event’s magnitude and the pressure I had put on myself to perform.

The second critical point is to adhere strictly to your pacing and race plan. At the start line, I connected with runners aiming to form a 2:25 target group, which initially seemed ideal given my last minute change of plan. As we lined up, we naturally clustered together. The race began and immediately the pace felt effortless—I was floating again. This sensation, however, is where many runners encounter trouble. Most who have trained diligently and tapered appropriately will feel this way early on. This tempts us to start too fast. In a marathon, this urge is even stronger than in shorter races. By 600 meters, it was clear the pace was too quick, and by the first kilometer the 2:25 group surged far ahead. I faced a choice: panic and chase or stay disciplined and form a secondary group. Choosing discipline was my second-best decision of the day. I resisted the urge to pursue them, a choice validated later when I passed nearly all of that group in the latter stages. Some were even walking as their early pace led to a complete collapse. By 5 kilometers, they were out of sight around corners and bends. This experience highlights how critical pace execution is to marathon performance. It also highlights that even at the highest levels it is not discussed enough. Regardless of whether their training plans were optimal or flawed, these runners’ suboptimal race-day pacing rendered their training and preparation irrelevant.

The race felt comically effortless for the first 15 kilometers. The ease is deceptive, as with any race pacing strategy. You have a limited number of matches to burn, and they must be used judiciously. Beyond 15 kilometers, I knew I was truly in a race. At the halfway point or around Tower Bridge, I had twice already questioned whether my pace was sustainable on the Chris Boardman scale of “maybe”. Pacing remained steady, although there was a lively pub on the corner approaching Tower Bridge and I did consider stopping and just settling in with a pint of continental lager to watch the FA Cup semi-finals.

I closely monitored my heart rate throughout. This is the only distance I find more than a glance at HR useful. For a well-trained aerobic runner, the average HR during a marathon typically ranges from 86–88% of maximum heart rate, potentially reaching 89–90% for some (Burke et al., 2011). However, HR often spikes higher toward the end, making it critical to track in the early stages. My goal was to maintain a stable 82–85% of MHR for most of the first half, positioning me well for the race. If HR reaches 86–90% by halfway, the outcome is likely unfavorable, as sustaining that intensity becomes nearly impossible without a significant pace drop off.

A picture around the 40k mark with what can be only described as an unpleasant look depicting the deep black hole I was in. You are running on sheer will by this point, as much as any training you have done. You somehow have to dig into the last reserves of mental toughness you have left. I was almost looking for excuses to quit and walk, but it is something you will regret for years to come. I managed to tap into mental reserves I didn’t know I had and finished strong.

The effort intensified at 30 kilometers; this is probably the so-called “wall”. This point will be overwhelming if you have under-fueled. If that is the case you can kiss the race goodbye. A lot of people run into a double damage issue here if they are under-fueling and have also messed up their pacing. There is also so much going on mentally at this point in the race. No training can prepare you for the grueling final 7–8 kilometers of a marathon. I would talk you through it, but quite frankly it's all a blur. It is an intensely challenging experience. I am grateful I incorporated demanding workouts during the latter stages of training. It helped me endure this pure hell. Sheer willpower and mental fortitude can carry you to the finish if you have done the following: 1) put in the work, 2) made it through the 30km “wall checklist” of pacing, fueling and mental preparation and 3) set a realistic goal based on an honest assessment of your capabilities. Even if you do everything right, you still have to put yourself in the proverbial “pain cave.”

Crossing the line, I was unexpectedly overwhelmed with emotion. So much so that I forgot to check the clock and only learned my time of 2:24 through messages received shortly afterward. The overriding feeling was satisfaction. Satisfaction in having approached the marathon unconventionally and arriving at race day in one piece while executing at a high level (minus some toenail fatalities).

Post-marathon recovery is highly individual (other than the well-earned post-race beer!). That being said, this training approach avoids the deep fatigue some more intense plans may induce. I took a few days off but resumed normal training with my first (reduced intensity) workout six days later. By day 16, I felt phenomenal and set a new 5k personal best. This point probably marked the end of my ability to “hold on” to my marathon taper and high training load. Others that have executed this build have had similar experiences. It is the plan I would recommend to others. That said, my next challenge at 42 years old—aiming to break 2:20—may be an entirely different story.

## Adjuncts

Post Workout Fueling

As someone who once advised you to eat freely during marathon training and on race day, I’m not here to lecture you on balancing your diet. On most days I typically run for an hour and eat what I want. However, I make an exception in the minutes following a workout. Immediately after a workout I focus on post-workout nutrition. Initially, I didn’t prioritize this, but in retrospect, I wish I had. Consuming carbohydrates and protein after exercise is critical for recovery. I aim for a 3:1 ratio of carbs to protein, ideally within 30 minutes, but no later than an hour after exercise. I take this most seriously on workout days.

Running causes partial depletion in muscle glycogen stores and minor muscle fiber damage. Glycogen stores are the body's stored form of carbohydrates and consuming carbohydrates post-workout helps to replenish glycogen. This is essential for energy restoration. The muscular microtrauma needs substrate to rebuild and protein supports muscle repair and synthesis. I target approximately 1.0–1.2 g of carbohydrates per kg of body weight and about 0.25–0.3 g of protein per kg, taken together to optimize recovery. Consuming carbs and protein simultaneously enhances glycogen synthesis and muscle repair due to increased insulin sensitivity post-exercise. This facilitates nutrient uptake (Margolis et al., 2021). A study showed that co-ingesting 1.2 g/kg/hour of carbohydrates with 0.3 g/kg of protein post-exercise significantly improved glycogen re-synthesis compared to carbohydrates alone, with benefits most pronounced when nutrients are consumed within 30–60 minutes (Ivy et al., 2004). Another study confirmed that 0.2–0.4 g/kg of protein, when paired with carbohydrates, supports muscle protein synthesis and recovery in endurance athletes (Burke et al., 2018).

I’ve experimented with exceeding these amounts but saw no additional benefits. That is because glycogen synthesis and protein synthesis rates plateau beyond recommended thresholds. To test if the effects were merely a placebo, I skipped this routine for a week, and my legs felt noticeably more fatigued, reinforcing the importance of this practice.

Carbohydrates are generally affordable and easy to incorporate; for example, a medium banana (about 120 g) provides roughly 27 g of carbs, a slice of white toast with a tablespoon of jam (30 g bread + 15 g jam) offers about 45 g of carbs, or—my absolute favorite, because who can resist that sugary bliss—a jam doughnut (about 70 g) delivers around 30 g of carbs to fuel my post-run joy. Protein can be pricier, but cost-effective options like peanuts (28 g serving, or about a handful) deliver approximately 7 g of protein, a small serving of Greek yogurt (100 g) provides about 10 g of protein, or a protein shake (25 g whey protein powder mixed with water) offers around 20-25 g of protein. Additionally, a 400 ml serving of low-fat chocolate milk provides about 43 g of carbs and 13 g of protein, making it a convenient and affordable option that combines both nutrients. The specific food choice matters less than meeting these approximate targets. It’s a small habit, but these efforts compound in the ongoing battle against fatigue and muscle damage.

Sleep

Sleep may not be a novel topic, but it’s a critical factor in performance. You will notice that patterns of poor sleep will lead to lesser performance and diminished recovery. This typically happens when life’s demands interfere, and there’s little you can do to avoid it entirely. However, disrupted sleep can impair not only your workouts but also your race performance. Research confirms that sleep deprivation, particularly partial sleep restriction (e.g., less than 7 hours per night for multiple days), reduces physical performance, including endurance, strength, and reaction time, while increasing perceived fatigue (Craven et al., 2022). A 2018 study found that two nights of partial sleep restriction (4 hours per night) significantly reduced time to exhaustion during running and cycling tasks, underscoring the impact on endurance athletes like runners (Oliver et al., 2018). The key is to be mindful of this and manage what you can within the confines of a busy life. I’ve observed that when sleep falls below a certain threshold for two to three consecutive days, my performance noticeably declines. If your training or races aren’t going as planned and the cause isn’t clear, consider whether a pattern of inadequate sleep might be the culprit.

Dealing with injuries or illness

Injuries are an unfortunate reality of running, and the key is being honest with yourself. Deep down, I know when I can run and when I can’t. A slight niggle in the calf? I’ll likely keep running. But if the discomfort compromises my form and prevents normal running mechanics, I take the day off. Running with altered mechanics due to injury can exacerbate biomechanical issues, such as increased hip adduction or reduced rearfoot eversion, potentially leading to further injuries like iliotibial band syndrome or anterior knee pain (Ceyssens et al., 2019). One or two days of rest can be a sensible precaution to avoid a more serious, long-term issue. While injuries are likely reduced with this cautious approach, injuries or niggles sometimes occur. It’s about weighing the risks against the rewards. If running isn’t possible but cycling or cross-training is an option, you can adapt this training plan to another discipline. I’ve seen people maintain this books’ schedule through six weeks of cross-training, using heart rate as a guide, and lose very little running fitness.

When it comes to illness, my general rule is to run through minor issues like a common cold. However, if I experience physical sickness or a fever, I prioritize rest over running. Shedding the fatigue caused by illness or fever is more critical than training. Metrics like resting heart rate (RHR) and heart rate variability (HRV), if available via a capable watch, can provide valuable guidance. A 2019 study showed that a drop in HRV or an elevated RHR can signal illness onset and reduced training readiness. They found HRV to be a more sensitive early indicator. Monitoring these metrics can help determine when to resume training safely (Wegerif, 2019). If RHR is significantly elevated or HRV is notably lower than usual, it’s a signal to scale back or, in more severe cases, take the day off.

Shoes

Shoes may not be the most thrilling topic, but they are critical for runners. Finding the right shoe can lead to efficiency improvements which can translate to personal bests. At a certain point in training, a free 1-2% improvement just for lacing up is an enormous gain. There is no universal “best” race shoe out there. Responses to shoes are individual. Finding the right pair comes down to testing and determining your ideal balance of comfort and speed. If your budget allows, one effective method is to conduct A/B tests on a track. If you follow me on Strava, you will notice I often hit the track for this purpose. The approach is simple: during workout days, run consistent efforts and switch between shoes. By alternating pairs in a pattern like A-B-B-A-B-A (insert your favorite ABBA lyric here) over 6x5-minute or 10x3-minute intervals, you will quickly discern which shoes feel fastest. While not lab-controlled, this method offers real-world insights by comparing shoes under similar conditions (same weather, fatigue, and terrain). Over a few sessions, with the randomized order reducing bias, you will begin to notice differences. After a couple of sessions, I found that the slowest “super shoe” I tested is a few seconds slower per kilometer compared to the best. That translates to roughly 10 seconds in a 5k, 20 seconds in a 10k, or nearly 1 minute 30 seconds in a marathon. That is a significant gain for no extra effort. If you’re open to this approach, you can resell the slower shoes on eBay or similar marketplaces with minimal financial loss.

Beyond race shoes, selecting high-quality shoes for both easy runs and workouts is essential. As of 2025, there are remarkable options for non-plated training shoes. Advanced PEBA and A-TPU foams offer superior leg protection, providing a stark contrast to the cheaper, less durable, and less protective EVA (ethylene-vinyl acetate, lightweight but less resilient) foams. PEBA (polyether block amide) is a lightweight, more resilient foam with exceptional energy return (up to 80% compared to EVA’s 40–55%), absorbing impact and reducing muscle fatigue. A-TPU (Aliphatic Thermoplastic Polyurethane) balances cushioning and durability, offering stability and shock absorption to minimize joint stress whilst also providing superior energy return. PEBA is softer and bouncier but less durable, while A-TPU is sometimes firmer and longer-lasting, but both provide huge benefits over standard EVA. Studies, such as one by Hoogkamer et al. (2018), show PEBA’s higher energy return and lower density preserve more mechanical energy per footfall, reducing the impact on legs compared to EVA. Another study by Rodrigo-Carranza et al. (2023) found PEBAbased shoes improve running economy, suggesting less strain on muscles and joints over time. To illustrate, I conducted an informal experiment after finding an older pair of EVA-based shoes I thought long lost in the boot of my car. Having primarily used non-plated super foam shoes, I switched to the EVA shoes for a week. Despite their low mileage and good condition, the difference was striking. My legs felt noticeably more fatigued, and both workouts and easy runs felt harder—roughly a point higher on the RPE scale. Investing in quality training shoes promotes more sustainable, lower-risk training. While the choice is yours, I see no downsides to making the switch. Unfortunately, shoe companies all label their foams differently and come up with new foam names all the time. That means you will need to do a little investigation on your own when exploring shoes or ask a local shoe store employee.

Concerns about wearing plated shoes daily (though I don’t share them) are easily addressed with the wide range of non-plated training shoes featuring super foams available in 2025 and beyond. Lighter models, designed for faster running without plates, offer speed assistance comparable to top-tier carbon-plated shoes. The plates in many shoes primarily enhance stability rather than propulsion, with much of the performance boost derived from the energy return of the advanced foams. The 2018 study by Hoogkamer et al., found that the resilient foam in shoes like the Nike Vaporfly contributes significantly more to energy return than the carbon plate, which primarily acts as a lever to enhance stability and optimize ankle mechanics. Another study by Healey and Hoogkamer (2022) showed that even when the plate’s bending stiffness is reduced, the foam’s energy return remains the primary driver of running economy improvements, accounting for most of the efficiency gain in super shoes. As a result, you can confidently expect your workout paces to align closely with those from your recent races when using these shoes. However, if you train in shoes significantly less advanced than your race shoes, you may need to adjust your workout times by a small margin, perhaps 1–2%. In my experience, those I’ve encouraged to upgrade to high-quality, protective shoes have had no regrets. For comprehensive comparisons, the excellent website RunRepeat is an ideal resource to explore further.

Finally, after continued recommendations, I have explored the Chinese shoe market. I recognize that this section has the potential to not stand the test of time like the other sections of this book. However, I know that the price of shoes can be a barrier for some runners recovering and racing at their peak. For that reason, I thought it would be important to inform readers that if you're willing to learn the ropes on sites like AliExpress or Taobao, you can grab shoes just as good as (and in some cases, better than) Western brands. They are often priced 50% or even up to a whopping 70% off the equivalent priced Western shoe. Sizing can be a bit of a puzzle to crack, but if you match your size in good old freedom units (US sizing) you'll be spot-on 95% of the time. For my UK and EU friends, trying to cross-match brands in our nonconsistent units is a recipe for confusion. CM sizing can help, but sometimes it's the insole they're measuring and sometimes it's your actual foot length. In my experience a US 10 fits true-to-size across every brand I've tried, Western heavyweights and budget Chinese gems alike. There may be the odd exception for individual shoes, but it's been rare in the quite frankly ridiculous amount of running shoes I have tried.

Of course, some of you might be hesitant about buying from the Chinese market. However, if you've made it this far—rethinking your training and cracking open this book—you're probably open-minded enough to save a bundle on what can be an expensive hobby. Maybe you need to keep the wife/partner/significant other (insert whoever holds the purse strings) off your case. Or perhaps you just want to stash a few extra quid for more important pursuits... like the pub on Saturday night, your favorite takeaway, or those ridiculously overpriced race entry fees that leave you skint before the starting line.

Some of these shoes are so good that even in a blind test you wouldn't know if they were from Li-Ning, 361 Degrees, Do-Win, or Dynafish versus a "premium" brand like Nike, Adidas, or Puma (the vast majority of which are made in China anyway). Currently, at the time of writing, many Chinese brands are using CPU (Casting Polyurethane) outsoles—a more durable rubber compound that outlasts traditional ones by a mile (or many more). So, you're not only getting a shoe that's just as protective with super foam cushioning, but way more durable too. Trust me, your legs won't care—they're just happy to be protected.

## Cross Training

With limited hobby hours, running is the most effective way to improve as a runner. Research supports this, showing that runners who prioritize running over cross-training activities like cycling or swimming see greater improvements in running economy and race performance (Paquette et al., 2017). However, there are times when running isn’t possible due to injury, for instance. Alternatively, you might just enjoy cycling and want to mix it in. For others, running seven days a week may be too taxing. Low-impact activities like cycling or the elliptical can reduce stress on joints while building aerobic fitness. So, how much cross-training is appropriate, and how do you balance it to stay focused on running?

In my experience coaching runners within this training framework, I have seen people maintain fitness during injury by adapting their running schedule to cycling or the elliptical. A 2016 study found that injured runners who cycled at a similar aerobic intensity to their running workouts maintained cardiovascular fitness and returned to running with minimal performance loss (Liu & Wang, 2016) I’ve also seen runners blend running and cycling simply because they enjoy both. The key is ensuring running remains the primary driver if your goal is to improve as a runner (and since you’re reading a running book, I assume it is). A good starting point is a 60/40 split, with at least 60% of your training time dedicated to running and up to 40% to cycling or other aerobic activities. This aligns with research suggesting that cross-training should not exceed 30–40% of total training volume (by total time) for runners aiming to improve race performance. A 2000 study found that runners who replaced more than 40% of their running with cycling experienced declines in running economy due to reduced sport-specific stimulus (Dolezal et al., 2000). Anecdotally, I’ve seen this play out: a close friend shifted from mostly running to a 60/40 balance of running and cycling. Up to this point, their running performance held steady. However, once cycling dominated their weekly focus, their running performance declined. While 60/40 is a rough benchmark, it’s a practical guide for maintaining running-specific fitness while incorporating cross-training, especially considering our limited hours.

From a classic running schedule—three easy runs, three subthreshold sessions, and one long run—I recommend keeping at least two subthreshold runs to preserve running-specific adaptations. These higher-intensity sessions are important for improving running economy (Paquette et al., 2017). If you choose to cross-train, cycling can extend one session for additional aerobic stimulus with lower injury risk.

For runners who can handle some running but find seven days a week too demanding, cycling can complement your schedule by reducing joint stress while maintaining aerobic fitness (Millet et al., 2020). For example, you might replace one easy run with a longer cycling session to build low-end aerobic endurance without the physical impact of running. Replacing the long run with a bike ride offers a chance to cross-train. A longer bike ride can develop aerobic endurance in a low-impact way, with research showing that long, low-intensity cycling sessions improve aerobic capacity in runners (Foster et al., 2015). This can be especially useful for runners managing minor injuries or needing a break from high-impact training.

As a rough guide, cycling sessions should be extended by about 20–30% compared to running time to achieve similar aerobic benefits. This is due to differences in energy expenditure and muscle recruitment (Millet et al., 2020). For instance, if an easy run takes 60 minutes, an equivalent cycling session might last 72–78 minutes. This is a conservative estimate, but it’s a practical starting point for balancing the two. It should also be pointed out that 1 hour on the turbo or trainer is not equal to one hour outside. Keep in mind that on a trainer, you are pedaling the entire time and outside there are periods where you are not.

Real-life examples show how effective this balance can be. British runners Stephanie Davis and Beth Potter, both low-mileage runners, have achieved remarkable results by incorporating cycling and other cross-training. Davis, a full-time finance professional, ran a 2:27:14 marathon to win the British Olympic Marathon Trials in 2021, averaging 54 miles per week of running supplemented by cycling and elliptical training. Potter, a triathlete, ran a 14:41 5k in 2021, with only 30 miles per week of running but extensive cycling and swimming. These athletes demonstrate that strategic cross-training can support elite performance while reducing injury risk.

If your goal is to become a better runner, cross-training should complement, not replace, running. For injured runners, cycling or the elliptical can temporarily take a larger role, but maintaining at least 1–2 running sessions per week (if possible) helps preserve running mechanics (Pizzuto et al., 2013). Aim for that 60/40 split and experiment to find what works for you. By prioritizing running while strategically using cross-training, you can build fitness, reduce injury risk, and enjoy the benefits of both sports. Injuries can be a major setback for runners, sometimes forcing you to stop running entirely. I’ve experienced this myself, and it’s easy to feel discouraged and down during such times. However, I encourage you to view this as an opportunity to focus on serious training through cross-training and remain positive. The mechanical stress of running is a primary driver of fatigue and injury risk, so taking a break from it can allow your body to recover while still building fitness.

If cross-training doesn’t aggravate your injury, you can often do more than you would with running. For example, when I was forced to stop running, I went from averaging eight-hour running weeks to eleven-hour weeks, splitting my time evenly between the elliptical and indoor cycling. The main challenge here is mental rather than physical—staying motivated to complete these sessions requires discipline. I was quickly reminded that I could handle much more subthreshold work, without the lingering fatigue that running brings. You might also incorporate harder sessions you’ve missed due to running’s limitations, provided they don’t worsen your injury.

For instance, I incorporated sessions like 10x1 minute +1 minute float recovery on the bike (120% and 85% FTP).

In my case, I increased to about 200 minutes of subthreshold work compared to 100 minutes when running, resulting in around 35% of total weekly volume. The rest consisted of easy sessions, and this balance—with no running—produced manageable fatigue levels similar to my previous running schedule. This highlights how cautious we must be with running due to its physical demands. If you find yourself injured, try to reframe the situation as a chance to improve your fitness while carefully managing your recovery. Avoid rushing back into running before you’re ready, as this could lead to further setbacks. When you do return, ease into it gradually—perhaps running just a few times per week for the first couple of weeks before slowly rebuilding your full schedule. Some runners discover that cross-training is so effective that it becomes a permanent part of their routine. In fact, many elite runners incorporate cross-training long-term after experiencing its benefits during injury recovery.

## Conclusion

I hope you got something out of this book. I tried to provide a comprehensive overview of a framework. The goal of that framework is to minimize the pressure from your training while delivering new personal bests. While not the only approach, I have found that this method maximizes load for a given weekly time. The stories here are intended to prompt reflection on whether your current training aligns with some of the suboptimal patterns discussed. The aim is to equip you with the knowledge and tools to craft a training regimen that is sustainable and focused on long-term progress. I hope we as a running community can begin moving away from the repetitive boom and bust cycles that lead to stagnation and attrition.

Is it essential to follow every recommendation here? Certainly not. You might choose to adjust to a six-day schedule, experimenting with different structures, or opt for five days with two dedicated to cross-training. Perhaps a recurring injury necessitates targeted gym or strength work. If your focus is solely on 5ks, by all means, incorporate specific workouts and VO2 max sessions if you think they will be more helpful. However, each time you step outside this framework I encourage you to pause and consider why you’re doing so, what benefits it brings and what risks it carries. My own journey illustrates that the method is surprisingly versatile for a range of distances and that some habits are harder to break than others. Even now I occasionally question myself. I find myself wondering, “Can this really keep working?” I might be tempted to try X or Y, but I remind myself of the evidence from my own training and stick with what delivers.

Will this approach guarantee improvement? Of course not. Anyone who presents a training plan as a surefire path to success is, at best, offering a false sense of certainty. At worst, they are peddling ‘Fools Gold’ like Ian Brown spinning a Stone Roses riff. No single training method is the best, and no reputable coach or physiologist would claim otherwise. Subthreshold training may not even be the optimal path for everyone. Yet, for those with limited time, this framework provides a way to structure your running to maximize training and consistency. These two things will translate to long-term progress. While it is simple, it is not easy. It demands hard work, dedication and, for some, a willingness to challenge your core running beliefs. There are no shortcuts or miracles here—or anywhere, for that matter.

Above all, I hope this book inspires you to rethink what defines a strong runner. I hope you refine your race strategy and consider perspectives you hadn’t previously explored. Perhaps you will realize you’ve been pushing too hard on easy days. Maybe you’ll recognize how much fatigue your previous workouts were accumulating. Or it could be the shift away from viewing training in rigid 12-16 week blocks. If you are already on a solid path, this book may simply help refine a few areas. For others, it might serve as final reassurance that you’re on the right track. There’s something here for every runner.

The hope is that you embrace tomorrow as the day to maximize your running potential. At the very least, shift your focus towards the practices that will make you a stronger runner. The responsibility is now yours to roll up your sleeves, put in the effort and see how far this can take you. Whether your goal is to break 20 minutes in a 5k or to tackle an ambitious 2:20 marathon, you have to put in the training. I’ll be out there doing the same.

## Glossary

A-TPU (Aliphatic Thermoplastic Polyurethane): A durable, high cushion, high energy returning foam often used in newer running shoes.

Blood Lactate: The amount of lactate that is shuttled from the muscle cell and not immediately taken back up, accumulating in the blood.

Carbohydrate: A macronutrient that the body can rapidly break down for energy production.

Cross Training (XT): Any form of training done that is not running (e.g. cycling, swimming, etc.).

EVA (ethylene-vinyl acetate): A somewhat durable but slightly heavy and outdated foam used in running shoes.

Fatigue: The accumulation of load over a short (acute) period of time.

Fitness: The accumulation of load over an extended (chronic) period of time.

Lactate: An organic compound that serves as a fuel for cellular energy metabolism. It acts as a key substrate for cellular signaling and a regulator of NADH/NAD+ balance during glycolysis.

Glycogen: A stored form of glucose in the body.

Grade Adjusted Pace (GAP): The pace of a run adjusted for the amount of elevation gained and lost over a certain distance. Meant to normalize paces across changing elevations.

Heart Rate (HR): The amount of time the heart beats in a single minute.

Heart Rate Max (HRmax): The highest possible achievable heart rate in an individual.

Heart Rate Variability (HRV): The amount of time (in ms) measured between each individual heartbeat.

Intensity Factor: An adjusting coefficient that accounts for the effort relative to your physiology, typically dividing the pace, HR, or power output over an activity by your typical estimated pace, HR, or power output at lactate threshold.

Kilometer (km): A unit of measurement equal to 1000 meters or 0.63 miles.

Lactate Curve: The graphed relationship between blood lactate and intensity or speed.

Lactate Threshold 1 (LT1): The first point along the lactate curve where lactate becomes elevated above its minimum value. AKA: First lactate turning point.

Lactate Threshold 2 (LT2): A mathematical estimation for the point at which the body exceeds a metabolic steady state, resulting in net blood lactate accumulation over time.

Lactate Threshold Heart Rate (LTHR): The heart rate at which lactate threshold typically occurs in a controlled environment.

Maximal Lactate Steady State (MLSS): The maximum intensity at which lactate accumulation is balanced with lactate clearance. Typical protocols include a 30-minute session with lactate readings at minutes 10 and 30. MLSS is defined as the maximum speed at which an athlete can maintain a lactate rise less than 1.0 mmol between those two time points.

Mechanics: The movement patterns of different parts of the body while running, which affect efficiency of movement and the way the body experiences impact and imparts force into the ground.

Metabolism: The chemical process by which energy is derived from substrates in the body.

Mile (mi): A unit of measurement approximately equal to 1609 meters or 1.6 kilometers.

Onset of Blood Lactate Accumulation (OBLA): The point at which the body begins to produce more lactate than it can clear via aerobic processes, resulting in net blood lactate accumulation over time.

PEBA (polyether block amide): A durable and lightweight material used for more responsive running shoes with high energy return.

Performance Management Chart (PMC): A chart that graphically represents fatigue, fitness and form. Often used to monitor cycling training load.

Plyometrics: A form of exercise…

Power: The amount of work done per unit time, in this case by an athlete. Measured in watts.

Ramp Rate: The rate at which training load is increased over a given period of time.

Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE): The difficulty perceived by an individual running a given effort.

Resting HR (RHR): The typical number of times the heart beats per minute when an individual is sitting or lying down. Ideally measured at a consistent time of day, whether it be while sleeping by a watch or immediately upon waking in the morning.

Respiration Rate (RR): (For our purposes) the number of breaths taken each minute.

Running Economy: The amount of oxygen an individual requires to move their weight a given distance.

Strides: Short, controlled repetitions (typically of 10–30 seconds) where the athlete opens up their stride in an effort to practice moving a bit faster at a relaxed effort.

Sweet Spot (SS): A training intensity between the first and second lactate thresholds meant to be representative of a zone where you can accumulate a lot of quality volume with a low muscular stress.

Time Trial Cycling: A discipline where cyclists are set at time intervals to race independently across a course, competing for the fastest time. Most recognizable for their aerodynamic positioning and gear.

Training Load Score (TLS): A mathematical representation of the amount of load a particular session places on the body.

VDOT: A system pioneered by late running coach and scientist Jack Daniels, representing an athlete’s performance level. It comes from V̇, the symbol commonly used for the time derivative of a value—in this case the volume of oxygen consumed per unit of time.

VO2max: The maximum volume of oxygen that can be consumed by the body. Typically evaluated via a ramp test where an individual gradually progresses the pace until oxygen consumption plateaus or peaks (VO2peak).

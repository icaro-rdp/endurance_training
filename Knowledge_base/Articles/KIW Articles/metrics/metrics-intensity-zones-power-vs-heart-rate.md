# Time Spent in Intensity Zones Differs If Using Power vs Heart Rate

**Source:** knowledgeiswatt.substack.com/p/91-time-spent-in-intensity-zones **Date:** August 6, 2025

Evaluating the time spent in zones (intensity distribution) is a very common practice to evaluate
training sessions to manage training stimulus and to evaluate the physiological demands of races.
Each zone target specific physiological stress, fuel utilisation and neuromuscular load, so it can
be useful to understand the stimulus given on the body. Training analysis software, like Training
Peaks, have specific charts representing time spent in intensity zones.

Screenshot from Training Peaks. Each number represents an intensity zone.

Screenshot from Training Peaks. Each number represents an intensity zone.

The two most used intensity distribution models by successful world-class endurance athletes are
pyramidal and polarized:

In the pyramidal model, most of the time is spent at low intensity, and a decreasing proportion of
time at medium and high intensity.

In the polarized model most of the time is spent at low intensity, but the amount of time spent at
high intensity is more than the amount of time spent at medium intensity.

Polarized training seems to be the best solution to boost performance on short term (4-10 weeks).
(1) On the other hand, when looking at the reported annual intensity distribution of some successful
world-class road cyclists (2,3), they adopt a pyramidal intensity distribution with a switch from a
less to a more polarized (even if still pyramidal) model when approaching the competition period.
So, at the beginning of the periodization, they focus more on zone 3 (in a 5/7 zones model), while
in the second part they reduce time spent in zone 3 (medium intensity) and increase the amount of
work at high intensity (zone 4, 5, 6). The fact that polarized training is not adopted throughout
all the season can be due to the fact that:

performing high intensity training consistently for all the season can be too stressful (both
physically and mentally) some data (4) also showed that doing the pyramidal-to-polarized progression
seems also to be more effective from a pure physiological and performance point of view, when
compared to maintaining a similar intensity distribution for all the year or doing the opposite
switch (starting from a more polarized and progressing through a more pyramidal then).

The above cited intensity distribution model of world-class road cyclist was analysed using power
output to describe the time spent in intensity zones. However, also heart rate can be used.

Can power and heart rate be usable interchangeably to evaluate the time spent in intensity zones, or
the intensity distribution differs if described with power vs heart rate?

A study published in 2017 by a group led by Dajo Sanders (Newman University, Birmingham, UK - and
World Tour Cycling Coach) on International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance tried to
answer this question. (5)

WHAT DID THEY DO?

Fifteen male well-trained but not professional cyclists (VO2max: 55-69) competing at international
and national level were recruited.

First, they performed a step test in the lab to establish training zones using a 3-zones model:

Zone 1: below power/heart rate at first lactate threshold

Zone 2: power/heart rate between first and second lactate threshold

Zone 3: power/heart rate above the second lactate threshold.

Then, they performed a 10 weeks training camp with each cyclists performing a personalized training
plan prescribed by their own coach without any standardization between different athletes.

The goal was to see the agreement in time spent in intensity zones when using power vs heart rate.

WHAT DID THEY FIND?

The percentage time spent in zones resulted:

Polarized when using Power Zones:

Zone 1: 80 ± 7%

Zone 2: 9 ± 6%

Zone 3: 11 ± 3%

Pyramidal when using Heart Rate Zones:

Zone 1: 87 ± 6%

Zone 2: 9 ± 6%

Zone 3: 4 ± 2%

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYS:

Data from Sanders et al. (5)

Intensity distribution/time spent in zones differs when expressed in power vs. heart rate.

Specifically, heart rate intensity distribution underestimate time spent at high intensity when
compared to using power output.

GABRIELE’S COMMENT

These data are very simple but still useful: I think they suggest us to not use power and heart rate
intensity distribution interchangeably.

What is the reason behind the underestimation of time spent at high intensity when using heart rate?

The lower time spent at high intensity in heart rate vs. power zone is very likely due to the lag of
heart rate response compared to power output when doing intervals. For example, when performing a
two minutes’ high intensity effort, it is only in the last minute that heart rate reaches the high
intensity zone. Furthermore, when performing very short high intensity efforts (read sprints below
15s duration), heart rate doesn’t even reach zone 2 in a 3 zones model. So, the mismatch between
power and heart rate intensity zones is very likely to be larger when performing high intensity
stochastic training sessions or races, especially criteriums, cyclocross and cross-country mountain
bike events. Other scenarios which could increase the mismatch between hear rate and power time
spent in zones are:

altitude training: you can be at high/medium intensity when using heart rate but at medium/low
intensity if looking at power.

heat training/hot environment: same as altitude.

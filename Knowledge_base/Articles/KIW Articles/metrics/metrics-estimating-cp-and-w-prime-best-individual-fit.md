# How to Accurately Estimate CP and W': the 'best Individual Fit' Method

From the well-established hyperbolic relationship between power output and the duration for which
that power can be sustained, two fundamental parameters can be derived: Critical

Power (CP) and Anaerobic Work Capacity (W′).

Critical Power (CP) corresponds to the horizontal asymptote of the power–

duration curve. Physiologically, it represents the Maximum Metabolic Steady State (MMSS) at which
aerobic metabolism can meet the energetic demands without a continual accumulation of fatiguerelated
metabolites. In practical terms, CP defines the boundary between exercise intensities that can be
sustained for a prolonged period with stable physiological responses and those that inevitably lead
rapidly to progressive fatigue and exhaustion. (1)

CP—not the second lactate threshold (LT2) or FTP—is considered the gold-

standard estimate of the maximal metabolic steady state (MMSS), and therefore the best
representation of the second exercise threshold.

The Anaerobic Reserve (W′) represents the curvature constant of the power–duration relationship and
can be interpreted geometrically as the area under the curve above

Critical Power at any given point. Physiologically, it corresponds to a finite amount of work
(expressed in joules) that can be performed above CP.

Importantly, the magnitude of W′ is theoretically fixed and independent of the specific power output
selected above CP. While exercising at a higher intensity accelerates the rate at which W′ is
depleted, the total amount of work that can be completed above CP remains the same. W′ is considered
a parameter reflecting anaerobic qualities and the capacity to sustain high-intensity efforts above
CP. (1)

The power–duration relationship—and, so, the calculation of CP and W′—is derived from a series of
maximal efforts of varying durations. To obtain accurate and reliable estimates of CP and W′, it is
essential to follow specific guidelines when performing these maximal efforts. Key factors to
consider are:

Number of efforts

Duration and Intensity of each effort

Recovery between efforts

Terrain

Cycling Position

Cadence

Pacing Strategy

Typically, three maximal efforts are considered sufficient to construct the hyperbolic
power–duration relationship and calculate CP and W′ with good accuracy. (1) Please note that with
only two efforts it is not possible to assess the accuracy of the CP/W′ estimates as metrics such as
the standard error of the estimate (SEE) and the best individual fit method (described in the next
sections) cannot be applied. Therefore, we recommend performing three trials.

All efforts should be performed in the severe intensity domain (above CP). It is recommended to set
the shortest trial at 2–3 minutes and the longest trial at 12–15 minutes. This range ensures that
the power–duration curve is well covered, allowing a good fit while avoiding skewness. The shortest
effort should not be shorter than 2–3 minutes; if it is too brief, the intensity may be so high that
fatigue occurs before reaching VO₂max (maximal oxygen uptake) and it would represent a type of
effort not covered by the CP-Wʼ power duration-relationship (extreme intensity domain). Conversely,
the longest effort should not exceed 12–15 minutes, as longer durations increase the influence of
glycogen depletion, motivation, and pacing strategies on the results. (1, 4) Given these
considerations, a good scientific practical approach can be:

1st effort: 3 minutes 2nd effort: 8 minutes 3rd effort: 12-13 minutes “Inter-trial recovery between
prediction trials should be set to a minimum of 30 min during a single visit or 24 h during multiple
days. The benefit of multiple days if that any fatigue induced by the initial prediction trial does
not affect the subsequent one, but possible error due to day-to-day variation in power output is
introduced”.

- Leo et al. 2022 - (5)

Another factor to be considered is whether the all-out efforts should be performed on flat or uphill
terrain. A study reported that mean maximal power outputs across different durations are on average
about 3% higher uphill (6–7% gradient) compared with flat terrain. (5) So, the advice is to perform
all trials on similar terrain. Which one? The type where you train and where you want to be most
competitive: for example, uphill for climbers and flat for classics specialists. When applying the
resulting training zones to a different terrain, just adjust them by approximately 3-5% above or
below to account for the difference relative to the test conditions.

Cycling position (standing, seated, or time trial (TT) position) can significantly affect mean
maximal power output during all-out efforts. So, CP and W′ values obtained in different positions
should not be used interchangeably. For example, likely due to the more compressed position on the
bike, CP measured in a time trial position is usually lower than on a standard road bike, (6) with
the magnitude of the difference depending likely on the aggressiveness of the TT position and the
riderʼs experience on a TT bike. When performing a CP test on a road bike, it is recommended to use
your preferred position (seated, standing, or alternating), as this will most closely reflect how
you ride in training and races, making the results more valid and practically useful.

Cadence should be kept consistent across all trials, as CP calculations are influenced by pedalling
rate. (7) A useful guideline is to aim for the same cadence within ±5 rpm for all efforts. In
practice, this consistency in cadence across different trial is often achieved naturally without
giving any specific indications to the riders.

You should aim for an optimal even pacing rather than starting all-out and fading. A good practical
approach is to begin with a fast but controlled start of about 30 seconds—

around +10% faster but not all-out—to help accelerate the rise of oxygen consumption. Then maintain
an even pacing (at your best) throughout the effort, trying to increase power in the final 30
seconds (if possible). Of course, to attain an optimal even pacing, it is crucial to choose a route
free from interruptions—no traffic lights, traffic jams, strong wind, and a constant gradient.

Once you have applied all three models within the app, you will likely obtain three different

CP values and three different W′ values, each corresponding to one of the three power–

duration mathematical models described above. Why different values? While in a virtual perfect
dataset all the 3 models would theoretically yield identical CP and W′ estimates, real-

world human performance is inherently variable, meaning the mathematical fit is never 100% correct.
As a result, each model can produce different parameter estimates (CP and Wʼ) due to unavoidable
measurement error and biological variability.

So, which CP and W′ should you choose?

You can apply the “best individual fit” method: select the model that produces the lowest combined
error for both CP and W′ (8, 9). In practice, this means choosing the model that best fits the
specific dataset obtained from that particular test. Importantly, the most appropriate model may
differ between athletes — and even between different tests performed by the same athlete. Each time,
you should select the model that provides the most accurate and precise estimates for the data
collected in that specific assessment. The key statistical parameter used to evaluate model fit is
the Standard Error of the Estimate (SEE, or Std Err). SEE quantifies the magnitude of the error
between the predicted and observed values, thereby reflecting the accuracy and precision of the CP
and W′ estimates. A high SEE indicates a poor fit and lower accuracy, whereas a low SEE indicates a
better fit and greater precision.

When should you consider repeating one or more efforts because no model fits your data well enough?

You should consider performing another trial to improve the accuracy and precision of

CP if, in all three models, the Standard Error of the Estimate (SEE or Std Err) for CP exceeds 5%
(1).

Similarly, you should consider performing another trial to improve the accuracy and precision of W′
if, in all three models, the SEE for W′ exceeds 10% (1).

Keep in mind that these thresholds are not absolute. For example, if the CP SEE is 6%, it may still
be acceptable, but if it reaches 7.5–10%, you can consider performing another effort. A higher SEE
indicates that, in one or more trials, pacing may have been suboptimal or the athlete just
underperformed. In such cases, it is suggested to repeat the exact trial(s) that were not performed
optimally.

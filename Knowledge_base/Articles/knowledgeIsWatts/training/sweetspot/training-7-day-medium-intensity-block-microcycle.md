# A 7-day Medium Intensity Block Microcycle Boosts Aerobic Adaptations and Performance!

**Source:** knowledgeiswatt.substack.com/p/119-a-7-day-medium-intensity-block **Date:** December 2,

An important note before reading this KIW Research Note:

Low intensity training: below the first threshold;

Medium intensity training: between the two thresholds;

High intensity training: above the second threshold;

When looking at the annual training plan, classic linear periodization—a progressive increase in
volume first, followed by intensity (while maintaining or slightly reducing volume)— is the most
commonly used and evidence-based strategy among successful endurance athletes. However, for
timecrunched cyclists, this approach is not always feasible. Many can train only about 1 hour from
Monday to Friday, with the possibility of doing longer rides only on some weekends. As a result,
they lack the time needed to progressively increase volume and properly build a classic linear
periodization. Consequently, many of these cyclists introduce a substantial amount of mediumand
high-intensity training early in the season. This leads them to quickly reach a plateau in both
volume and intensity—which if then maintained for several months without any progressive overload,
increases the risk of stagnation.

In previous KIW Research Notes, we saw that for these athletes an effective alternative could be
high-intensity (HIT) block periodization/microcycles—that is, concentrating HIT sessions over a few
consecutive days (5-7) followed by recovery days or weeks, rather than distributing 2–3 HIT workouts
evenly every week throughout the training plan. Specifically, we saw that:

1. When training volume is kept constant at around 8/10 hrs per week. 12 weeks of HIT block periodization lead to superior performance and physiological adaptations compared to an equally distributed HIT approach. (Learn more here)
2. A 6-day HIT shock microcycle followed by 6 days of active recovery is effective in improving cycling performance and so may serve as an optimal tapering strategy.

But does the same hold true for medium intensity?

Can a medium intensity block-microcycle increase improve performance more than regular training?

A study published by Mølmen and Colleagues (University of Inland Norway, Lillehammer) in August 2025
on Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise tried to answer this question. (1)

WHAT DID THEY DO?

30 male professional or highly trained cyclists (age ~20, VO2max ~71) were recruited.

All participants completed both a 15-day regular training block (REG) and a medium-intensity
training block (MIT), in a randomized order. This means that participants were randomly assigned to
start with either the regular training block or the medium-intensity block, with roughly half
beginning with one condition and half with the other. Between the test at the end of each block and
the start of the next one, participants rested or performed light training for two to three days.

MIT BLOCK

It consisted of six interval sessions at medium intensity (MIT) distributed over 7 days followed by
a 6-day active recovery period:

What was the MIT intensity? MIT intervals were prescribed using an RPE (Rating of Perceived
Exertion) of 14–15 (“somewhat hard” to “hard”) on the Borg 6–20 scale. This corresponded to about
85% of maximum heart rate and a blood lactate concentration of roughly 2.8 mmol/L. Therefore, it is
very likely that the actual exercise intensity fell between the first and second physiological
threshold.

“All MIT sessions were initiated by a standardized 15-min progressive warm-up protocol succeeded by
5 min of rest before starting the first work interval. Each session was finalized with 5 min of
active recovery” (1), so they all lasted around 1h30min.

REGULAR TRAINING BLOCK:

During the Regular Training Block, participants continued following their usual training plan, which
included:

2x week MIT sessions, or 1x MIT session + 1xHIT session per week.

MIT sessions involved interval workouts consisting of 4–8 × 6–20 minutes performed at a Borg RPE of
14–16.

HIT sessions generally consisted of 4–6 × 4–8 minutes, performed at a Borg RPE of 16–19.

Long endurance rides lasting 3–4 hours.

A higher overall training volume compared to the MIT block.

Additionally, “those who routinely engaged in resistance training were permitted to continue their
sessions as usual” (1).

OTHER POINTS ABOUT THE STUDY DESIGN:

The final two days of the protocol before the physiological and laboratory tests were kept easy and
identical for both the MIT and the Regular Training block, in order to minimize any fatigue-related
influence on the study results.

WHAT DID THEY FIND?

The MIT block produced greater aerobic physiological and performance improvements compared to the
Regular Training (REG) block:

VO2max:

MIT Block: from 70.1 to 71.4 ml/min/kg: +1.9%

REG: from 70.8 to 70.8 ml/min/kg: 0%

Power at 4 mmol/L of blood lactate concentration:

MIT BLOCK: from 4.05 to 4.21 W/Kg: +4%

REG: from 4.16 to 4.11 W/Kg: -1.2%

Cycling Efficiency

MIT BLOCK: from 19.4 to 19.6%: +1%

RE: from 19.4 to 19.4: 0%

Mean Power Output 15 min Time Trial

MIT BLOCK: from 4.22 to 4.36 W/Kg: +3.3%

RE: from 4.30 to 4.31 W/Kg: +0.2%

Data from Mølmen et al. 2025 (1).

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYS:

A Medium Intensity one week block microcycle followed by 6 days active recovery induces greater
aerobic physiological and performance adaptations when compared to a time matched period of regular
training.

GABRIELE’S COMMENT

I found these data are particularly interesting because they suggest that, in addition to
highintensity block microcycles, the same block periodization principle applied at medium intensity
can also enhance aerobic adaptations and performance. In other words, condensing a training stimulus
into a few days—a “block/shock” microcycle—can be effective when using both HIT and MIT.

Here below some considerations to keep in mind when putting into practice MIT block periodization:

This approach can be particularly useful for time-crunched cyclists, who often cannot follow a
classic linear periodization without maintaining similar volume and intensity for several weeks,
which can lead to performance stagnation.

However MIT block periodization could also be applied to professional or non-timecrunched cyclists.
After building a base with progressive increases in volume during winter, MIT or HIT block
periodization can be used. In this scenario, total training volume during the MIT/HIT block should
be reduced compared to usual, but it is probably best to keep it higher than what was used in this
study.

Since a MIT block microcycle is likely to put a high level of strain on the body, it is important to
start the block fresh and well-rested, so that the body can fully absorb the load.

A strategy which can be useful to manage the high volume of medium-intensity (or highintensity)
training during block periodization is to include double sessions instead of a single daily session.
As shown in a previous KIW research note, this can help to maintain intensity while reducing
perceived fatigue and muscle soreness. However, if developing durability is also a goal, double
sessions should be limited to once per week, in order to also practice sustaining effort under
fatigue.

terms of aerobic adaptations and performance!

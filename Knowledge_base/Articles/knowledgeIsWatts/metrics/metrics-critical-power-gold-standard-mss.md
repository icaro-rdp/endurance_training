# Critical Power: the Gold Standard for the Maximum Metabolic Steady State

Properly identifying exercise intensity is essential in endurance sports:

in training, accurate intensity prescription allows for effective management of physiological stress
and training load—both within individual sessions and across the microcycle—helping to have the
intended stimulus leading to positive adaptations.

In racing, a precise understanding of sustainable effort is essential for optimal pacing strategies,
particularly in time trials.

This is where exercise thresholds come into play. Exercise thresholds represent key physiological
turning points at which relatively small changes in workload lead to substantial shifts in metabolic
response, fatigue development, and overall internal load.

Exercising above or below these thresholds influences what happens inside the body and, ultimately,
stress and load.

Sport science recognize three different metabolic response and so three big physiological intensity
zones (1,2):

SCIENTIFIC ZONE 1 - THE LOW/MODERATE INTENSITY DOMAIN

Intensity: Below the first lactate/ventilatory threshold.

Metabolic response: oxygen consumption (VO2) and blood lactate reach steady-

state values rapidly and there is no relevant accumulation of metabolites or ions at muscular level.

Determinants of Fatigue: when exercising (for hours) at this intensity, fatigue is due to depletion
of muscle glycogen, central factors and muscle damage.

SCIENTIFIC ZONE 2 - THE MEDIUM/HEAVY INTENSITY DOMAIN

Intensity: between the first lactate/ventilatory threshold and the maximum metabolic steady state
(MMSS, the maximum exercise intensity at which a steady state in oxygen consumption and muscle
metabolites is still achievable).

Metabolic response: steady-state values for 𝑉O2 and blood lactate are delayed, but still reached.

Determinants of fatigue: At this intensity, fatigue is a mix of metabolic/ionic muscular
perturbation and muscle glycogen depletion.

SCIENTIFIC ZONE 3 - THE HIGH/SEVERE INTENSITY DOMAIN

Intensity: Above the Maximum Metabolic Steady State.

Metabolic response: at this exercise intensity, even when performing a constant power bout, a steady
state physiological canʼt be reached with oxygen consumption and blood lactate continuing to
increase until VO2max and the limit of tolerance are then shortly reached, and power output canʼt be
maintained anymore.

This occurs upon reaching a critical intramuscular metabolic and ionic environment:

reduced phosphocreatine availability, decreased potassium concentration, and increased hydrogen ions
accumulation.

Determinants of fatigue: the critical intramuscular metabolic and ionic environment described above
(linked to phosphocreatine, potassium and hydrogen ions).

background, but the suggestion is to use whatever model you deem appropriate as long as you know in
which scientific exercise intensity domain each of your training zone is.

Before diving deeper into training zones and exercise thresholds, itʼs important to clarify a key
point. Using physiological thresholds is essential for properly managing load within a session and
across a microcycle. They help us dose intensity, distribute stress, and structure training in a
logical way. However, there is no strong evidence — and in some cases scientific data suggest the
opposite — that small deviations around a given physiological threshold lead to meaningfully
different training adaptations. In other words, thresholds are extremely useful for organizing and
controlling training load, but they should not be interpreted as sharp biological borders that
dramatically change the nature of adaptation. For example, training slightly below or slightly above
maximal metabolic steady state (MMSS) will likely stimulate very similar aerobic adaptations. The
same principle applies to work performed around the first threshold: small differences in intensity
do not result in completely different physiological outcomes.

Use thresholds to manage stress and plan your microcycles and interval sessions effectively

— not to fragment training adaptations into different categories.

Based on what we have discussed so far, it should now be clear that identifying training intensity
zones essentially comes down to defining two key physiological benchmarks:

The First Lactate/Ventilatory Threshold: the boundary between the moderateand heavy-intensity
domains.

The Maximum Metabolic Steady State (MMSS): which marks the transition between the heavyand
severeintensity domains. MMSS can be defined as “the bioenergetic threshold above which fatigue
development is accelerated and both systemic and muscular homeostasis can no longer be maintained.”
(2)

From a practical standpoint, these two boundaries are sufficient to structure intensity in a
physiologically meaningful way.

There are two possible options to estimate the Maximum Metabolic Steady State:

1. LACTATE MEASUREMENTS
2. CRITICAL POWER

Blood lactate is the most widely used physiological marker for estimating the maximal metabolic
steady state. Why lactate? Because when the oxidative (aerobic) pathways are unable to meet the
energy demands of the working muscle, pyruvate is increasingly converted into lactate rather than
entering aerobic metabolism. This shift is accompanied by metabolic disturbances, including an
accumulation of hydrogen ions (H⁺), which contribute to muscle fatigue and a decline in performance.

There are two options to use lactate measurements to estimate the MMSS:

THE MAXIMUM LACTATE STEADY STATE (MLSS, note the ‘Lʼ instead of the ‘Mʼ)

THE SECOND LACTATE THRESHOLD identified from an incremental step test

Definition: MLSS is defined as the highest power output at which the increase in blood lactate
concentration is less than 1 mmol·L⁻¹ between the 10th and 30th minute of constant-load exercise.
(3) It represents the maximal intensity at which a dynamic equilibrium still exists between lactate
appearance and lactate clearance in the bloodstream. (2)

Testing Protocol: a series of 30-minute constant-power trials (typically 3–5), each performed on
separate days.

During each trial, blood lactate is measured at rest and every 5 minutes throughout the exercise
bout. MLSS is identified as the highest power output at which blood lactate increases by less than 1
mmol·L⁻¹ between minute 10 and minute 30. (4)

Image from Jones et al. (2)

Is MLSS a good estimate of MMSS?

Yes — provided that strict methodological criteria are followed, MLSS can offer a valid estimate of
MMSS.

However, from a practical perspective, we do not recommend using MLSS in applied settings.

The reason is simple: it is highly impractical. A proper MLSS determination typically requires 4–6
separate 30-minute constant-power trials (with a minimum of three), each performed on different
days. This means several days of testing, repeated prolonged efforts at near-threshold intensity,
and a large number of lactate measurements.

For this reason, MLSS remains largely a research tool. It is not by chance that athletes and coaches
rarely — if ever — use MLSS directly to determine MMSS or to define training zones.

The other common lactate-based approach to estimate the Maximum Metabolic Steady State is through
determination of the second lactate threshold (LT2) during an incremental step test. In this
scenario, blood lactate concentration is measured at the end of each step of a graded exercise test,
and power output is plotted against lactate values. Once the lactate–

power curve is constructed, mathematical modelling is applied to identify the point at which blood
lactate begins to rise disproportionately — the breakpoint that is assumed to approximate MLSS, and
therefore MMSS.

From a practical standpoint, this method is far more convenient than directly determining

MLSS. It requires only a single incremental test, does not involve multiple 30-minute constant-load
trials on different days.

However, despite its practicality, there are two important methodological and conceptual limitations
that must be considered.

1. THE LIMITED ACCURACY OF COMMERCIAL LACTATE HAND DEVICE The widely used commercial lactate hand device (such as the Lactate Pro 2) typically has an error of 0.2–0.4 mmol/L which might affect the calculation of the second lactate threshold. (5)
2. DIFFERENT LT2 DEFINITIONS, DIFFERENT LT2 OTUCOMES This is the main limitation.

There are multiple definitions of the second lactate threshold, each derived from different
mathematical models applied to the relationship between power output and blood lactate concentration
obtained during the incremental step test.In other words, once lactate and power data are plotted,
various computational approaches can be used to identify the LT2 breakpoint.

Below are some of the most popular methods:

*OBLA: ‘Onset of Blood Lactate Accumulationʼ

OBLA 3.0 mmol/L: power at a fixed blood lactate concentration of 3 mmol/L

OBLA 3.5 mmol/L: power at a fixed blood lactate concentration of 3.5 mmol/L

OBLA 4.0 mmol/L: power at a fixed blood lactate concentration of 4 mmol/L

Dmax: the maximum perpendicular distance between the lactate curve and a straight line drawn
connecting the first and last lactate point values

Many different evolution of the Dmax method, which use different mathematical modelling, such as:

ModDmax

Exp-Dmax

Log-Exp-ModDmax

Log-Poly-ModDmax

But here is the thing: different definitions of the second lactate threshold yield different power
outputs for LT2.

In other words, the threshold you identify depends on the method you choose. The physiology of the
athlete does not change — the mathematical model does.

So which definition/method of LT2 should you use?

There is currently no gold-standard procedure for estimating MLSS — and therefore

MMSS — using the LT2 approach.

The outcome of an LT2 calculation depends heavily on the specific parameters of the incremental step
test: step duration, power increase between steps, number of steps, and starting intensity. The same
method — for example, Dmax — can yield different LT2 values depending on how these parameters are
set. More importantly, research has yet to consistently validate any specific test protocol for a
given LT2 definition as a reliable means of accurately estimating MLSS.

For example, a comprehensive study (6) comparing several LT2 definitions and incremental test
protocols found that 4-minute step durations using the Log-PolyModDmax method provided the most
accurate estimate of MLSS. This is counterintuitive, as longer steps (6–10 minutes) are generally
assumed to improve accuracy by allowing the physiological steady state to be reached. Nonetheless,
these findings highlight that estimating MLSS/MMSS from LT2 remains an evolving area of research,
and further investigation is required to determine whether precise and standardized guidelines can
be established.

So, what is the practical takeaway about LT2 derived from incremental step tests?

The validity of the second lactate threshold methods for determining the Maximum

Metabolic Steady State is limited. For this reason, Critical Power is preferred for establishing the
true MMSS, as we will discuss in the next section.

This doesnʼt mean that lactate testing is useless. It still provides valuable information,
particularly because LT2 is generally correlated with MMSS. Lactate testing might be more
interesting for tracking the evolution of lactate at a fixed power over time in the same athlete,
rather than accurately establish the MMSS for training zones.

A hyperbolic relationship between speed (or power output) and the duration for which that speed or
power can be sustained was first described by the legendary British exercise physiologist Sir
Archibald Hill. In a seminal paper published in Nature in 1925 (!), Sir Hill identified this pattern
by plotting the speed–time relationship derived from world record performances in running, swimming,
and rowing (both in men and women). (7)

Photograph of Sir Archibald Hill alongside his original world-record hyperbolic performance
relationships. Images adapted from Burnley et al. (8)

A century later, this hyperbolic relationship continues to be supported by a vast body of scientific
evidence and has proven resilient even as human performance standards have dramatically improved.

“The hyperbolic running speed–time relationship plotted for the current (as of March 2019) world
records from 1500 m to 5000 m (in blue, records held by different athletes) and the personal best
times over the same distances run by an individual elite distance runner (Eliud

Kipchoge, EK, in red).” (2) Image modified from Jones et al. (2).

Furthermore, this hyperbolic relationship between sustainable speed or power output and time has
also been observed in other animal species—including salamanders, rats, racehorses, and land crabs.
So, it has been proposed to represent a “fundamental bioenergetic property of living systems,” (2)
rather than a phenomenon unique to humans.

From this hyperbolic power-duration relationship:

Critical Power (CP) is defined as the power output corresponding to the horizontal asymptote of the
curve. It represents the maximum metabolic steady state at which aerobic metabolism can sustain
energy production without the continuous accumulation of fatigue-related metabolites, thereby
allowing exercise to be maintained without rapid exhaustion. (9)

The Anaerobic Reserve (Wʼ) is defined as the curvature constant of the relationship or the square
area between CP and the curve at any point. It corresponds to a finite amount of work (expressed in
joules) that can be performed above CP. Importantly, the magnitude of W′ is theoretically fixed and
independent of the specific work rate chosen above CP: a higher power output results in a faster
depletion of W′, but the total amount of work that can be performed above CP remains the same.

(9) It seems to represent the anaerobic capacity of the athlete.

Image modified from Poole et al. 2016 (9)

The hyperbolic relationship between speed or power and sustainable duration can be expressed in two
alternative, linearized forms:

Plotting power or speed versus the inverse of time (1/time): in this case, the slope represents W′,
and the y-intercept corresponds to CP.

Plotting total work versus time: in this representation, the slope of the resulting line corresponds
to Critical Power (CP), while the y-intercept corresponds to W′, the finite work capacity above CP.

This is the key point of this KIW Research Note: yes—Critical Power has been repeatedly shown to
accurately mark the boundary between the heavy and severe exercise domains. In other words,
scientific evidence consistently demonstrates that exercising just above versus just below CP leads
to fundamentally different metabolic responses and rates of fatigue development, confirming that CP
reliably represents the

Maximum Metabolic Steady State. (11)

Specifically, some of the most relevant studies investigating metabolic responses at intensities
just above and just below CP have found that when exercising slightly below or at Critical Power
(CP), a steady state—even if delayed (after 2-3min)—is reached for both oxygen consumption and blood
lactate, and no signs of fatigue appear after 20–

30 minutes. In contrast, exercising just 5-10% above CP elicits a markedly different metabolic
response typical of the severe/high-intensity domain: oxygen consumption, blood lactate,
intramuscular inorganic phosphate and hydrogen ions rise continuously, until VO₂max is reached,
after which task failure occurs (approximately in 15-20 minutes (12,13).

While modelling the power–duration curve allows us to calculate Critical Power (CP) down to a single
watt (for example, 350 W), it should never be interpreted as an absolute, fixed number. (2)

Why? For two reasons:

1. Mathematical modelling inevitably carries a margin of error. Even when best-practice testing guidelines are followed — see next section and the upcoming KIW Research

Note) — some uncertainty always remains.

2. Physiology itself is not static. Day-to-day variability driven (likely) by recovery status, accumulated load, sleep, nutrition, and normal biological fluctuation means that the “true” CP is not a perfectly stable value.

For these reasons, CP should be considered a (small) transition bandwidth, not a single-point
metric. (2) Practically speaking, if testing yields a CP of 350 W (assuming the test quality is
solid), it is more realistic to interpret it roughly as a 340–355/360 W range.

This is precisely why, when building a 5/7 training zones model), the suggestion is defining

Zone 4 as ±5/10% around CP. Doing so ensures that:

Zone 3 clearly sits within the medium/heavy intensity domain

Zone 5 clearly falls within the high/severe intensity domain

Zone 4 captures the maximum metabolic steady-state transition (MMSS)

Within that Zone 4 bandwidth, you can target the lower, middle, or upper end depending on the goal
of the session and how you feel on the day — while still training within the intended metabolic
domain.

So how do you test and obtain your CP and W′ values? Performing a series of all-out efforts within
the high/severe-intensity domain. To obtain a valid and accurate estimate, the CP testing protocol
should follow precise guidelines: number of efforts, duration/intensity of efforts, recovery between
efforts, terrain, position on the bike, cadence and pacing.

The power and duration values obtained from these trials should then be plotted, and CP and

W′ calculated using each of the three mathematical models described above.

a. The 2 Parameters Hyperbolic Model (CP 2-P Hyp) b. The 2 Parameters Linear Power-Inverse Time
Model (CP 2-1/time) c. The 2 Parameters Linear Work–Time Model (CP 2-linear)

Image from Leo et al. 2022. (10).

You will likely obtain three different CP values and three different W′ values, each corresponding
to one of the three power–duration mathematical models described above. Why different values? While
in a virtual perfect dataset all the 3 models would theoretically yield identical CP and W′
estimates, real-world human performance is inherently variable, meaning the mathematical fit is
never 100% correct. As a result, each model can produce different parameter estimates (CP and Wʼ)
due to unavoidable measurement error and biological variability. So, which CP and W′ should you
choose? You should apply the

‘best individual fitʼ method, that is selecting the CP and W′ values derived from the one of the
three models that demonstrates the highest accuracy and precision. (1, 14)

Failing to apply the best individual fit approach can lead to meaningful underor overestimation of
Critical Power (CP). Moreover, if none of the three models provides adequate accuracy and precision,
you should consider repeating one or more of the all-out trials. In most cases, this reflects
suboptimal pacing or underperformance in at least one effort.

1. Establishing training zones requires knowing two exercise thresholds: the first lactate/ventilatory threshold (LT1/VT1) and the Maximum Metabolic Steady State (MMSS).

2. The MMSS represents the highest exercise intensity at which a steady state in oxygen consumption and muscle metabolites can still be maintained. It marks the transition between the heavy/medium intensity domain and the high/severe intensity domain.

3. The Maximum Lactate Steady State (MLSS) can provide an accurate and valid estimate of MMSS. However, it is impractical, as its assessment typically requires 4–6 separate 30-minute constant-power trials (at least three), each performed on different days.

4. While calculating the Second Lactate Threshold (LT2) from an incremental step test is more practical, there is currently no gold-standard procedure for estimating MLSS —

and therefore MMSS — using LT2. This is due to several reasons:

There are multiple definitions of LT2, each producing different power outputs. The threshold you
identify depends on the method you choose.

LT2 results are heavily influenced by the design of the incremental test: step duration, power
increase between steps, number of steps, and starting intensity.

Even the same LT2 definition (Dmax for example) can yield different values depending on these test
parameters. Research has not yet consistently validated any specific test protocol for LT2 as a
reliable means of accurately estimating

MLSS.

5. Critical Power (CP), the asymptote of the curve between power output and sustainable duration, represents a practical measure that has been repeatedly shown in scientific studies to accurately estimate the Maximum Metabolic Steady

State (MMSS).

6. To ensure this robust physiological basis, CP testing must follow precise guidelines, and the quality of the estimation can be evaluated using statistical and mathematical indices (the best individual fit method).

# The Role of Zone 2 Training in Endurance Sports

**Source:** knowledgeiswatt.substack.com/p/108-the-role-of-zone-2-training-in **Date:** October 15,

In a very debated previous KIW Research Note, we saw that, when matching total work (read energy
expenditure), zone 2 (low intensity, below the first threshold) induces less aerobic adaptations
gains (increase in VO2max and thresholds) when compared to higher exercise intensities (zone 3, 4,
5, 6).

However all successful endurance athletes spend about 80-90% of their total training time at low
intensity, irrespectively of the endurance speciality considered: running, swimming, triathlon,
rowing, cross-country skiing, road cycling.

In addition, also some scientific studies seems to suggest that high volume low intensity training
is crucial for superior endurance performance. For example, in the last KIW Research Note we saw
that marathon runners with lower marathon finish time (faster runners) perform the same weekly
amount of training (minutes) at medium and high intensity but a significantly higher volume of low
intensity, than marathoners with higher marathon finish times (slower runners).

So, why a high volume of low intensity training (zone 2 in a 5/7 zones model) is largely used by
successful endurance athletes?

A study published by Pekka Matomäki (Paavo Nurmi Centre & Unit for Health and Physical Activity,
University of Turku, Finland) on European Journal of Applied Physiology in 2025 tried to answer this
question. (1)

WHAT DID HE DO?

This scientific piece was a perspective. What does it mean? No data were collected and no
experiment/training protocol was performed. The author (Matomäki) ‘just’ tried to make some
hypothesis about why a high volume of zone 2 (low intensity) training is largely used by successful
endurance athletes based on all the previously scientific published data.

WHAT DID HE SUGGEST?

Here below you can find some of the Matomäki’s different hypothesis. Of all those made by the
author, I have included only those that make the most sense to me, while I have excluded those that
I considered less relevant. If you want to see all Matomäki’s hypothesis you can read the original
full text paper, it is free access! Full Text.

From here the abbreviation ‘LIT’ refers to low intensity training (zone 2).

HYPOTHESIS 1 “LIT PROVIDES MAINTENANCE OR SLIGHT IMPROVEMENTS TO PERFORMANCE WITHOUT CUMULATING
STRESS”

In my point of view, while this Matomäki’s statement can be considered a good start, I think it
could be more appropriate to say:

“LIT can stimulate physiological and performance adaptations, while cumulating less stress and
fatigue when compared to higher exercise intensities”

In fact complete cardiac autonomic recovery (heart rate variability), which can be read as readiness
to absorb a given load and successfully adapt to a training stimulus, can take 2-3 days to return at
baseline level after a session with focus medium or high intensity sessions. On the other hand, low
intensity training (of course, depending on the duration) seems to affect much less negatively
cardiac autonomic recovery. So, to obtain long-term performance gains, progressively increasing the
training volume at low intensity to complement the more powerful but limited (2/3 times per week)
amount of sessions with focus on medium and high intensity training which can be completed within
each microcycle (week) is probably the best sustainable way.

HYPOTHESIS 2 “LIT IS AN ALTERNATIVE METHOD FOR MOLECULAR ADAPTATION SIGNALS”

If you want to be a master of endurance training methodology, you need to understand how training
adaptations occur. Here’s how: A certain workout, or even just a particular moment within it,
induces a mechanical, ionic or metabolic perturbation in certain body’s physiological systems. These
changes trig the activation of molecules (such as PGC-1α), that stimulate the expression of certain
DNA genes, which in turn make positive endurance related adaptations happen.

The peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1 (PGC-1α) has been described by
some as the ‘‘master switch’’ for mitochondrial biogenesis. (2) Mitochondria are the the place where
energy is produced through the oxidative pathways in the muscle cells. An increase in mitochondria
means you become aerobically stronger and so higher your power at thresholds, FTP, Critical Power
and VO2max.

PGC-1α seems to be activated through various different signals:

an increase in free radicals an increase in muscle calcium (Ca++) an altered energy status (i.e.
lower ATP and higher AMP concentrations).

Interestingly, both low and high intensity training increase PGC-1α content, but it seems like they
act through two different pathways:

HIT stimulates PGC-1α through primarily metabolic pathways (lower ATP and higher AMP concentrations)

LIT through ionic perturbation (increase in muscle calcium (Ca++). Therefore low intensity training
can represent an alternative adaption pathway which, introducing variability, strengthen in a
complementary way the metabolic adaptive stimulus given by higher exercise intensities.

HYPOTHESIS 3 “LIT IS NEEDED PSYCHOLOGICALLY”

Both research data and experience from the field seem to suggest that high intensity training is
much more psychologically demanding when compared to low intensity training. This can be another
explanation about why the amount of medium and high intensity training doable within a microcycle in
the long term is limited, and so it should be complemented with a progressively increased high
volume of low intensity training to improve performance.

*HYPOTHESIS 4 “A HIGH VOLUME OF LIT OPTIMIZES DURABILITY”

This hypothesis have not been mentioned by Matomäki (this the reason of the ‘*’), but I think it’s
very relevant for endurance sports with competition longer than ~2 hours, so I added it. In fact,
previously published scientific data (3) showed that a high volume of low intensity training is
important to optimize durability (fatigue resistance/fatigue resilience), that is decreasing as less
as possible the performance level during prolonged exercise.

A BRIEF SUMMARY ON LOW INTENSITY TRAINING (ZONE 2)

So, based on this and the lasts previous KIW Research Notes, here a brief summary about the role of
low intensity or zone 2 (in a 5/7 zones model, below the first threshold) training in endurance
sports:

When matching total work (read energy expenditure), zone 2 (low intensity, below the first
threshold) induces less aerobic adaptations gains (increase in VO2max and thresholds) when compared
to higher exercise intensities (zone 3, 4, 5, 6)

However, all successful endurance athletes spend about 80-90% of their total training time at low
intensity, irrespectively of the endurance speciality considered: running, swimming, triathlon,
rowing, cross-country skiing, road cycling.

Marathon runners with lower marathon finish time (faster runners) perform the same weekly amount of
training (minutes) at medium and high intensity, but a significantly higher volume of low intensity,
than marathoners with higher marathon finish times (slower runners).

So, a high volume of low intensity training seems important to optimize endurance performance.

Why? Here some hypothesis:

1. LIT can stimulate physiological and performance adaptations, while cumulating less stress and fatigue when compared to higher exercise intensities. So, to obtain longterm performance gains in a sustainable way, progressively increasing the training volume at low intensity to complement the more powerful but limited (2/3 times per week) amount of sessions with focus on medium and high intensity training which can be completed within each microcycle (week) is likely the best way.

2. LIT is an alternative method for molecular adaptation signals, which means that it stimulates aerobic physiological adaptation through a different (and likely complementary) pathway from high intensity training. So, summing LIT+HIT can induce more aerobic adaptations when compared to use only LIT or only HIT.

3. LIT is psychologically less demanding t when compared to higher exercise intensities.

4. A high volume of LIT optimize durability (fatigue resistance).

Based on all these points, my suggestion is that every good training program should include 2/3
weekly session with focus on medium or high intensity training. As a consequence, if you are
timecrunched and training only ~5-10 hours per week, it doesn’t make sense to train 80-90% of your
time below your first threshold (like pro athletes do). If you trained like that, you wouldn’t be
optimizing your time and you would be leaving something on the table. After you’ve already inserted
these 2/3 weekly medium or high intensity training sessions, my suggestion to further improve your
performance (in a long term sustainable way) is to progressively increase the amount of low
intensity training.

Gabriele Gallo, PhD in Exercise and Sport Science

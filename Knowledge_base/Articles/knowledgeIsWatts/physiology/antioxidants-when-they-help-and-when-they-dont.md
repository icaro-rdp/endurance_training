# Antioxidants for Endurance Performance: When They Help and When They Get in the Way

**Source:** knowledgeiswatt.substack.com/p/118-antioxidants-for-endurance-performance **Date:**
November 28, 2025

Endurance training is, at its core, a stress-and-adaptation process. This stress is supposed to
happen. It signals your cells to get stronger by building more mitochondria, improving muscle
efficiency, and positively refining how your body uses fuel during exercise. Every hard ride creates
small amounts of muscle damage, metabolic stress, and the production of reactive oxygen species
(ROS). Reactive oxygen species are byproducts of normal metabolism. They are highly reactive
molecules formed when oxygen is used to produce energy. During exercise, oxygen demand is high and
more ROS are generated inside the working muscles.

While large amounts can damage cells, moderate increases in are actually useful because they act as
signals that help the body adapt by upregulating its own protective systems and stimulating the
production of new mitochondria. This is where antioxidants come in. Antioxidants are molecules that
can neutralize, or “scavenge,” excess ROS before they cause damage. They include both dietary
compounds like vitamins C and E, carotenoids, and polyphenols from fruits and vegetables, as well as
antioxidant enzymes that the body produces itself. The balance between ROS and antioxidants
determines whether oxidative stress leads to harm or to beneficial adaptation.

Antioxidant supplements often come up in the context of training because they can reduce oxidative
stress. But not all antioxidant strategies are equal. And in some cases, too much antioxidant intake
(especially from supplements) can actually blunt the signals we’re trying to enhance through
training.

Let’s break down how this works, and how cyclists can use antioxidants strategically without getting
in their own way.

1. What Actually Happens During Hard Training

During exercise, your muscles produce ROS (“oxidants”). These are often portrayed as “bad,” but
that’s quite misleading. ROS serve as one of the messengers that turn on training-related
adaptations—especially mitochondrial biogenesis and improvements in endurance capacity. This is what
we want!

This process involves several intracellular signalling pathways, including:

PGC-1α (drives mitochondrial growth)

AMPK and p38 MAPK (respond to metabolic stress)

NF-κB (involved in inflammatory and antioxidant defense gene expression)

When ROS rise during exercise, they activate these pathways, helping your body “learn” how to handle
stress more efficiently over time.

Chronic exercise itself is associated with a significant increase in antioxidant capacity and
reduction in pro-oxidant parameters, regardless of intensity, volume, type of exercise and studied
population [1]. In fact, a study elite rowers found that the number of years of training had a
larger effect on antioxidant status than dietary intake [2]!

However—too much oxidative stress can contribute to excessive fatigue or slow recovery during dense
training blocks. So, the goal is not “remove all ROS,” but maintain balance.

2. How Antioxidants Work
1. Scavenging Free Radicals (Direct Neutralization)

This is what most people think of: antioxidants bind and neutralize free radicals before they can
damage cells. Supplements like Vitamin C, Vitamin E, and some polyphenol extracts work mainly in
this direct scavenging role.

2. Upregulating Endogenous Defense Systems (Building Your Own Shield)

Your cells also have internal antioxidant systems—superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione, etc.
These can be upregulated by certain nutritional and training stimuli. This process is mediated
through transcription factors, especially NF-κB, which increases the expression of your cells’ own
defense enzymes. This internal defense system can be stimulated by plant foods rich in polyphenols
(berries, cocoa, olive oil, coffee, tea), and even by exercise itself, not by eliminating ROS, but
by helping your body handle them better long-term.

This distinction is critical:

Scavenging eliminates the signal. Upregulating strengthens your response.

And for endurance athletes, we want the response, not the removal of the signal. Several studies
have shown that adaptations to endurance training largely depend on exercise-induced oxidative
stress [3, 4].

3. Where Supplementation Can Go Wrong

Some antioxidant supplements—particularly high-dose vitamin C or E—have been shown to blunt
endurance training adaptations. They reduce the ROS signal so much that pathways like PGC-1α and
AMPK are activated to a lesser degree, which may reduce mitochondrial adaptations over time.

This effect has been noted in research examining supplement use during endurance training programs,
where vitamin C and E supplementation did not improve performance and in some cases attenuated
adaptations such as improvements in mitochondrial enzymes and VO₂max [5].

Similarly, antioxidant supplementation during short-term recovery periods is discussed in
postexercise recovery research, where it may reduce muscle damage markers but at the potential cost
of limiting adaptive remodeling in the long term [6].

4. So Should Cyclists Avoid Antioxidants? Not Exactly.

The key is timing and source.

Best case for antioxidants:

Heavy training blocks with accumulated fatigue

Multi-day stage races

Back-to-back hard sessions where recovery matters more than adaptation

Illness, high travel stress, or poor sleep

Worst case for antioxidants:

Base phase

When targeting aerobic/mitochondrial development

Immediately post-workout when adaptation signals are highest

If you blunt the stress signal when you want to get fitter, you may adapt less.

If you blunt the stress signal when you just want to survive tomorrow’s race, that’s strategic.

5. Food First, Then Supplements

Research suggests whole-food antioxidants do not interfere with training adaptations the same way
isolated supplements may. This is likely because whole foods contain compounds that support ROS
signaling regulation rather than simply blocking it.

Great choices include:

Berries

Citrus fruit

Leafy greens

Olive oil

Tomatoes

Nuts

Dark chocolate

Coffee and tea

Think of these as building your internal antioxidant capacity—not removing the training signal.

Supplements (vitamin C, vitamin E, NAC, resveratrol, curcumin, etc.) should be used purposefully,
not automatically.

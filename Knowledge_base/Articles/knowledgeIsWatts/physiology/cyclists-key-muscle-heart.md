# The Cyclist's Key Muscle: the Heart

**Source:** knowledgeiswatt.substack.com/p/115-the-cyclists-key-muscle-the-heart **Date:** November
21, 2025

Endurance performance is largely determined by the power of the aerobic engine. When athletes rely
on oxidative pathways to produce energy, they use systems that generate fewer metabolites and delay
peripheral fatigue. The main performance indicators that reflect the power of the aerobic system are
VO₂max, lactate/ventilatory thresholds, FTP, and Critical Power.

The main determinant of strong aerobic qualities is the cardiovascular system, because oxygen is
transported from the lungs to the working muscles through the bloodstream. The king of the
cardiovascular system is the heart— which acts as a pump responsible for delivering blood, and
therefore oxygen, to the working muscles. Because oxygen transport depends on cardiac output, having
a bigger and more functional heart is a key determinant of endurance performance. And, just like any
other muscle, the heart’s size can differ between different individuals, influencing the aerobic
qualities.

How do cardiac dimensions differ among athletes of different performance levels?

A study published by Brown and Colleagues (Liverpool John Moores University, UK) on Scandinavian
Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports in 2020 tried to answer this question. (1)

WHAT DID THEY DO?

Using conventional and speckle tracking 2D echocardiography, the heart dimension of the 3 following
groups was evaluated:

1. 69 male elite cyclists competing in UCI World Tour or UCI Pro Continental level events;

2. 30 sub-elite cyclists actively racing under a 1st, 2nd, or 3rd category British Cycling license;

3. 46 healthy, non-smoking male non-athlete university students/ staff engaging in fewer than 3 hours recreational activity per week;

WHAT DID THEY FIND?

Here some heart size and functionality parameters:

Left Ventricle End Diastolic Volume:

ELITE: 162±18 ml

SUB-ELITE: 149±19 ml

UNTRAINED: 104±21 ml

Data from Brown et al. (1)

Left Ventricle Mass (g):

ELITE: 210±31 g

SUB-ELITE: 163±26 g

UNTRAINED: 133±24 g

What is the left ventricle?

Data from Brown et al. (1)

The heart has four chambers: two upper chambers (atria, right and left) and two lower chambers
(ventricles, right and left). The left ventricle is the chamber where blood collects before being
pumped into the systemic circulation, delivering oxygen and nutrients to the working muscles. For
this reason, it can be considered the most important heart’s chamber for endurance performance.

What is the end diastolic volume?

End-diastolic volume is the amount of blood in the ventricle at the end of diastole, which is the
phase when the heart muscle relaxes and the chamber fills with blood. It represents the maximum
volume of blood the ventricle holds before contraction (systole), when blood is pumped into the
systemic circulation to go to the working muscles. End-diastolic volume is an important measure
because it reflects both the size of the heart chamber and its functional capacity to pump blood
efficiently.

So, left ventricular mass reflects the size of the heart, while end-diastolic volume reflects both
its size and its functional capacity.

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYS:

Cardiac size and function are greater in elite athletes than in sub-elite athletes, and greater in
sub-elite athletes than in sedentary individuals.

GABRIELE’S COMMENT

There’s not much more to add here. Elite endurance athletes have bigger, stronger hearts than their
lower-level peers, and sub-elite cyclists still outperform sedentary individuals.

These differences align with the variations in aerobic power across these groups, highlighting the
main role of heart and cardiovascular system for endurance performance.

Is this about genetics or training? Like most traits linked to athletic performance, it’s probably a
mix of both. An interesting insight could come from the exceptionally large chest size of some great
cyclists—think Fausto Coppi or Jonas Vingegaard—suggesting that cardiac size may be at least in part
inherited.

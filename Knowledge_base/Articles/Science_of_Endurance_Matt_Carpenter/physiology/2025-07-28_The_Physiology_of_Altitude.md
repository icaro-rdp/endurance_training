Altitude has a range of impacts on physiology that are of interest to athletes. Before we start, we need to define some key terms and definitions:

Homeostasis: the tendency towards stability, maintained by physiological processes.

## Why Altitude?

Extreme environments impact physiology

  * Heat/cold

  * Space

  * Altitude

(I’ll cover cold, space and heat soon!) 

Understanding altitude is relevant to understanding the human body, and to better adapt to these environments

Out body is always aiming to maintain ‘homeostasis’, so in response to a new environment, our body adapts to better survive in the environment we place it.

Altitude training for athletes becomes of interest particularly following the Mexico olympics of 1968. As the figure below shows, it was conducted at very high altitude and as a result, threw up some very strange results…

In short duration events times were much faster than expected, with world records in all mens track events 400m or shorter.

But what happened in the endurance events? They got slower…. in fact, the marathon was 8 minutes slower than 1964…

But it gets more interesting, the athletes that had backgrounds of living at altitude did better than expected.

**Lets take a look at why all this happened**

People often say ‘the air is thinner’ at altitude. And whilst this isn’t overly precise, it outlines roughly what is changing. There are less gas molecules, so air particles are more spread out. As a result, for each breath, you are inhaling less oxygen.

While there's the same proportion of oxygen in the air at high altitudes, the reduced atmospheric (barometric) pressure means there's less oxygen in each lungful of air.

This makes it harder for the body to obtain the oxygen it needs.

Therefore, you enter a state of hypoxia.

In response to this, a number of changes occur rapidly (and some over a longer period of times).

The figure above shows the the bodies physiology adapting to the reduced oxygen availability to make sure we are still getting the oxygen we need.

Key areas of interest when it comes to the acute and prolonged response and adaptation include

  * Lungs —> increased ventilation and depth of breathing

  * Heart —> Heart rate increases initially, then drops. Stroke volume decreases.

  * Blood —> increased production of red blood cells (due to EPO growth Factor) which increases oxygen carrying capacity.

Since you’re getting less oxygen in, it’s unsurprising that performance is worse at altitude.

But what happens over time? Well the kidneys are send into overdrive making EPO. This is the hormone that produces red blood cells.

EPO elevation stimulates an increase in red blood cells.

Hemoglobin is the main part of the red blood cell, so an increase in red blood cells means an increase in hemoglobin. This allows for better oxygen transport around the body and into the working muscles.

This increase in red blood cells takes more time, so you can see in the study below, performance improves over time at altitude due to the adaptations, but never gets back to the sea level performance.

The body adapts over time, but VO2max remians 7% lower.

But what happens when you go back to sea level? 

You take that enhanced oxygen delivery back to sea level and the theory goes that performance improves as a result. 

But research has not consistently supported this, for a few reasons, the main one being that whilst you’re at altitude, you cannot train as hard. Research now looks at segmented strategies, such as live high train low, to seek to get all the benefits of altitude training…

## What is live high train low training?

The "Live High, Train Low" (LHTL) model is a strategy in altitude training designed to get the physiological benefits of altitude exposure without compromising training intensity. 

The concept is that athletes reside at elevations typically between 2,000 and 2,500 meters above sea level (or mimic using an altitude tent if you’re Mo Farah!). This prolonged exposure triggers adaptations like increased erythropoietin (EPO) production, leading to elevated red blood cell counts and enhanced oxygen-carrying capacity.

But rather than compromising training, workouts are conducted at or near sea level, where oxygen availability is higher. This allows athletes to maintain higher intensities and avoid the performance detriments often observed when training in hypoxic conditions.

The goal here is simple, combine the physiological advantages of altitude with the neuromuscular stimulus of sea-level intensity, allowing for the benefits of altitude training.

Training camps in locations like Flagstaff (USA), Font-Romeu (France), or Sierra Nevada (Spain), offer accessible low-altitude training facilities alongside high-altitude accommodation. This is aimed exactly at the benefits outlined above.

It also partially explains the interest in altitude tents that athletes such as Mo Farah were known to sleep in!

There may well be benefits to this method of training, but it’s not without drawbacks and a number of vital considerations, which I would be more than happy to cover in a deeper dive, to scratch the surface, a recent review highlighted the need for close monitoring of Sleep Quality, Fatigue, and Hydration Status to Avoid Developing Infections, Illnesses, or Overreaching/Overtraining States in order to best support athletes. It’s also vital to have normal iron levels to get the desired response.

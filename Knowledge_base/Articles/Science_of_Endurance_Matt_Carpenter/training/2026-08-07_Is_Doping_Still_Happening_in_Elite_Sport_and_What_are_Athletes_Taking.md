# Less Than One Percent of Athletes Doping??

WADA’s 2024 testing figures say sport is almost clean. The sad fact is, that no one believes the testing data.

In 2024, its accredited laboratories analysed 278,000 samples from across world sport, urine and logged every result centrally. Of all those samples, 0.78% came back as what’s called an Adverse Analytical Finding: a positive. The year before it was 0.80%, so it nudged down, essentially no change over the year.

Read quickly, that sounds like good news bordering on boring. Fewer than eight samples in a thousand flag positive, and the rate is falling. Sport, apparently, is clean.

But as I’m most people reading this will agree, elite sport is not clean. The question is, how dirty is it?

Let’s dig into some details of WADA’s testing over 2024, alongside some physiology, to see what this all means.

## A positive is not a ban

Start with what an Adverse Analytical Finding (AAF) actually is, because the phrase bandied about a lot. An AAF is a lab saying a prohibited substance, or its marker, was present in a sample. It is not a conviction. Every finding then goes through a results management process, and some of those positives have entirely legitimate explanations, most commonly, a granted Therapeutic Use Exemption for a medication. The number of positives and the number of athletes actually sanctioned are two different figures, and WADA is careful to say so.

So the headline already isn’t “dopers caught.” But there’s a deeper problem, and it’s the one that matters most.

A testing figure measures what the tests catch. It does not measure how much doping happens. And it seems clear that there is a discrepancy between these numbers. A low positive rate can mean very few people are doping, or it can mean plenty are, and the testing has holes in it. The number alone cannot tell you which. Hold on to that distinction, because everything below turns on it.

## The average is lying to you

The first thing worth doing with any headline statistic is refusing to accept it as a single number.

Split WADA’s 0.78% by sport category and it comes apart immediately. In Olympic sports, the positive rate was 0.56%. In non-Olympic sports, it was 1.87%, more than three times higher. Blend them into one overall figure and that entire gap disappears into a tidy sub-one-percent average that describes neither group well. But it also tells its own story, do we really believe olympic sports are more clean than non-olympic sports, that’s something I am very wary of believing!

In any case, the summary statistic is meaningless, it takes two different worlds and reports their midpoint as if it were the truth about both. If you only ever read the headline, you’d never know that one half of the sporting world is flagging at triple the rate of the other.

But we can also separate by drug, and here things get more interesting.

## EPO: the drug that never left

EPO (erythropoietin) is, in its natural form, one of your own hormones. Your kidneys release it when they sense your blood running low on oxygen, and it travels to your bone marrow with an instruction to make more red blood cells. More red blood cells mean more haemoglobin, and haemoglobin is what carries oxygen from your lungs to your muscles. For an endurance athlete, that delivery system is pivotal. The more oxygen you can shuttle to working muscle, the harder and longer you can go, the fitter and faster you will be.

This is exactly why a synthetic version, available since the late 1980s, became the most transformative (and destructive) drug in endurance sport. Injecting recombinant EPO tells the marrow to keep building red cells well past the point your physiology would ever choose. Oxygen-carrying capacity climbs, and with it the numbers that predict endurance performance. Professional cycling in the 1990s was reshaped around it. Read Tyler Hamilton’s brilliant book ‘The Secret Race’ and you get one hell of an expose on how EPO was used and abused in the TdF.

The danger sits in the very same mechanism as the benefit. Red blood cells are, in bulk, what makes blood thick. Push the proportion of red cells (the haematocrit) high enough and the blood turns viscous, making it harder for the heart to pump, and far more prone to clotting. Do that to an endurance athlete, whose resting heart rate is already low and whose blood concentrates further overnight or across a long stage, and you create a risk of thrombosis, stroke and cardiac failure. Put simply, the thing that makes EPO work is the thing that can kill you.

So EPO matters. And here’s what the data does when you line up the last three years of findings for EPO and the drugs like it:

2022: seventy-three positives. 2023: a hundred and seventeen. 2024: a hundred and forty-nine. It’s roughly doubled in two years, while the overall positive rate barely moved. Sitting underneath that flat, boring headline was one category climbing steadily the whole time.

Now the honest part. That rise has at least two completely different explanations, and the numbers can’t tell you which is true. Either more athletes are using EPO (maybe), or the testers are simply getting better at catching the ones who always were (probably). I suspect it’s some of both. Because the one thing we can say with confidence is that EPO never went away. It has been a staple of endurance doping for thirty years, and nothing about human physiology or human ambition has changed to retire it. A rising number of positives is an interesting signal — but “more use” and “better detection” create the same upward line on the graph, so this data doesn’t tell us that exactly.

But if testing is getting better at detecting EPO, there are some substances that it’s definitely not getting better at…

## The suspects that get away

Now for the strangest part of the report, and the one I find most revealing.

WADA singles out three classes of substance as risky enough that a minimum amount of testing for them is mandatory. EPO and its relatives are one. The other two are growth hormone, and the growth-hormone-releasing factors, the peptides that prod your own pituitary into pumping out more growth hormone of its own. These are the prime suspects. You’d expect them to be where the positives pile up.

They’re almost nowhere.

Growth hormone: roughly 19,500 tests in 2024, and four positives. The growth hormone releasing factors: over 72,000 tests, fifteen positives. That’s a hit rate around two-hundredths of one percent. EPO, at under a quarter of a percent, is the trigger-happy one of the three.

Why so few? The physiology of detection is tough here. Your body makes its own growth hormone, in pulses, and clears it from the blood within minutes. Levels swing with sleep, stress and exercise. Catching an injected dose means catching a needle in a haystack that empties itself while you’re looking. Testers have got clever, one method spots the altered mix of hormone isoforms that recombinant GH leaves behind, another tracks slower-moving downstream markers like IGF-1 and a collagen fragment that stay elevated a little longer, but the window is narrow and the drug is a nightmare to pin down. The releasing factors are, if anything, worse: you’re chasing a peptide whose whole job is to tell the body to do something it already does.

Which brings us back to the distinction from the start. A near-zero hit rate is the most ambiguous result in the whole report, because it has three readings that point in opposite directions. Maybe almost nobody uses these drugs (I’m sceptical). Maybe the mandatory testing is deterring people before they start (somewhat I suspect) which would be the system working exactly as intended. Or maybe plenty use them and the tests simply can’t see it (also somewhat…). Those are three very different worlds, and four positives out of nineteen thousand is consistent with all of them. Sometimes catching almost nobody is what success looks like. Sometimes it’s what failure looks like. The number can’t tell you which.

## The one test that keeps working

For contrast, look at the test that does earn its keep. The physiology behind it is, to me, the most elegant thing in the entire anti-doping arsenal.

The problem with testosterone and the anabolic steroids built around it is that testosterone is endogenous: you already have it, so you can’t catch a doper simply by finding it. For years the workaround was a ratio. Testosterone measured against a related compound the body produces alongside it, with an abnormal balance raising suspicion. But suspicion isn’t proof, luckily, there is now a solution.

The solution here comes from the idea that synthetic testosterone is manufactured from plant sterols. Plants build their carbon through photosynthesis, and in doing so they very slightly favour the lighter of carbon’s two stable isotopes, carbon-12, over carbon-13. That means testosterone synthesised from a plant carries a subtly different carbon-isotope signature than the testosterone made in the body. Labs are now able to differentiate between these, which has allowed for much more effective detection of synthetic testosterone.

WADA have stated that this method has the highest proportion of positives of any test they run, and they ran ten percent more of them in 2024 than the year before. While most of the tests catch pretty much no-one, this one keeps finding people, big result!

There’s a related idea worth knowing, because it’s another big development in the anti doping world. The Athlete Biological Passport. The ABP doesn’t try to catch the drug at all. Instead it tracks an athlete’s own blood values over time and watches for the fingerprint the doping leaves behind, a red-cell count that shifts in a way biology wouldn’t choose on its own. You don’t need to catch the needle if you can catch the consequence.

The issue here is that tools such as altitude could be used to mask EPO use, and the recent book Dope (which I would highly recommend), outlines this explicitly.

## What the numbers actually mean?

So, is sport clean? The answer is that 0.78% is not a true figure, and most research that tries to quantify this tells us that surprise surprise, it’s a huge underestimation. 

What 0.78% tells you is that across the samples that were collected, using the tests we currently have, at the sensitivities those tests currently reach, fewer than eight in a thousand flagged. And the drugs that are hardest to detect, the ones the system itself flags as highest-risk, are exactly the ones contributing least to the total. 

Are athletes avoiding these are or these in fact the ones most likely to be used and abused? I would suspect the latter.

Saying this, the testing is more extensive than it has ever been, it’s quite possible that the low catch rate might mean deterrence is working, at least to a point. The point isn’t that everyone’s doping, I don’t believe this, and nor do most rationale people. But it’s important to know that the 0.78% stat is not the final word, or a gospel stat. The interesting story is not the headline number, it’s in the breakdown of the data.

I’ve always found doping in sport a fascinating topic, possibly because of the sceptic in me, but looking into these WADA figures was great fun! I look forward to the next data release!

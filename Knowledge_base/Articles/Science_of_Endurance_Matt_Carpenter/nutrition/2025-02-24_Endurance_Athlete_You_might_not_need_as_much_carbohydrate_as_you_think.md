# Endurance Athlete? You might not need as much carbohydrate as you think!

As an endurance athlete, you’ve probably been bombarded with supplement companies and influencers telling you how much carbohydrate you need. For most athletes, recommendations have varied but been between 60-90 grams per hour. In practically terms, 2-3 standard gels per hour.

[Saying this, more recently, there have been suggestions that maybe up to 120 grams of carbohydrate would benefit performance.](https://www.endureiq.com/blog/carbohydrate-ingestion-rates-during-exercise-90-vs-120-grams-per-hour-which-is-best)

I try to stay as objective as possible, but to be clear, I do not think 120g per hour of carbohydrates is something that the vast majority of people should aim for. Though there are some interesting hypothesis around it, as research has shown that only 75% of the 120g/hour of carbohydrate is used for energy, so what happens to that other 25%? Why would it be beneficial? These are all questions being asked. One interesting hypothesis is the idea that it comes back to the brain, and stimulating the higher centres of the brain to improve performance. This is why in some cases, carbohydrate mouthrinse improves performance, even when you don’t actually ingest the carbohydrate!

Anyway, back to the study in question, [Prins et al. (2025)](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/ajpcell.00583.2024) looked to differentiate between the two glucose pools in the body, the muscle and the liver. They did this by splitting participants into 2 groups, high carb and low carb. The high carb would have high muscle glycogen, the low carb group would have low muscle glycogen. Then liver glycogen would be manipulated by in-race carbohydrate feeding (no carbs = low liver glycogen, carbs = maintained liver glycogen).

After this, they gave both groups 10g per hour of carbohydrate (Yes, only 10 grams per hour!), or a placebo with no carbs in at all. They reported that performance was no different between low carb and high carb groups when they consumed this 10 grams per hour. But the 10 grams per hour improved endurance performance.

## Why is this?

The overriding theory of this group (which to be transparent, I have some sympathy with, maybe not in 100% agreement, but agree with the general premise that I’m about to outline), is that of these two glucose pools, we have overestimated the importance of muscle glycogen. Research in the 1960’s identified glycogen as central to exercise performance (hence the many years of recommending carbohydrate loading). But what often gets missed in the studies testing this is that alongside muscle glycogen being depleted when exercise stops (hence the idea that muscle glycogen is vital), blood glucose has also dropped. This research team outline the idea that if you can prevent blood glucose dropping into hypoglycaemic levels, you can get all the benefits to performance. The interesting aspect of this is that blood glucose is tightly regulated by the second glucose pool (the liver), and just 10 grams per hour of carbohydrate can support the maintenance of blood glucose.

    This figure outlines the two glucose pools. In most individuals, we have around 400 calories of carbohydrate in the liver, and 1500 in the muscle. That glycogen in the liver is the constant source of blood glucose which helps us maintain homeostasis.

This is why there was no difference between low carb and high carb groups on performance, quite simply (as the story goes), muscle glycogen is not to important. Muscle glycogen is likely depleted in low carb athletes, but so long as they avoided hypoglycaemia, and all the negative aspects of this state (such as nausea, light-headedness, weakness and disorientation) as well as the signal it sends to the brain to reduce intensity, then performance can be maintained.

This is an open debate in the research, but a really interesting one, closely linked to my own research. The takeaway here is, for athletes out there, if you’re currently consuming more than 10g/hour carbs during your long workouts, try dropping this slightly and see how you feel. You may feel awful, if so, add them back in. But my predication based on this research and the accompanying theory, is that most athletes could get away with lowering their carb intake during training and races.

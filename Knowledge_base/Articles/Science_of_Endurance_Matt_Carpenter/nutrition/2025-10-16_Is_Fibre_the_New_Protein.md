# Is Fibre the New Protein?

Today’s article is not so much about science, but about prediction (hopefully somewhat based on science). So you can look out for what I think is a growing trend within the nutritional sciences and across social media. I’m sure I’m not alone in saying that the protein obsession is getting out of hand. You can’t walk down an isle in the supermarket without seeing a food labelled as ‘Protein X’. I don’t dislike this as a premise (other than finding is moderately annoying), there are great protein based options, and most people would benefit from increasing their protein intake. Though not everything needs to have protein added to it, more choice is generally good and empowering for the consumer. 

But the lack of regulation on labelling has meant that foods with relatively negligible protein contents can be labelled as ‘protein’, adding the perception of health to at best, health neutral products. 

This obsession stems from a large grain of well established science, that being that there is good data on the role of protein in health and performance. This has eventually resulted in public understanding of the benefits of increasing protein intake. This is largely been positive, more people understand the importance and benefits of increasing protein intake. But as always, this is capitalised on by those looking to market products with the term protein, hence the obsession with protein labelled foods.

Now I’m not about to go all Joe Wicks, I’m partial to a protein bar and getting circa 20g protein in a quick snack is a helpful and practical way of boosting protein intake. Companies like MyProtein (not sponsored) have a fantastic range of products to conveniently increase protein intake, and they aren’t the only ones.

But not all brands are created equal, nor are all protein fortified products. A snickers protein bar contains a mere 10g protein. I would argue this is not worth the protein label, and is a fairly brazen attempt to protein wash in order to add the perception of health to something slightly less health negative that a regular snickers.

Akin to the joke that British comedian Al Murray made about the USA, protein is a good idea that’s got out of hand. It’s the current health obsession. Largely for good in my opinion, but like anything, it’s being capitalised on by those who piggyback off the good name protein has.

**So how does this link to fibre?**

I think fibre is following in the footsteps of protein. Data has repeatedly demonstrated the health promoting effects of dietary fibre for a wide range of health outcomes. Particularly with the growing science of gut health, the data on fibre seems to be becoming more clear cut, at least in the simple takeaway that much like protein, most people should be consuming more fibre. Reduced satiety, improved gut health, improved lipid profile, improved glycemic parameters, there’s a good argument to say improved focus on fibre intake would make a noticeable difference to public health.

However, it often takes a while for science to translate into public perception. This is something scientists have to get better at, and possibly are. There are lots of brilliant science communicators using their social media to translate science to the interested public. As science translates to public understanding, I have no doubt brands will follow by creating fibre supplements, fortifying fibre to foods and adding fibre to supplemental drinks and foods and using it as a marketing tool. 

And again, with proper regulation this could only be a good thing. Like protein (but possibly even more dramatically??), most people would benefit from increasing fibre intake, for health and performance, there is a good argument to be made here. 

**So will fibre become the new protein?** Ubiquitous in labelling on health foods, used by brands to market their products and hard to avoid to the point of it becoming a daily frustration when walking up and down supermarket isles, or scrolling past instagram ads? That’s my prediction, I think it’s already happening in its early stages, just like protein. And once more, I would see this move as net positive for health and public understanding on the role of fibre. That probably won’t stop it being annoying, and it’ll come alongside waves of misinformation. But improved public understanding of fibre consumption may be one of the next big steps for science communication and brands looking to capitalise. We shall see.

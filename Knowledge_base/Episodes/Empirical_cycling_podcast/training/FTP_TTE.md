# FTP and TTE: Why the One-Hour Rule Misleads — Complete Guide

_Source: Empirical Cycling Podcast — Kolie Moore & Kyle (Spotify: [episode](https://open.spotify.com/episode/1EQufX5qpuNtxLqVCQX6b4?trackId=1EQufX5qpuNtxLqVCQX6b4))_

## What FTP Really Means

FTP is better understood as a physiological boundary than as a guaranteed 60-minute effort. The episode argues that the real variable is **time to exhaustion (TTE)** at threshold: many athletes can hold FTP for less than an hour, and that does not mean the number is wrong.

> Practical summary: above FTP, fatigue accelerates; below FTP, fatigue slows.

## Why the One-Hour Definition Is Limited

- The hour is easy to remember, which is why it became culturally sticky.
- It is not biologically special.
- A full one-hour time trial can be a poor routine test because it is highly fatiguing and can interfere with training for days afterward.
- FTP is individual, so some athletes can hold it close to an hour while many cannot.

The episode contrasts two common framings:

| Framing | Usefulness | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FTP as "one hour" | Memorable and simple | Treats an approximation like a law |
| FTP as a threshold boundary | Better matches how fatigue changes | Requires thinking in TTE, not just power |

## The Paper Behind The Episode

The podcast centers on a study of 11 masters endurance runners who completed six weeks of threshold training twice per week while keeping the rest of their running routine unchanged. In the study, FTP-like work was measured as **maximal lactate steady state (MLSS)** speed.

| Measure | Before | After | What it suggests |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| TTE at MLSS | 45 min | 63 min | Threshold duration improved substantially |
| MLSS as % VO2max | 85% +/- 4 | About the same | The main gain was longer duration, not a big shift in relative intensity |
| Absolute VO2max | 3.82 L/min | 3.96 L/min | Small increase |
| Test distance | 10.4 km | 15.7 km | Better sustained performance |

This was a running study, so the exact numbers do not transfer directly to cycling. The episode uses it as evidence that threshold training can extend the amount of time an athlete can actually hold threshold.

## How The Training Was Progressed

The study used a classic extensive-threshold approach:

- Two threshold sessions per week.
- The rest of the weekly training stayed the same.
- Total time in zone increased from about 30 minutes to 60 minutes over six weeks.
- Progression came from adding time, not pushing intensity higher.

Example progression pattern:

| Week | Example session shape |
| --- | --- |
| Early block | 3 x 10, then 2 x 15 |
| Middle block | 3 x 12, then 2 x 18 |
| Late block | 3 x 20, then 2 x 30 |

The episode's practical message is simple: if you have never extended threshold intervals, add time in zone and see what happens.

## What The Lactate And Fuel Data Suggest

The episode spends a lot of time on the idea that threshold adaptation is not just about power, but also about how the body handles fuel and lactate.

- Blood lactate at minute 10 of the time-to-exhaustion test correlated strongly with TTE, with lower lactate tending to mean longer TTE.
- Carbohydrate use at minute 10 also correlated with TTE, again with lower values tending to align with longer TTE.
- The average carbohydrate use across the group did not show a dramatic global shift, so the clearest story is the individual relationship between fuel handling and threshold duration.

The episode's interpretation is that threshold training likely improves the ability to oxidize lactate and manage carbohydrate flux, rather than making threshold a simple "100% carbohydrate" state.

## How To Think About Threshold Progression

Threshold work should be progressed by adding time in zone, not by endlessly repeating the same interval duration and hoping FTP rises on its own.

| Progression method | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Add time per rep | 3 x 10 -> 3 x 12 -> 3 x 15 |
| Add a rep | 2 x 15 -> 3 x 15 |
| Connect work bouts | 3 x 20 -> 2 x 30 -> 1 x 40+ |

The episode also notes that volume supports extensive threshold work. In other words, more overall aerobic work helps threshold sessions work better.

## When This Matters Most

- When FTP has been treated as a fixed one-hour number instead of a trainable boundary.
- When threshold intervals are always the same length and never extend.
- When an athlete can produce good power but seems capped by TTE.
- When a coach wants to decide whether more threshold work or more VO2max work is the better next step.

If TTE is rising while FTP is flat, the block may still be working. If FTP stalls and the ceiling needs to move, the episode points toward adding VO2max work somewhere in the plan.

## Pros and Cons Summary

**Pros**

- Separates FTP from TTE, which makes threshold training more practical.
- Explains why longer threshold intervals often matter more than a single hard test.
- Gives a realistic way to progress work over time.

**Cons**

- The one-hour definition is easy but misleading.
- The study is on runners, not cyclists.
- Some of the mechanistic explanation is inferred rather than directly measured.

## Decision Checklist

1. Are you assuming FTP must equal a full one-hour effort?
2. Has threshold time in zone actually increased over the last block?
3. Is the athlete ready for more threshold volume, or does the plan need more VO2max work?
4. Is there enough total volume and recovery to support extensive threshold work?
5. Are you using TTE as the main marker of threshold progress, not just power alone?

## Key Takeaway

FTP is the boundary; TTE is the trainable piece. The one-hour rule is a useful convention, but the episode argues it should not be treated as a physiological constant.

# Ten Minute Tips #22: Training Myths, Part 2 — Complete Guide
_Source: Empirical Cycling Podcast — Kolie Moore & Kyle Houston_

---

## What Are the Core Specificity Myths?

A common coaching error is over-applying the "principle of specificity" to the point where athletes believe they must only train exactly how their target event looks on paper:
1. **Myth 1:** *"Sprinters and non-sprinters need fundamentally different aerobic training programs."*
2. **Myth 2:** *"Crit racers mostly need to focus on anaerobic efforts and micro-intervals."*
3. **Myth 3:** *"You don't need to train FTP if you aren't a time trialist."*

```
  ┌─────────────────────────────────┐      ┌─────────────────────────────────┐
  │         Pervasive Myth          │      │      Physiological Reality      │
  ├─────────────────────────────────┤      ├─────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Sprinters need different base   │ ──►  │ Aerobic stimulus is universal   │
  │ Crits = Anaerobic focus only    │ ──►  │ Crits are primarily aerobic     │
  │ FTP is only for Time Trials     │ ──►  │ FTP sets all aerobic throughput │
  └─────────────────────────────────┘      └─────────────────────────────────┘
```

## Key Physiological Mechanisms / How to Think About It

### 1. Myth 1: Aerobic Training Across Phenotypes

#### A. Universality of the Aerobic Stimulus
At the cellular level, the upstream signaling pathways for aerobic adaptation do not change based on muscle fiber composition:
- **Signaling Triggers:** Intracellular calcium flux ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$/CaMK), elevated $\text{AMP}/\text{ATP}$ ratio (AMPK), and shear stress driving **PGC-1$\alpha$** transcription.
- **Structural Adaptations:** Capillarization of Type I and Type IIa fibers, mitochondrial enzyme upregulation (citrate synthase, $\beta$-HAD), and left ventricular eccentric hypertrophy.

#### B. The True Phenotype Difference: Fatigue and Power Preservation
- While the aerobic *stimulus* is universal, sprinters generate massive torque and recruit high-threshold Type II motor units more readily, creating greater neuromuscular and structural fatigue.
- **"Fatigue is the Ultimate Power Blocker":** When sprinters carry chronic aerobic fatigue, peak sprint power drops by 150–300+ Watts. Sprinters require targeted recovery management and slightly lower interval volume close to competition, not an absence of base or threshold training.

---

### 2. Myth 2: Criterium Demands and the "Anaerobic Battery"

#### A. The Bioenergetics of Criteriums
- A typical 60-minute criterium requires 800–1,200 kJ of work. Over 85–90% of total energy expenditure is supplied by **oxidative phosphorylation**.
- Anaerobic work capacity ($W'$) acts like a finite battery (15–30 kJ). 

```
  Scenario A: Low FTP (280W) in a 320W Surge
  ├── Surge is SUPRA-THRESHOLD (+40W above FTP)
  ├── Obligate draw on W' battery + high lactate accumulation
  └── Result: Battery empties after 8–10 surges; dropped before the finish.

  Scenario B: High FTP (350W) in a 320W Surge
  ├── Surge is SUB-THRESHOLD (-30W below FTP)
  ├── 100% Oxidative ATP supply; ZERO draw on W' battery
  └── Result: Full W' battery preserved for the final 200m field sprint.
```

#### B. The Superiority of Soft Skills (Race Craft)
- Drafting, smooth cornering lines, and pack positioning save 100–150 Watts per lap.
- Athletes who lack pack positioning waste their anaerobic capacity fighting the accordion effect, mistaking a tactical failure for an anaerobic fitness limiter.

---

### 3. Myth 3: FTP as the Foundation for All Road Events

- **The Floor of Performance:** Functional Threshold Power ($\text{MLSS}$) dictates the upper limit of steady-state aerobic metabolic flux.
- **Lactate Clearance Engine:** Higher threshold capacity increases monocarboxylate transporters (MCT1) and mitochondrial density, accelerating the clearance of lactate and hydrogen ions between attacks.
- Training FTP via sustained intervals ($2\times 20'$, $3\times 15'$, $2\times 30'$) builds durability and fatigue resistance that cannot be developed through intermittent race simulations alone.

## Practical Application & Prescriptions

### 1. Pacing VO2max Intervals for Sprinter Phenotypes
Sprinters with large $W'$ and high $\text{P}_{\max}$ frequently ruin VO2max workouts by executing extreme all-out starts:
- **The Sprinter VO2 Trap:** Pushing 150% FTP in the first 45 seconds exhausts anaerobic glycolysis, causes severe acidosis, and forces power to collapse below aerobic threshold.
- **Prescription:** Sprinters should ride VO2max intervals with **even pacing or a controlled progressive split** (starting at ~115–120% FTP and building), ensuring full oxygen uptake ($\dot{V}\text{O}_2\text{max}$) is sustained across the entire 3–5 minute duration.

```
  Sprinter VO2 Pacing:
  [ 0:00 - 0:45 ]  Controlled entry @ 115–120% FTP (Do not sprint out of saddle)
  [ 0:45 - 3:00 ]  Sustained maximum breathing rate (High cadence > 105 RPM)
  [ 3:00 - 4:00 ]  Empty the tank aerobically
```

### 2. High-Quality Sprint Training Protocol
- **State of Freshness:** Perform maximal sprints only when fully rested (first workout of a block or early in a session).
- **Execution:**
  - Repetitions: 4–6 maximal efforts.
  - Duration: 10–15 seconds.
  - Recovery: **Full recovery (4–5 minutes minimum)** between reps to allow 100% phosphocreatine ($\text{PCr}$) resynthesis.
- **Termination Rule:** If power drops below **95% of session peak**, stop the sprint workout immediately.

---

## Common Pitfalls & Limitations

1. **Sprinting at the End of Exhausting Base Rides:**
   - Attempting maximal sprints after 4 hours of riding. Peak power is blunted by 15–20%, converting a neuromuscular recruitment workout into a low-power glycolytic slog.
2. **Replacing Base Training with Endless "Crit Intervals":**
   - Doing 30/30s and microbursts year-round while neglecting sub-LT1 aerobic volume and sustained threshold extensions.
3. **Surging to "Generate Lactate for Fuel" on Base Rides:**
   - Intentionally attacking hills during endurance rides under the mistaken belief that extra blood lactate fuels the ride. This prematurely depletes stored muscle glycogen.

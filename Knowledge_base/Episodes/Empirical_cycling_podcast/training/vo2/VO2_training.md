# VO2max Training — Complete Guide

_Source: Empirical Cycling Podcast — Kelly Moore & James McKay_

---

## Why VO2max Training Matters

VO2max is the ceiling on your FTP. You cannot have an FTP higher than your VO2max, and for most athletes FTP sits somewhere around 80–86% of VO2max. If you are already near your ceiling, hammering more FTP intervals returns almost nothing. Raising VO2max by 10 W opens room to add far more than 10 W to FTP.

This is the "raising the roof" concept: VO2max is necessary but not sufficient for high-level performance, and it must be trained directly once an athlete has plateaued on threshold work.

---

## The Underlying Goal: Eccentric Cardiac Hypertrophy

Long-term VO2max improvement is driven primarily by increasing **stroke volume** — how much blood the heart pumps per beat. The mechanism is **eccentric hypertrophy**: the heart chambers expand when the heart is repeatedly filled to maximum capacity (Frank-Starling law).

The only lever available to force this adaptation is **cardiac preload** — the volume of blood filling the heart during diastole. Unlike skeletal muscle (where you can add external load eccentrically), the heart can only be overloaded by maximizing blood return.

Everything in this guide is targeted at maximizing preload duration per session.

---

## Why Power Targets Don't Work for VO2max

Assigning VO2max intervals as a percentage of FTP is unreliable:

- VO2 is not tightly linked to power output. Two athletes can produce the same watts at very different oxygen uptakes.
- Athletes with high anaerobic capacity (FTP at ~70–75% of VO2max) may need 30–40 minutes above FTP before breathing becomes truly maximal — that is an enormous kilojoule cost with little cardiac stimulus early on.
- Athletes with low FTP headroom or accumulated fatigue may be unable to hold a power target at all, discouraging them from continuing intervals that would still be productive.

Heart rate is better correlated with VO2max but still imperfect — it shifts with hydration, caffeine, heat, sleep, and fatigue from prior intervals. Chasing a fixed HR number is also not reliable.

**Primary feedback to use:** breathing. If you are gasping and cannot moderate your breath, you are in the zone.

## The 5 Tips

### Tip 1 — Increase Cadence (Most Important)

At the same power output, pedaling faster significantly increases cardiac output and stroke volume:

| Cadence | Heart rate | Stroke volume | Cardiac output | VO2       |
| ------- | ---------- | ------------- | -------------- | --------- |
| 70 RPM  | 160 BPM    | 114 mL        | 18.2 L/min     | 3.2 L/min |
| 90 RPM  | 163 BPM    | 130 mL        | 21.0 L/min     | 3.4 L/min |
| 110 RPM | 169 BPM    | 145 mL        | 24.5 L/min     | 3.7 L/min |

_Data from: Cycling Cadence Alters Exercise Hemodynamics_

Adding 40 RPM at the same power raises cardiac output by ~33% and stroke volume by ~30 mL, while increasing heart rate only ~9 BPM. By comparison, increasing power output enough to raise heart rate by 10 BPM typically increases stroke volume only ~5 mL.

**Why it works:** more frequent muscle contractions increase venous return via the muscle pump, directly increasing preload — the very mechanism responsible for eccentric hypertrophy.

**Practical note:** your power will likely drop at higher cadence. That is fine. The cardiac stimulus more than compensates, and peripheral muscular fatigue is reduced, allowing athletes to sustain quality across more intervals.

---

### Tip 2 — Go Max, Ignore Power

Assign no power target. Instead:

1. Start each interval **~20–30% harder than you think you can hold** for the full duration.
2. Allow power to fade through the interval.
3. Maintain maximum perceived exertion — keep pushing even as watts decline.
4. Stop when you can no longer sustain high enough effort to keep breathing at or near maximum.

This approach ensures you spend the maximum time near max preload. With fixed power targets, athletes either coast below their real stimulus (if the target is set too conservatively) or blow up and stop early.

**Volume implication:** going truly max means a lower total interval count. 15–25 minutes of work intervals is typically sufficient — done right, this yields 10–15 minutes near peak preload values.

---

### Tip 3 — Shorten Recovery

Short recoveries prevent full metabolic reset between intervals. This means:
- Less anaerobic reconstitution before the next effort
- The subsequent interval reaches VO2-level stress faster
- Less total "ramp-up" time wasted per session

**Rule of thumb:** start with shorter rest than you think you need, then add time only if you cannot generate sufficient effort in the next interval. The goal is the hardest possible next interval, not arbitrary symmetry.

This applies more to steady-state intervals. When going truly max (Tip 2), recovery needs to be sufficient to actually go max again — balance accordingly.

---

### Tip 4 — Assess After the Fact via Heart Rate Range

During the interval: focus entirely on effort. After the interval, review:

- **Was breathing maxed out?** This is the primary cue. If yes, the interval was effective regardless of power or heart rate.
- **Did heart rate plateau near recent max values?** Look for a high, stable HR across the last portion of the interval — not a specific BPM ceiling.
- **Do not use your all-time HR max as a target.** That number may have been set on a hot day with no sleep after caffeine. Seeing it again while trained and rested may indicate something went wrong.

> "Pay attention to breathing. If you're gasping like a fish on a boat, you nailed it."

---

### Tip 5 — Individualize Interval Duration

There is no universally correct interval length. Match duration to the athlete:

- **Minimum:** 2 minutes (takes ~60–90 seconds to reach max preload)
- **Typical range:** 2–8 minutes
- **Longer is not always better** — some athletes generate only 90 seconds of adaptive stimulus in a 5-minute interval (trepidation reduces pacing quality), whereas the same athlete gets 2+ minutes from a 3.5-minute interval done fully committed

The goal is **total time at max preload**, not total interval minutes. Three well-executed 3.5-minute intervals may provide more stimulus than five 5-minute intervals done cautiously.

**Signs to shorten:** heart rate never plateaus, breathing never fully maxes, or power stays artificially controlled.

**Signs to lengthen (or add intervals):** recovery between intervals is complete and the next interval doesn't feel maximally hard.

## Workout Structure

| Variable          | Recommendation                                         |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Cadence           | As high as possible while maintaining control — 95–110 RPM |
| Intensity         | Max perceived exertion; no power target                |
| Interval duration | 2–8 min depending on athlete (start 3–5 min)           |
| Total work        | 15–25 min (skip first interval if warm-up isn't sufficient) |
| Recovery          | Shorter than instinct; add time only if needed to push max again |
| Sessions/week     | Discussed in annual planning episode — not covered here |

## Why HIIT (30/15s, 40/20s) Does Not Develop VO2max in Trained Athletes

Short intermittent intervals like 30/15s or Tabata-style work are a different tool:

- Each 30-second work interval produces only ~10–15 seconds at truly maximal preload.
- Three sets of 8-minute HIIT (24 min total) → ~6 minutes of adaptive cardiac stimulus.
- Three 5-minute max VO2 intervals (15 min total) → ~10 minutes of adaptive cardiac stimulus.

HIIT also costs more kilojoules and uses larger motor units, requiring more recovery for less cardiac adaptation. It is useful for anaerobic capacity and motor unit recruitment — not for expanding the long-term VO2max ceiling in well-trained athletes.

> The Ronstadt 2020 study comparing 30/15s to 4×5-min intervals is frequently cited as evidence for HIIT superiority. The 4×5-min "control" group worked at ~72% of VO2max — below threshold for elite cyclists. The comparison was between HIIT vs. sub-threshold work, not HIIT vs. actual VO2max intervals.

## Decision Checklist Before Prescribing VO2max Work

1. Has the athlete **plateaued on FTP/threshold development**? If not, VO2max blocks may be premature.
2. Is the athlete **trained enough** that standard cadences no longer provide adequate preload stress?
3. Can the athlete commit to **going max by feel**, not by a power number?
4. Is **recovery volume adequate** to support the fatigue these sessions produce?
5. Is the **interval duration matched** to the individual (short enough to commit fully, long enough to reach preload)?

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake                              | Why it limits adaptation                                   |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fixed % FTP power target             | Misses real VO2max range for most trained athletes         |
| Low cadence during VO2max intervals  | Reduces muscle pump, lower preload and stroke volume       |
| Too many intervals or too long       | Fatigue degrades quality; 15–25 min total is sufficient    |
| Using HIIT for VO2max development    | Insufficient time at max preload per unit of fatigue cost  |
| Chasing all-time HR max              | Artificially constrains effort on good training days       |
| Giving up when power drops           | Power fading is expected and okay — keep perceived effort max |

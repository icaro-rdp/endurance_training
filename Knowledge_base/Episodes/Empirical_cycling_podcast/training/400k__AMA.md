# 400k AMA: Metabolic Signaling, Periodization Models, Pacing Dynamics & Nutrition — Q&A Guide
_Source: Empirical Cycling Podcast — Kolie Moore & Kyle Hanson_

---

## Q: Why Should Endurance Rides Be Paced with a Negative Split Rather Than Hammering Early?

**Context & Listener Inquiry:**
A listener asked why coaches recommend finishing an endurance ride harder than it started (negative splitting) instead of riding hard early when feeling fresh and fading toward the finish, and how this relates to total training volume.

**Mechanisms & Core Explanation:**
- **Lipolysis Kinetics & FFA Availability:** At the onset of exercise (first 20–30 minutes), adipose tissue lipolysis is still ramping up, and blood free fatty acid (FFA) concentrations are relatively low. Surging hard early forces heavy reliance on finite intramuscular glycogen stores.
- **Concentration Gradients:** As the ride progresses past 60 minutes, catecholamine-driven lipolysis elevates plasma FFA concentration, increasing passive substrate flux into muscle cells and maximizing fat oxidation rates.
- **Negative Decoupling:** In well-warmed, well-fueled athletes, power output can rise slightly during hours 2–4 at the same heart rate and RPE due to optimal muscle temperature, stable cardiac preload, and peak fat utilization.

**Coaching Rules & Practical Application:**
- **Pacing Structure:**
  - *Minutes 0–30:* Conservative start in Zone 1 / Low Zone 2 ($50\text{--}60\%$ FTP).
  - *Minutes 30–120+:* Settle into steady conversational Zone 2 ($65\text{--}72\%$ FTP, strictly below LT1).
  - *Final 30–60 min:* Allow power to drift upward to upper Zone 2 ($70\text{--}75\%$ FTP) only if RPE remains $\le 3\text{--}4/10$.
- Never hammer the opening 30 minutes of a 4-hour ride; early glycogen depletion forces a catastrophic pace collapse later.

## Q: When Should High-Intensity Intervals Be Placed in a Ride (Fresh vs. Late in the Session)?

**Context & Listener Inquiry:**
Listeners asked whether hard intervals (VO2max, threshold, sprints) should always be done at the start of a ride when fresh, or whether placing them late in a long endurance ride provides superior adaptation.

**Mechanisms & Core Explanation:**
- **Neuromuscular Intent & Power Ceiling:** For pure physiological adaptation (raising VO2max, expanding FTP, maximal motor unit recruitment), intervals require maximal neural drive and peak metabolic power, which are maximized when fresh after a proper warmup.
- **Durability & Late-Race Specificity:** Performing intervals after 2,000–3,000 kJ tests fatigue resistance and late-race repeatability, but absolute power output will be lower due to glycogen depletion, central fatigue, and elevated core temperature.
- **Cellular Signaling:** AMPK and mitochondrial biogenesis signaling are driven by high energy turnover and calcium flux; compromising interval power by inducing extreme pre-fatigue reduces the primary adaptive stimulus.

**Coaching Rules & Practical Application:**
- **For Capacity Building (Base & Build Phases):** Always place hard intervals at the **start of the ride** (after a 20-minute progressive warmup).
- **For Race Durability (Specialty Phase):** Place intervals late in long rides only when specifically preparing for multi-hour road race finishes, accepting lower power numbers as a targeted fatigue test.

## Q: Is Splitting Intervals Across Two Daily Sessions Equivalent to Continuous Time-in-Zone?

**Context & Listener Inquiry:**
An athlete asked whether executing $1 \times 20\text{ min}$ at threshold in the morning and another $1 \times 20\text{ min}$ in the evening provides the same training adaptation as a single $2 \times 20\text{ min}$ or continuous 40-minute threshold workout.

**Mechanisms & Core Explanation:**
- **Continuous Stimulus Accumulation:** Key intracellular signals for aerobic adaptation (calcium accumulation, AMPK activation, glycogen depletion, and metabolic byproduct accumulation) require continuous, un-interrupted time under tension.
- **Metabolic Reset:** Splitting work into two sessions separated by 8–10 hours allows complete clearance of metabolic intermediates, full resynthesis of intramuscular phosphocreatine ($PCr$) and ATP, and autonomic reset.
- **Loss of Overload:** A split $20\text{m} + 20\text{m}$ routine creates two minor, easily managed bouts rather than a singular progressive overload stimulus that challenges sub-threshold Time-to-Exhaustion (TTE).

**Coaching Rules & Practical Application:**
- Maintain interval continuity within a single session: execute $2 \times 20\text{ min}$ with 4–5 minutes of rest, or progress toward continuous $1 \times 40\text{ min}$.
- A brief 15–20 minute cafe stop during a long endurance ride preserves metabolic momentum; full multi-hour resets divide the day into two low-volume sessions.

## Q: What Are the Daily Protein Requirements for Endurance Athletes and Mitochondrial Biogenesis?

**Context & Listener Inquiry:**
Cyclists often focus exclusively on carbohydrate fueling and ask whether dietary protein intake matters for athletes who are not trying to build muscle bulk.

**Mechanisms & Core Explanation:**
- **Endurance Protein Synthesis Demands:** Endurance adaptations require continuous de novo protein synthesis:
  1. Mitochondrial enzyme synthesis (citrate synthase, cytochrome c oxidase).
  2. Monocarboxylate transport proteins (MCT1/MCT4 for the lactate shuttle).
  3. Angiogenesis and capillary endothelial growth factors (VEGF).
  4. Blood plasma expansion (hepatic albumin synthesis).
  5. Structural myofibrillar and connective tissue repair.
- **The 1.5 g/kg Baseline:** Inadequate protein ($<1.0\text{ g/kg/day}$) impairs systemic recovery, increases lean tissue breakdown during high-volume blocks, and limits mitochondrial remodeling.

**Coaching Rules & Practical Application:**
- Target **$1.5\text{ to }1.8\text{ grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day}$** ($1.5\text{ g/kg/day}$).
- Distribute protein across 3–4 meals daily (approx. $0.3\text{--}0.4\text{ g/kg per meal}$), including a high-quality protein and carbohydrate feeding immediately post-ride.

## Q: How Should Strength Training and High-Intensity Cycling Be Sequenced on the Same Day?

**Context & Listener Inquiry:**
Athletes balancing heavy strength training with cycling intervals ask which modality should be performed first when training twice in a single day.

**Mechanisms & Core Explanation:**
- **Central Nervous System (CNS) Freshness:** Cycling intervals (VO2max, threshold, sprints) require high neuromuscular firing rates, precise motor coordination, and full glycogen stores. Lifting heavy weights beforehand induces central and peripheral fatigue that degrades cycling power output.
- **Gym Adaptations Under Fatigue:** Heavy compound lifting (squats, deadlifts) targets high-threshold motor unit recruitment and neural rate of force development (RFD). Performing gym work in the afternoon after morning cycling retains high neural stimulus while protecting bike quality.

**Coaching Rules & Practical Application:**

```
Optimal Double-Day Sequencing:
- Morning (Primary):  On-bike sprinting or high-intensity intervals (freshest state).
- Midday:             Substantial recovery meal (carbohydrates + protein) & 2-4 hr rest.
- Evening (Secondary): Heavy gym session (squats, trap bar deadlifts, split squats).
```

- **Trap Bar vs. Conventional Deadlifts:** Use **trap bar (hex bar) deadlifts** rather than straight-bar conventional deadlifts to reduce lumbar spine shear forces and match cycling hip kinematics.

## Q: Why Do Athletes Feel Sluggish After a Rest Week and How Should Openers Be Structured?

**Context & Listener Inquiry:**
Riders exiting a 5–7 day recovery week often report heavy, sluggish legs and "flatness" when attempting their first hard workout or race, asking if they rested too much.

**Mechanisms & Core Explanation:**
- **Autonomic Downshift:** During extended rest, parasympathetic tone becomes dominant, resting blood pressure and plasma volume stabilize, catecholamine release decreases, and neuromuscular motor unit excitability temporarily declines.
- **The Opener Activation:** A short, structured "energy system round-robin" activation ride stimulates sympathetic drive, elevates muscle temperature, and primes glycolytic and oxidative pathways without inducing fatigue.

**Coaching Rules & Practical Application:**

```
Opener Protocol (45-60 min total):
- 15 min: Progressive Zone 2 warmup.
- 5 min:  Zone 3 / Tempo cadence roll (90-95 RPM).
- 3 min:  FTP / Threshold effort (smooth, rhythmic).
- 2 min:  VO2max effort (breathing open, 105+ RPM).
- 5 min:  Easy recovery spin.
- 2-3 x 10-second maximal sprints from cruising speed (3 min recovery between).
- 10 min: Cool-down spin.
```

- Execute this opener 24 hours prior to a key race or testing session to eliminate sluggishness.
- Openers are a "privilege for the well-rested"; never perform openers if already fatigued.

## Q: Does Fasted / Low-Carb Training Enhance Fat Oxidation Without Impairing Glycolytic Performance?

**Context & Listener Inquiry:**
Athletes ask whether chronic fasted riding or ketogenic diets are necessary to maximize fat burning capacity for long endurance events.

**Mechanisms & Core Explanation:**
- **Training vs. Dietary Fat Adaptation:** Endurance training volume naturally expands mitochondrial density and fat oxidation capacity while preserving full glycolytic enzyme function.
- **The Downregulation of Pyruvate Dehydrogenase:** Chronic carbohydrate restriction downregulates pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH), crippling the body's ability to burn carbohydrates at high intensities and destroying top-end VO2max, threshold power, and sprinting ability.

**Coaching Rules & Practical Application:**
- Fuel all high-intensity interval and threshold sessions with ample carbohydrates ($60\text{--}90+\text{ g/hr}$).
- Rely on long, sub-threshold Zone 2 volume to build fat oxidation pathways naturally without compromising glycolytic performance.

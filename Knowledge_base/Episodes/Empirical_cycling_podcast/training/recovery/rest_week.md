# Rest Week — How Long Recovery Should Be

_Source: Empirical Cycling Podcast_

---

## What Is a Rest Week?

A rest week is a planned reduction in training load that gives the body enough time to recover from accumulated stress.

The basic idea is simple: training creates the stimulus, but adaptation happens during recovery. A rest week is the period where fatigue drops, the athlete repairs, and fitness can actually show up.

Traditionally, people talk about three hard weeks followed by a lighter week, but the real rule is not the calendar. The rule is that the more stress you accumulate, the more recovery you need.

## When Do You Take a Rest Week?

You take a rest week when the current load stops being productive.

That can happen after a few hard days, after a full block, or after a longer stretch of training depending on the athlete and the block. The practical trigger is not "week four" by default. It is the point where accumulated stress is high enough that another hard session would mostly add fatigue rather than adaptation.

## How to Think About It

Traditional training models often place a rest week after several weeks of rising load. That can work, but the exact length should not be treated as fixed.

| Factor                      | What it suggests                               |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Lower training load         | Shorter recovery may be enough                 |
| Higher accumulated stress   | Longer recovery is needed                      |
| Harder block, like VO2 work | More rest than a threshold or sweet spot block |
| Deeper training history     | Recovery may need to be more individualized    |

The key principle is that the more stress you accumulate, the more rest you need.

Recovery is not separate from training progression. It is part of the training dose.

## Common Mistake

One of the most common errors is shortening the rest week too much.

Examples:

- finishing a hard block on Sunday, taking Monday off, then doing endurance, group rides, and intervals again by Friday
- taking only two or three easy days after a VO2 block and testing too early

That is not a real recovery week. It is just a brief interruption in training.

Another mistake is assuming that a rest week always means the same thing for every block. A VO2 block tends to require more recovery than a threshold or sweet spot block because the physiological cost is deeper.

## How Long Should It Be?

There is no single universal answer.

| Situation                            | Typical rest length      |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------ |
| Lower-volume athlete                 | A few days may be enough |
| Moderate accumulated stress          | Around 4 to 7 days       |
| Hard VO2 block or overreaching block | Often longer than a week |

The right duration depends on:

- training load
- workout type
- training history
- how the athlete is actually recovering

It can even be longer than 7 days when the accumulated stress is high enough. The term "week" is convenient, not sacred.

## How Long Should You Rest Between Workouts?

A rest week is just the large-scale version of the same principle.

If you do a hard FTP session at 1:00 pm, you usually cannot do the same session again at 5:00 pm. The same is true after heavy squats: if the set was truly hard, it is not repeatable immediately.

That analogy matters because it helps frame recovery as a real biological process, not a motivational one. The system needs time to restore itself before it can absorb more stress.

## What to Use to Judge Recovery

### Subjective signals

- daily or weekly feedback from the athlete
- overall feeling
- readiness to train
- how fresh the athlete feels going into key sessions

### Objective signals

- chronic training load
- HRV trends
- resting heart rate

TrainingPeaks-style chronic training load is useful because it gives some sense of the accumulated fitness and stress context, but it still does not tell the whole story.

Objective data can help, but it still needs interpretation. HRV may trend down during a hard block, which can be useful. But it can also behave unexpectedly, so it should not be the only decision-maker.

In the episode, one useful warning is that HRV can sometimes improve even as fatigue is mounting. So a good-looking HRV number does not automatically mean the athlete is fresh enough to keep pushing.

| Signal             | How to use it                                 |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| HRV                | Watch for trend, not a single number          |
| Resting heart rate | Helpful for context, not a standalone answer  |
| Athlete feedback   | Often the most important practical signal     |
| Training history   | Essential for judging how much rest is needed |

## How Training History Changes the Answer

Athletes with more consistent training and better tolerance for overload may be able to handle a longer block before resting.

Less experienced athletes often need shorter blocks and shorter rest periods, but that does not mean they always need more stimulus. Sometimes they are simply limited by recovery capacity.

As the athlete becomes more experienced, you can sometimes:

- extend the block
- add a third week of overload
- add more double-session days

But every increase in stimulus also increases the rest needed afterward.

That is why communication matters so much. The athlete with more training history may tolerate longer overload, but the coach still needs daily or weekly feedback to decide whether to continue or stop the block.

## When to End the Block

End the hard block and rest when:

- fatigue is accumulating too far
- HRV and resting heart rate trend the wrong way for too long
- the athlete reports feeling run down
- the next hard session is clearly not going to be productive
- recovery is no longer keeping pace with the training stress

In practice, this may show up as a decision like: "This third week is turning into too much, so let's cut it to two sessions, or end the block now and rest."

The point is not to squeeze one more workout into the block. The point is to recover enough to absorb the work already done.

## Analogy for Recovery

Think about heavy squats.

If a set of 3 to 5 by 5 is all you had in the tank, you probably cannot do it again an hour later, and probably not the next day either.

Cycling training works the same way. A hard FTP session at 1:00 pm is not usually repeatable at 5:00 pm. Recovery time matters because the system needs time to restore itself.

## Pros and Cons Summary

**Pros**

- Helps absorb prior training instead of just piling on more stress
- Reduces the chance of carrying fatigue into the next block
- Can reveal whether performance was limited by stimulus or recovery
- Supports better long-term progression

**Cons**

- Easy to underdo it and return to hard training too soon
- Easy to overinterpret data like HRV without context
- A short break is not always enough after a very hard block
- If misjudged, the athlete may start the next block still fatigued

## Chapter Takeaway

The chapter structure of the episode points to one main message: rest weeks are not a fixed template.

They depend on:

- the size of the stimulus that just happened
- how deep the block went into physiology
- the athlete's training history
- what the objective and subjective recovery signals are saying

If the athlete is not recovered, the next hard session is not productive, even if it looks good on paper.

## Decision Checklist

1. How much accumulated stress is the athlete carrying?
2. What type of block just ended?
3. Does training history suggest the athlete can recover quickly or slowly?
4. What do HRV, resting heart rate, and daily feedback say together?
5. Is the athlete actually ready for another round of quality work?

If the answer is unclear, err on the side of more recovery rather than less. A real rest week should restore readiness, not just interrupt training for a couple of days.

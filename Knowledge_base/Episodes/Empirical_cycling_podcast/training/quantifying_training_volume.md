# Quantifying Training Volume & Load: Hours vs. Kilojoules vs. TSS — Complete Guide

_Source: Empirical Cycling Podcast — Kolie Moore & Marinus Petersen_

---

## Core Problem: How to Measure Training Volume

Training volume is universally acknowledged as a primary driver of endurance performance, yet coaches and athletes frequently debate the best metric to quantify it. 

The debate centers on three primary tools for measuring training load:
1. **Total Hours:** Measuring time spent on the bike.
2. **Kilojoules (kJ):** Measuring total mechanical work performed.
3. **Training Stress Score (TSS):** Weighting duration by relative intensity ($IF^2 \times \text{hours} \times 100$).

While total hours remains the practical standard for time management and pacing discipline, mechanical work (Kilojoules) directly reflects the bioenergetic stimulus for sub-LT1 adaptations. Meanwhile, TSS attempts to integrate high and low intensities into a single score, but introduces major assumptions and potential failure modes.

> **Core Insight:** Cellular adaptation pathways do not care about time spent sitting on a saddle with a GPS unit running. They respond to cellular energy flux (ATP/ADP/AMP ratio), calcium signaling, and mechanical strain.

---

## Physiological Mechanisms of Sub-LT1 Adaptation

Sub-LT1 (below the first lactate threshold / aerobic threshold) training drives peripheral aerobic adaptations through several interconnected signaling pathways:

- **AMPK Activation:** Driven by ATP breakdown and elevated AMP/ADP ratios during energy expenditure.
- **CaMK (Calcium/Calmodulin-dependent Kinase):** Triggered by continuous intracellular calcium release during muscle contractions.
- **Mitochondrial Biogenesis & Enzymes:** Increases in Citrate Synthase and HAD (3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase) activity.
- **Vascular Adaptations:** Capillary shear stress and hypoxia-inducible factors promoting angiogenesis and blood flow capacity.

For sub-LT1 endurance riding, the total magnitude of these signals is directly proportional to **total mechanical work performed (Kilojoules)**, rather than calendar time alone.

---

## Metric 1: Total Training Hours

Measuring volume purely by hours is the most common approach in endurance sports.

### Practical Role
- **Fatigue Resistance:** In coaching data, total hours ridden sub-LT1 has the strongest practical correlation with long-term fatigue resistance.
- **Pacing & Intensity Discipline:** Structuring workouts by hours prevents athletes from trying to "compress" volume by riding too hard.

### Pros
- **Simplicity:** Extremely easy to understand, plan, and schedule around daily life.
- **Prevents Over-intensity:** Discourages athletes from riding at high intensity on base days just to complete a volume target faster.
- **Sacred Recovery Days:** Helps maintain clear boundaries between easy recovery rides and long endurance rides.

### Cons
- **Ignores Work Rate:** Treats an hour at 140W identically to an hour at 220W, despite significantly different energy expenditures.
- **Arbitrary Thresholds:** Creates fuzzy boundaries about what minimum intensity "counts" as training time (e.g., does 30W or walking count?).
- **Recovery Erosion:** Can tempt athletes to blindly add low-quality hours to recovery days to hit weekly hour goals, leading to burnout.

---

## Metric 2: Kilojoules (kJ) — Total Mechanical Work

Kilojoules measures absolute mechanical energy output ($1 \text{ kJ} \approx 1 \text{ kcal}$ burned at ~22–25% human metabolic efficiency).

### Practical Role
For all sub-LT1 endurance work, total Kilojoules represents the actual metabolic and cellular load imposed on the body.

> **The Sub-LT1 Low-Fatigue Strategy:** Achieving a target kJ count by riding **longer and easier** generates less neuromuscular fatigue and autonomic disturbance than riding **shorter and closer to LT1**.

### Advantages of Riding Easier for the Same Kilojoules
1. **Lower Neuromuscular & Autonomic Fatigue:** Riding well below LT1 preserves fresh legs and autonomic balance for high-intensity interval days.
2. **Fueling & Gut Capacity Alignment:** At lower power (e.g., 200W vs. 300W), carbohydrate burn rate is closer to maximum gut absorption capacity (60–90g/hr), minimizing net glycogen depletion.
3. **Repeatability:** Allows athletes to execute high kJ totals back-to-back without accumulating chronic systemic fatigue.

### Pros
- **Physiologically Grounded:** Directly reflects energy expenditure and homeostatic disturbance.
- **Objective Quantification:** Completely independent of arbitrary zone boundaries or shifting FTP estimates.
- **Effective Recovery Caps:** Ideal for setting hard upper limits on recovery rides (e.g., capping recovery rides at 600–700 kJ).

### Cons
- **Harder to Plan Mentally:** Athletes find "go do 3,000 kJ" less intuitive than "go ride 4 hours."
- **Strictly Sub-LT1 Applicable:** Cannot compare kJ across threshold or VO2max efforts ($1,000\text{ kJ}$ above threshold causes drastically higher strain than $1,000\text{ kJ}$ below LT1).
- **Risk of Misinterpretation:** If athletes attempt to hit kJ targets faster by riding harder, they cross LT1 and ruin the endurance stimulus.

## Metric 3: Training Stress Score (TSS) & PMC

TSS scores workouts using Normalized Power (NP) relative to Functional Threshold Power (FTP):
$$\text{TSS} = \left(\frac{\text{sec} \times \text{NP} \times \text{IF}}{3600 \times \text{FTP}}\right) \times 100$$

### Pros
- **Multi-Intensity Integration:** Attempts to combine high-intensity and low-intensity rides into a single scalar number.
- **Long-Term Load Tracking:** Chronic Training Load (CTL) provides a rough historical benchmark of overall training volume when training composition is consistent.
- **Recovery Session Benchmark:** Useful for defining true active recovery (e.g., keeping recovery rides under 15 TSS or single digits).

### Cons
- **Ignores LT1:** TSS centers entirely on FTP (LT2). It ignores individual variation in LT1 location (which ranges from 50% to 85%+ of FTP).
- **Relies on Normalized Power:** NP uses a 4th-power weighted average that can distort stochastic rides and interval sessions.
- **False Equivalence:** Equates high-intensity short rides with long low-intensity rides in ways that break down physiologically.
- **Unreliable TSB:** Training Stress Balance (TSB) frequently fails to reflect actual leg freshness or readiness to perform.
- **Incentivizes High Intensity:** Athletes under volume constraints may substitute sweetspot or threshold work to chase high TSS, causing premature fatigue.

## Comparison of Volume Quantification Metrics

| Metric | Primary Foundation | Sub-LT1 Validity | Best Use Case | Major Limitation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Total Hours** | Duration / Calendar time | High (with RPE discipline) | Weekly planning, time management, pacing guardrails | Treats all power outputs within a ride identically |
| **Kilojoules (kJ)** | Absolute mechanical work done | Exceptional (Gold standard for sub-LT1) | Quantifying true metabolic load, setting recovery caps | Non-transferable to super-LT1 / high-intensity work |
| **TSS / CTL** | Relative intensity vs FTP ($IF^2$) | Moderate to Low | Long-term macro-tracking of mixed training blocks | Ignores LT1; distorts fatigue via NP algorithms |

## LT1 Dynamics, Fatigue, and Motor Unit Recruitment

### 1. LT1 Drift Over Duration
LT1 is not a fixed, immutable watt number during a long ride. Research (e.g., Sprague et al.) demonstrates that **LT1 power degrades by ~10% after 2.5 hours** of continuous riding.
- *Practical Impact:* A power target that starts at 85% of fresh LT1 (e.g., 230W) may cross above the degraded LT1 after 3 hours, shifting the metabolic state and elevating autonomic stress.

### 2. Monitoring LT1 in the Field
- **Heart Rate vs. Lactate:** Blood lactate testing in the field is noisy. Heart rate at LT1 is significantly more stable day-to-day than power or lactate.
- **Heart Rate Ceiling Rule:** Establish LT1 heart rate and set a strict ceiling 5–10 bpm below it (e.g., if LT1 HR is 145 bpm, target 125–135 bpm).
- **The Talk / Sing / Snack Test:** LT1 is the maximum intensity where an athlete can hold a conversational business meeting or eat dry food without choking on elevated ventilation.

### 3. Motor Unit Recruitment & Duration
- As an endurance ride extends beyond 2–3 hours, slow-twitch (Type 1) motor units fatigue, forcing the central nervous system to recruit larger motor units (Type 2 fast-twitch fibers) even at constant sub-LT1 power.
- **Time-Crunched vs. High-Volume:** 
  - Time-restricted athletes (2–3 hr max) can raise intensity closer to LT1 to recruit larger motor units.
  - High-volume athletes achieve full motor unit recruitment naturally through long duration at lower intensity, avoiding unnecessary autonomic stress.

### 4. Adaptability of Fast-Twitch Fibers
Endurance-trained fast-twitch fibers develop high mitochondrial density and oxidative enzyme capacity (Citrate Synthase, HAD), functioning effectively as high-capacity fat oxidation units without excessive lactate accumulation.

## Practical Application & Coaching Prescriptions

### Sub-LT1 Prescription Rules
1. **Target Power Range:** Prescribe base endurance rides at **60–80% of LT1 power** (or 50–70% of FTP).
2. **Pacing Strategy:** Negative split long rides. Start hour 1 conservative to account for cardiac drift and LT1 degradation later in the session.
3. **Volume Progression Rules:**
   - **Well-Trained Athletes:** Ramp volume by **1.5 to 2.0 hours per week**.
   - **Beginners / Rebuilding Athletes:** Ramp volume by **0.5 to 1.0 hour per week**.
   - **Time-Restricted Progression:** Extend ride duration before attempting to raise endurance ride power.

## Handling Special Situations

### Life Stress & Volume Adjustments
- **Never Add Intensity to High Life Stress:** When external life stress is high, adding high-intensity intervals causes negative recovery spirals.
- **Keep Easy Rides Easy:** Low-intensity spinning provides mental relief and maintains aerobic throughput without overwhelming the nervous system.

### Tapering & Race Preparation
- **Highly Individual:** Taper strategy varies drastically by athlete. While some require sharp volume reductions, elite/well-trained athletes often perform best with high volume maintained right up to race day.
- **Quality Over Rest:** Freshness is a path function, not just a state function. Maintaining routine low-fatigue volume preserves muscle tone and metabolic readiness.

### Non-Exercise Physical Activity (NEPA)
- Low-grade daily activity (walking, stairs, light under-desk pedaling) imposes zero minimum threshold to be effective. 
- Accumulating consistent low-level movement (e.g., an extra 10–15 min/day = 50+ hours/year) provides meaningful baseline aerobic support and metabolic health benefits without adding training stress.

## Pros and Cons Summary

**Pros**

- **Clear Framework:** Differentiates the unique roles of Hours (planning/pacing), Kilojoules (cellular load), and TSS (macro-tracking).
- **Low-Fatigue Volume Strategy:** Shows how riding easier for longer maximizes metabolic work while sparing legs for interval days.
- **Physiological Realism:** Accounts for LT1 decay over duration and progressive motor unit recruitment.
- **Practical Field Tests:** Replaces complex lab testing with reliable field markers (Talk Test, HR ceilings, RPE).

**Cons**

- **Metric Confusion:** Athletes can easily confuse volume metrics and default to chasing TSS or compressing kJ into high-intensity sessions.
- **Non-Linear Adaptations:** Volume progressions must be adapted dynamically to individual recovery rates rather than strict linear rules.
- **Single-Metric Blindness:** Over-relying on any single metric (Hours, kJ, or TSS) misses key aspects of athlete fatigue and readiness.

---

## Decision Checklist For Volume Quantification

1. **Is the session sub-LT1 endurance work?**
   - Use **Kilojoules** to measure total metabolic work done.
   - Use **Hours** to schedule duration and enforce pacing discipline.
2. **Is the athlete time-restricted (≤ 6–8 hrs/week)?**
   - Ride closer to LT1 to maximize kJ per unit time and recruit larger motor units within shorter windows.
3. **Is the athlete high-volume (15–25+ hrs/week)?**
   - Cap intensity well below LT1 (60–70% of LT1) to maximize total kJ while minimizing autonomic fatigue.
4. **Is the athlete preparing for high-intensity intervals tomorrow?**
   - Ensure endurance/recovery riding stays below the LT1 HR ceiling (5–10 bpm below LT1) and keep recovery rides capped (e.g. <700 kJ or <15 TSS).
5. **Is life stress elevated?**
   - Reduce or eliminate high-intensity work; maintain easy sub-LT1 volume for mental and physical maintenance.

# Over/Unders — Complete Guide

_Source: Empirical Cycling Podcast — Kelly Moore & James McKay (Episodes 43–44)_

---

## What Are Over/Unders?

Over/unders are structured intervals that alternate between a power target **above** threshold ("overs") and a recovery target **below** threshold ("unders"). The overs recruit large motor units and briefly stress the anaerobic system; the unders let you recover enough to repeat the over without blowing up.

**Key principle:** the goal is maximizing time in zone — overs should be hard but manageable, and unders should allow enough recovery to reach the next over at quality. If you fail the interval and have to pedal Zone 2 for the last 10 minutes, you're not getting the stimulus.

---

## Do Over/Unders Have Unique Physiological Benefits?

**Mostly no.** The commonly cited advantages — superior lactate shuttling, better lactate tolerance, higher MCT transporter expression — are theoretical. No direct study has compared them head-to-head against steady-state threshold work for these outcomes.

What they do offer:
- **Race-specificity:** practice accelerating and recovering while fatigued — directly useful for crits, cyclocross, gravel, and peloton chasing
- **Large motor unit recruitment** during the overs
- **Variety** and mental engagement on the trainer
- **Equivalent aerobic stimulus** to steady-state threshold, delivered differently

> If steady-state intervals work well for you, there is no physiological imperative to do over/unders. They're a tool, not a requirement.

## Power Targets

| Phase  | Target                                               |
| ------ | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Unders | Sweet spot: ~90% FTP (or LT1 + 10–20 W for tempo variants) |
| Overs  | 20–50 W above threshold — "hard but sustainable"     |

**Avoid prescribing overs at 120% FTP.** That misinterpretation conflates VO2max with anaerobic threshold. For most athletes, 20–50 W over FTP is plenty.

**For beginners:** don't assign a power target for the overs — just accelerate. Raise cadence to 110–120 RPM for 10 seconds and return to sweet spot. This reveals how hard someone naturally pushes and how well they recover without the risk of going all-out.

> When in doubt, lowball the overs slightly. A sweet spot workout with mild surges is still a high-quality session. Blowing up and coasting is not.

## Workout Formats

### Beginner (learning pacing & acceleration)

- **Format:** 20 min sweet spot + 10-second acceleration every 5 min
- **Sets:** 2–4 × 15 min
- **Over intensity:** 40–50% of a full sprint — or just raise cadence 20–30 RPM
- **Always start each interval with an over**, not an under (race-specific habit)

### Bread-and-Butter (most common prescription)

| Ratio    | Format                              | Notes                             |
| -------- | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| 1:4      | 30 s over / 2 min under             | Standard entry point              |
| 1:4      | 1 min over / 4 min under            | Step up once 30/2 is comfortable  |
| To feel  | "1 min over / 4 min under, by feel" | For athletes who know their range |

Typical progressions: 2–4 × 15 min → 3 × 20–25 min → 3 × 30 min → 3 × 32–48 min

### Advanced (threshold base)

- Run the unders at threshold instead of sweet spot
- Only for athletes with a high, well-established threshold TTE
- Not commonly prescribed; reserve for well-trained athletes

---

## Progression

Think of over/unders as **threshold work** — they should be progressed like threshold intervals.

**Primary lever:** add interval time per session, not more sets.

| Stage      | Target volume per session                        |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Early      | 2–4 × 15 min (30–60 min total)                   |
| Building   | 3 × 20–25 min (60–75 min total)                  |
| Advanced   | 3–4 × 30 min (90–120 min total)                  |

**Stall signals:** if you can't progress interval duration — check for cumulative fatigue, insufficient fueling, or overs that are too hard.

---

## When to Use Over/Unders

### Good use cases

- **Athletes who need to practice accelerating** — crits, cyclocross, gravel holeshots, peloton surges
- **Athletes who struggle with steady-state intervals** — variety reduces the dropout rate and keeps quality up
- **As a threshold block substitute** — one over/under session can replace a steady-state threshold session week-to-week
- **Race-prep specificity** — match the over duration to the effort you'll face on race day
- **Motor unit recruitment emphasis** — the overs recruit large, type II fibers repeatedly over threshold durations

### Not necessary for

- **Athletes who respond well to steady state** — no physiological reason to switch
- **TTE development** — steady-state threshold is equally effective (and often more direct)
- **Lactate clearance** — all aerobic work builds mitochondrial mass; 6-hour endurance rides and VO2max intervals are actually better for this

---

## Fitting Over/Unders Into a Training Week

- **Substitute** a scheduled steady-state threshold session — do not add them on top of existing intensity
- **One over/under day + one or two steady-state threshold days** per week is a solid structure for a threshold block
- Use threshold durations: sessions under 10–15 minutes of time-in-zone are too short to matter

## Cadence Variants (Track, Cross, Sprint-Specific)

For track cyclists, cyclocross, and crit racers, cadence targets can replace or supplement power targets:

| Level       | Cadence target on overs             |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Beginner    | Raise 20–30 RPM above base cadence  |
| Intermediate | 100–110 RPM                        |
| Advanced    | 130–160 RPM                        |

For cross/mountain bike athletes: start each interval **standing** and clipped in, simulating a race start acceleration.

---

## Anaerobic Capacity Over/Unders (Different Goal)

For athletes who want to target true anaerobic capacity — not the main over/under protocol.

- **Format:** 15/15s, 30/30s, or 20/20s — all-out overs, easy unders
- **Target feeling:** legs can barely contract by the end of each set
- **Volume:** 4–5 minutes per set, 2–4 sets maximum
- **Cost:** very high glycogen depletion and fatigue — do not combine with threshold work the same day

> These are a different tool for a different purpose. Standard over/unders are threshold training. Anaerobic capacity intervals are a separate stimulus entirely.

## Key Metrics to Track Progress

| Metric                            | What it tells you                                            |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Interval duration**             | Primary marker — can you sustain quality for longer?         |
| **RPE at target power**           | Same effort feeling easier = aerobic adaptation              |
| **Recovery quality during unders** | Recovering faster before each over = good sign              |
| **Race performance**              | Did over/under training solve the specific race limiter?     |

---

## Decision Checklist Before Prescribing Over/Unders

1. Does this athlete need to **practice accelerating and recovering** (race-specific need)?
2. Does steady-state training leave quality on the table — **boredom or adherence issues**?
3. Is there **enough time per session** to reach threshold-range durations?
4. Does this **replace** an existing threshold session (not add to it)?
5. Are the overs set at a **sustainable intensity** — not a blowup risk?

If yes to 1 or 2, and the other conditions are met → prescribe them. If steady state is working fine → no change needed. If the session is too short for meaningful time-in-zone → go straight threshold.

# FTP (Threshold) Training — Complete Guide

_Source: Empirical Cycling Podcast — Kolie Moore, Fabiano & Erica_

---

## What Is FTP?

FTP (Functional Threshold Power) is the highest power you can sustain in a quasi-steady state for a prolonged effort — physiologically approximated by maximal lactate steady state. It is **not** defined by any fixed test formula; it is what it is when you ride at it.

**The best test is the thing itself:** ride at FTP and you will know. Experienced athletes can find their FTP within ~5 watts in 10 minutes by feel. The best predictor of performance is performance.

> If your FTP feels wrong, check in order: fatigue → nutrition → training history → test validity. FTP does not change day-to-day. If it drops mid-week, that is fatigue, not detraining.

---

## How to Find Your FTP

| Method          | Value                                                                       |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Riding at it    | Start conservative, build to sustainable effort — most reliable             |
| 20-min test     | ~90–95% of best 20-min power (individual — not fixed at 95%)               |
| RPE             | 7–8 out of 10; hard but controllable; feel it in the legs, not the lungs   |
| Breathing       | Labored but not ragged; respiration rate should not run away                |
| Talk test       | Fragments only; complete sentences impossible                               |
| Muscle feel     | Level of burning you can sustain for 2×20 min = likely FTP                 |

> **Avoid extrapolating.** Building sweet spot to 90 min does not tell you where your FTP is. A 2×8 test massively overestimates due to anaerobic contribution. Ramp tests and other short-duration estimates are useful starting points — not measurements.
>
> **On lactate testing:** blood lactate at 4 mmol/L is a population average, not your threshold. Lactate tests frequently overestimate FTP, are expensive to repeat, and add definitional error where none is needed. If you can sustain a power and recover normally from it, you don't need a lab to validate it.

## How Long Can You Hold It? (TTE at FTP)

TTE at FTP is **highly individual** and one of the primary adaptation markers from threshold training:

| Training Level     | FTP TTE                      |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| Untrained / new    | 20–30 min                    |
| Moderately trained | 40–60 min                    |
| Well trained       | 60–75 min                    |
| Elite              | 75–90+ min                   |

**Key principle:** TTE extends meaningfully with threshold training and training age. When FTP stops increasing, TTE is the next lever — and typically still responds well to continued work.

> After a big FTP bump (10–20 W), TTE may drop temporarily and needs to be rebuilt. RPE will also feel disjointed until you adapt to the new wattage — this is normal.

## When to Use FTP Training in a Training Plan

### Good use cases

- **Early season** — produces FTP gains relatively quickly at a manageable fatigue cost
- **When sweet spot TTE has become very long** — 90+ min of sweet spot is the natural signal to move up to threshold
- **Time-crunched athletes with only 1 hour** — more stimulus per unit time than sweet spot
- **Maintenance of threshold fitness** — even 1 session/week preserves FTP through a mixed block
- **After a VO2 block** — threshold work consolidates a ceiling raised by VO2max intervals
- **Athletes needing TT, road race, or time-limited criterium specificity**

### Poor use cases

- **During a heavy race schedule** — recovery demands crowd out adaptation; results are unpredictable
- **When aerobic base is insufficient** — poor aerobic foundation slows recovery between reps and degrades interval quality
- **When FTP has stalled for weeks** — at that point, VO2 work (raising the "roof") is the correct tool, not more threshold
- **As the only training modality** — must coexist with VO2 work and volume for continued gains
- **Indoors without adequate cooling** — if ambient temp is 65–75°F+ without strong fans, sweet spot is more realistic; FTP indoors may require a 10–15 W reduction

## How to Prescribe It

### Session structure

- **Minimum useful interval length:** 12 min; build toward 20 min as a baseline. Shorter intervals risk drifting into VO2 territory and defeat the purpose.
- **Practical formats:**
  - 2×20, 3×15, 3×20, 4×15 — build time-in-zone progressively
  - Over/unders with FTP as the "over" target
  - Add reps or time *after* completing the prescription if legs feel good — never burn the match on the first rep
- **Pacing rule:** start 5–10 watts below target for the first rep. Feel the legs. Build. The last interval can be all-in or extended. Fading across reps signals over-pacing or fatigue, not low fitness.
- **Outdoor intervals:** average and normalized power within ~1 watt of each other is an excellent interval. ERG-perfect steadiness is not required outside. Learn to deliver power on climbs, flats, and rolling terrain — quality is measured by what you can hold on average, not moment-to-moment.

### Weekly volume / session progression

Think **per-session TTE**, not per-week totals. Progressive overload = adding time-in-zone per session across weeks. An FTP plateau after increasing reps and duration is the signal to stop adding threshold volume and shift toward VO2 work.

Early season gains may be 15–20 W in 4–6 weeks. Mid-season gains are 5–10 W over longer blocks. Late season may mean no power gain but improving TTE — this still represents meaningful adaptation.

### Frequency guidelines

| Frequency          | Notes                                                         |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1×/week            | Minimum to drive adaptation; works well in a mixed plan       |
| 2×/week            | Standard threshold focus; monitor recovery closely            |
| 3×/week            | High fatigue risk; only in a dedicated block with short reps  |

## Key Metrics to Track Progress

| Metric                         | What it tells you                                                  |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **TTE at FTP**                 | Primary threshold marker — if it goes up, adaptation is happening  |
| **RPE at target power**        | Same watts feeling easier = real adaptation                        |
| **Power drift rep-to-rep**     | Fading signals over-pacing or accumulated fatigue                  |
| **FTP (every 4–8 weeks)**      | Validates whether training is raising the number or just TTE       |

> FTP is also a reliable **fatigue marker** mid-block. If FTP sessions feel 15–20% harder than expected, that is fatigue — not detraining. Dropping to sweet spot for a session is often the right call.
>
> If TTE is rising but FTP isn't → stay the course; a power jump often follows.
> If both are stalled for 3–4 sessions → add VO2 work.

## Fueling FTP Sessions

FTP intervals are a high-kJ effort. Fueling quality directly determines whether adaptation happens.

| Priority                         | Recommendation                                                                               |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Muscle glycogen (day before)** | Most important — eat adequate carbs 24–48 h before                                          |
| **On-bike fueling**              | Critical for sessions >60 min; begin fueling from the first 20–30 min on very hard sessions |
| **Morning sessions**             | Hydration is as critical as food — 8 hours without drinking is a meaningful deficit         |
| **Post-session recovery**        | Fueling during the ride accelerates next-day recovery vs. eating everything afterward        |

> Skipping post-workout fueling after an FTP session and attempting a second hard session the next day is one of the most common reasons a block "doesn't work."

## FTP Training vs. Other Intensities

| Intensity          | Relative fatigue | Time to exhaust well-trained | Best for                                          |
| ------------------ | ---------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Endurance          | Low              | Hours                        | Base, recovery, volume                            |
| Sweet spot         | Moderate         | 1.5–3 hr                     | Muscular endurance, early season, maintenance     |
| **Threshold (FTP)**| **Moderate-high**| **30–75 min**                | **FTP development, time-efficient**               |
| VO2max             | High             | 6–15 min                     | Ceiling raising, anaerobic capacity               |

## Pros and Cons Summary

**Pros**

- Most time-efficient intensity for FTP improvement
- TTE advances even when power plateaus — continued adaptation signal
- Doubles as a fatigue marker mid-block
- Works well indoors when cooling is adequate
- Interchangeable with sweet spot in many contexts — pick whichever is achievable on the day

**Cons**

- Fatigue accumulates faster than it appears; recovery demands are real
- Short intervals risk drifting to VO2 territory if not carefully managed
- Diminishing returns without VO2 work — VO2max is the "roof" that limits FTP's ceiling
- Terrain and environment affect pacing; FTP feel on trainer ≠ FTP outdoors for many athletes
- Building FTP during a heavy race schedule is rarely productive

---

## Decision Checklist Before Prescribing FTP Training

1. Is the athlete **sufficiently rested** to accurately express their FTP and calibrate RPE?
2. Do they have **adequate session time** — at least 30–45 min available for meaningful TTE work?
3. Is their **aerobic foundation** sufficient for recovery between reps?
4. Is **fueling** accounted for — the night before, morning of, and on-bike?
5. Are they in a **training phase** (vs. race-heavy block) where adaptation is realistic?
6. Has their **FTP stalled** for weeks, suggesting VO2 work is the correct tool instead?

If all six are clear → prescribe it. If the athlete is racing heavily → maintain, don't build. If FTP has stalled → VO2 is the tool. If session time is very short → threshold over sweet spot for efficiency.

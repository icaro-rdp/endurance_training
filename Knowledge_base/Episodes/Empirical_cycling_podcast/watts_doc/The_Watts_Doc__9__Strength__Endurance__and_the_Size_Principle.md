# Strength, Endurance, & Henneman's Size Principle — Complete Guide
_Source: Empirical Cycling Podcast — Kolie Moore & Kyle Hanson (Watts Doc #9)_

---

## What Is Henneman's Size Principle?

Henneman's Size Principle is a fundamental law of neuromuscular physiology governing how the central nervous system (CNS) activates skeletal muscle. Formulated by Dr. Elwood Henneman in 1965, the principle states:

> **Motor units are recruited in an orderly, fixed hierarchy from smallest (lowest electrical threshold) to largest (highest electrical threshold) as the force demand or duration of contraction increases.**

```
          Henneman's Size Principle Recruitment Hierarchy
          
  Force / Fatigue Demand
    ▲
    │                                     ┌─────────────────────────┐
    │                                     │   High-Threshold Units  │
    │                                     │   • Fast-Twitch (Type IIx/IIa)
    │                                     │   • 300–800 fibers/unit │
    │                                     │   • High force / Fast fatigue
    │                                     └─────────────────────────┘
    │                                                  ▲
    │                                                  │ (Recruited when
    │                                                  │  force demands rise
    │                                                  │  or Type I fatigue)
    │                        ┌─────────────────────────┴───────────┐
    │                        │       Medium-Threshold Units        │
    │                        │       • Fast-Twitch Oxidative (IIa) │
    │                        │       • 100–300 fibers/unit         │
    │                        └─────────────────────────────────────┘
    │                                           ▲
    │                                           │
    │   ┌───────────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────┐
    │   │                  Low-Threshold Units                    │
    │   │                  • Slow-Twitch (Type I)                 │
    │   │                  • 10–180 fibers/unit                   │
    │   │                  • High oxidative / High fatigue resistance│
    │   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
    └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────► Motor Unit Size
```

### Motor Unit Mechanics:
* **The Motor Unit:** Composed of a single alpha motor neuron and all the individual muscle fibers it innervates.
* **All-or-None Activation:** When an action potential reaches the threshold of an alpha motor neuron, **every single muscle fiber** within that motor unit contracts simultaneously and maximally.
* **Gradation of Force:** The human body cannot "partially contract" a motor unit. To vary muscle force output, the CNS uses two mechanisms:
  1. **Spatial Recruitment:** Activating a greater number of motor units (following the size principle).
  2. **Rate Coding (Frequency):** Increasing the firing rate (action potentials per second) of active motor units.

## Key Physiological Mechanisms / How to Think About It

### 1. Deconstructing the 16-Week Elite Cycling Strength Study
A landmark 16-week study on elite Under-23 (U23) road cyclists investigated the effects of heavy resistance training paired with high endurance volume:
* **Endurance Group:** 10–18 hours/week of endurance riding.
* **Strength + Endurance Group:** 10–18 hours/week of endurance riding **plus** heavy lifting 2–3 times/week ($5\text{–}6\text{ RM}$ loads across squats, leg press, lunges, and calf raises).

#### The Surprising Results:
| Metric | Endurance Only Group | Strength + Endurance Group | Physiological Interpretation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$VO_2max$** | No change (0%) | No change (0%) | Strength training does not stimulate central cardiac remodeling. |
| **FTP (MLSS)** | No change (0%) | No change (0%) | Metabolic quasi-steady-state threshold was unaltered. |
| **5-Minute Power** | +3–4% | +3–4% | 5-min power is constrained by $VO_2max$ (cardiac delivery), identical in both. |
| **45-Minute TT Power** | **0% Change** | **+8% (+15 to 25 Watts)** | Highly significant performance surge at supra-threshold intensity. |
| **Body Mass / Muscle Size** | No change | No change | Concurrent endurance signaling blunted myofibrillar hypertrophy. |
| **Max Voluntary Contraction**| No change | **+12% to +20%** | Marked increase in central neural drive and rate of force development. |
| **Fiber Conversion** | Stable | **Type IIx $\rightarrow$ Type IIa** | Fast-twitch glycolytic fibers gained oxidative characteristics. |

### 2. Debunking the "Fractional Force Requirement" Myth

A prevalent coaching theory claims that increasing 1RM strength makes cycling "easier" because each pedal stroke requires a smaller percentage of maximal strength.

```
Pedal Force Math at 300W (FTP) @ 95 RPM:
• Average effective pedal force per stroke: ~170 Newtons
• Peak instantaneous pedal force: ~250–275 Newtons
• Maximal sprinting / isokinetic peak force on bike: >1,000 to 1,500 Newtons

Fractional Force Calculation:
• Baseline: 170 N / 1,000 N = 17.0% of maximal force
• Post-Strength (+20% force = 1,200 N): 170 N / 1,200 N = 14.2% of maximal force
```

* **The Reality:** Lowering pedal force requirement by **1.8–2.8%** cannot account for an **8% (25W) surge** in 45-minute power.
* **Metabolic Constraint:** Sub-maximal endurance cycling is limited by **continuous ATP resynthesis rates** (oxidative phosphorylation and glycogen flux), not absolute contractile mechanical force limits.

### 3. The True Mechanism: Neural Drive & Glycogen Access Under Fatigue

Why did the 45-minute time trial power increase so dramatically?

```
      The Neuromuscular Fatigue & Motor Unit Replacement Cycle
      
 0 min                                                           45 min
 ┌───────────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────┐
 │ Small Type I Motor Units Active   │ Type I Glycogen Depleted      │
 │ • Low force / High oxidation      │ • Force output drops          │
 │ • Fueled by local glycogen + fats │ • High CNS drive triggered    │
 └───────────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────┘
                                                     │
                                                     ▼
                                     ┌───────────────────────────────┐
                                     │ High-Threshold Units Recruited│
                                     │ • Type IIa motor units        │
                                     │ • Fresh glycogen reservoirs   │
                                     │ • Sustains 340W to the finish │
                                     └───────────────────────────────┘
```

1. **Progressive Motor Unit Rotation:**
   * During the opening 15–20 minutes of a hard time trial, the rider predominantly recruits slow-twitch Type I motor units.
   * As these small units deplete their intramuscular glycogen and accumulate metabolic byproducts, their contractile force diminishes.
2. **Central Neural Drive as the Limiter:**
   * To maintain the target wattage, the brain must send a stronger electrical command (central motor drive) to recruit higher-threshold Type IIa motor units.
   * In untrained or non-strength-trained riders, central neural drive fatigues rapidly; the CNS cannot sustain the high discharge rates needed to recruit and fire high-threshold motor units under systemic acidosis and fatigue.
3. **The Strength Training Adaptation:**
   * Heavy strength training ($5\text{–}6\text{ RM}$) conditions the central nervous system to generate and sustain high-frequency neural drive.
   * Crucially, it provides **neural drive fatigue resistance**, allowing the athlete to recruit high-threshold Type IIa motor units late in the effort.
4. **Accessing Untapped Glycogen Pools:**
   * High-threshold Type IIa motor units contain their own independent, un-depleted glycogen stores. By unlocking these motor units via superior neural drive, the rider taps into additional chemical energy substrate to sustain power above FTP.
5. **Type IIx $\rightarrow$ Type IIa Interconversion:**
   * Converting non-oxidative Type IIx fibers into oxidative Type IIa fibers ensures that when these larger motor units are recruited during the time trial, they can process pyruvate aerobically rather than flooding the muscle with rapid acidosis.

## Practical Application & Prescriptions

```
              Annual Strength Periodization for Cyclists
              
  Off-Season Base (Nov–Jan)        Build Phase (Feb–Mar)         Race Season (Apr–Sep)
 ┌──────────────────────────┐     ┌───────────────────────┐     ┌───────────────────────┐
 │ Heavy Strength Focus     │     │ Neural Maintenance    │     │ On-Bike Specificity   │
 │ • 2 sessions / week      │────►│ • 1 session / week    │────►│ • 0 gym sessions      │
 │ • 5–6 RM (Heavy/Low rep) │     │ • 3–5 reps @ 80–85%   │     │ • On-bike sprints &   │
 │ • Full rest (2–3 min)    │     │ • Low total volume    │     │   low-cadence torque  │
 └──────────────────────────┘     └───────────────────────┘     └───────────────────────┘
```

### 1. Off-Season Heavy Strength Protocol (8 to 12 Weeks)
* **Frequency:** 2 sessions per week (spaced $\ge 72\text{ hours}$ apart).
* **Load / Rep Range:** $3 \text{ to } 4 \text{ sets of } 5\text{–}6\text{ repetitions}$ at 80–85% 1RM.
* **Rest Intervals:** $2.5 \text{ to } 3\text{ minutes}$ between sets (complete phosphocreatine and neurological recovery).
* **Exercise Selection:**
  1. **Primary Bilateral Compound:** Barbell Back Squat, Box Squat, or Trap Bar Deadlift.
  2. **Unilateral Movement:** Bulgarian Split Squats or Walking Barbell Lunges.
  3. **Posterior Chain / Hip Extension:** Romanian Deadlifts (RDLs) or Barbell Hip Thrusts.
  4. **Plantarflexion:** Standing Heavy Calf Raises (gastrocnemius/soleus stiffness).

### 2. Session Sequencing Rules
* **Avoid Same-Day Exhaustion:** Perform heavy strength training on dedicated days, or at least 6–8 hours after low-intensity morning aerobic riding.
* **Never Lift Before Hard Intervals:** Never lift heavy immediately prior to threshold or $VO_2max$ cycling sessions. Residual neural fatigue degrades cycling power targets.

### 3. In-Season Strategy: When to Drop the Gym
For pure endurance road racers and time trialists:
* **The "Lifting Break" Performance Surge:** Discontinuing heavy lifting 4–6 weeks prior to major peak events removes residual autonomic fatigue and local muscle stiffness, frequently triggering immediate aerobic breakthroughs on the bike.
* **On-Bike Neural Maintenance:** Maintain high-threshold motor unit recruitment in-season via on-bike protocols:
  * **Standing Start Stomps:** $4–6 \times 10\text{–}12\text{s}$ maximal sprints starting from a near standstill in a heavy gear ($53\times 15$ or $52\times 14$).
  * **Low-Cadence Torque Intervals:** 50–60 RPM threshold climbs to demand high motor unit recruitment.

## Common Pitfalls & Limitations

> [!WARNING]
> **The High-Rep "Endurance Lifting" Trap:** Performing $3 \times 20\text{ to }30\text{ reps}$ with light dumbbells in the gym does not build cycling endurance. It generates massive local metabolic acidosis and DOMS without generating enough mechanical tension to recruit high-threshold motor units or stimulate central neural drive. Cyclists get endurance on the bike; the gym is for high-load neural recruitment.

1. **Expecting Strength to Raise FTP or $VO_2max$:** Heavy lifting does not alter $VO_2max$ (cardiac stroke volume) or MLSS in trained endurance riders.
2. **Lifting to Failure (Grinding Reps):** Training to absolute muscular failure causes excessive central fatigue and sarcoplasmic muscle damage, impairing subsequent cycling sessions for days. Always leave 1–2 repetitions in reserve (RIR 1–2).
3. **Neglecting the Size Principle in Cycling Pacing:** Surging excessively early in a race or time trial forces premature recruitment of high-threshold Type II motor units, depleting their glycogen reserves and accelerating central neural exhaustion.
4. **Ignoring Recovery Demands:** Adding 2 heavy gym sessions on top of a 15-hour riding week without increasing carbohydrate intake, sleep, or reducing interval volume leads directly to overtraining.

### Coach & Athlete Implementation Checklist

* [ ] **Target Neural Adaptations:** Keep resistance loads heavy ($80–85\%\text{ 1RM}$) and repetitions low ($3–6\text{ reps}$) with full 3-minute rest intervals.
* [ ] **Eliminate "Lifting for Cardio":** Do not perform high-rep circuit training with short rests in the gym; build cardiovascular capacity on the bike.
* [ ] **Respect Recovery Windows:** Ensure at least 48 hours between a heavy lower-body lifting session and high-intensity cycling intervals.
* [ ] **Monitor In-Season Fatigue:** Drop gym sessions immediately if on-bike interval power, HRV, or resting heart rate indicates systemic autonomic strain during the racing season.
* [ ] **Apply Pacing Discipline:** Pace long sustained efforts steadily to avoid premature recruitment and exhaustion of high-threshold Type II motor units.

# Getting Started with Strength Training for Cyclists — Complete Guide
_Source: Empirical Cycling Podcast — Kolie Moore & Kyle Hanson (Ten Minute Tips #12)_

---

## What Is Strength Training for Cyclists?

For cyclists, resistance training is not an exercise in competitive powerlifting or bodybuilding. It is a **targeted neuromuscular and structural intervention** designed to address the physiological blind spots of pure endurance cycling:

1. **Bone Mineral Density (BMD) & Skeletal Health:** Cycling is a non-weight-bearing, non-impact sport. Without heavy axial skeletal loading, chronic endurance riders frequently develop osteopenia and severe fracture susceptibility.
2. **Motor Unit Recruitment & Fast-Twitch Preservation:** As athletes age, high-threshold Type II motor units and peak rate of force development (RFD) naturally decline ("old man strength" preserving endurance while losing sprinting power). Heavy resistance training stimulates and preserves these pathways.
3. **Postural Stability & Comfort:** Strengthening the posterior chain, lats, and core stabilizes the closed kinetic chain between hands, pelvis, and feet, eliminating force leakage during sprints and reducing neck/back fatigue.
4. **Active Range of Motion (Mobility):** Cycling operates in a constrained, repetitive joint angle. Loading muscles at long muscle lengths (e.g., deep squats, Romanian deadlifts) builds active mobility and flexibility that passive stretching cannot replicate.

## Key Physiological Mechanisms / How to Think About It

```

          • Osteoblast stimulation                • Stretch-mediated tension
          • High-threshold motor unit firing      • Connective tissue remodeling
          • Rate of Force Development (RFD)       • Active range of motion (ROM)
                        │                                      │
                        └──────────────────┬───────────────────┘
                                           ▼
                              [ Cycling Adaptations ]
                                • Zero torso force leakage
                                • Sparing Type I fibers via high torque
                                • Resistance to bone fractures
```

### 1. Range of Motion (ROM) & Mechanical Tension
* **The Depth Equivalence Principle:** Achieving full depth (e.g., hip crease below knee in a squat) with lighter weights provides an equivalent or superior mechanical tension stimulus compared to overloading a quarter squat with excessive weight.
* **Joint Safety:** Quarter squatting massive loads places enormous shear stress on patellar and quadricep tendons without training the musculature through functional lengths. Full-depth lifting maximizes muscular development while minimizing load-induced joint trauma.

### 2. The Golgi Tendon Organ (GTO) & Connective Tissue Lag
* **Autogenic Inhibition:** Tendons and ligaments have minimal direct blood supply and adapt substantially slower than muscle tissue. When load is introduced too rapidly, the GTO senses excessive tension and involuntarily inhibits muscle contraction to prevent tendon avulsion.
* **Connective Tissue Adaptation:** Tendon stiffness and collagen remodeling require weeks of progressive, submaximal loading before heavy near-maximal forces can be safely tolerated.

### 3. Skewed Repetition Tables in Endurance Athletes
Standard Soviet and powerlifting 1RM-to-rep conversion charts (e.g., estimating 1RM from a 5RM or 8RM) **do not apply to endurance athletes**:
* **High Oxidative Resistance:** Due to elevated capillary density, rapid ATP recovery, and high fatigue resistance, an endurance cyclist might complete 5–7 reps at $90\%$ of 1RM, whereas a pure powerlifter might only manage 3 reps.
* **Sudden Neural Cliff:** Adding just 2.5–5 lbs above true maximal capacity causes immediate total failure. Endurance cyclists should use **Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE)** and **Reps in Reserve (RIR)** rather than testing true 1RMs.

### 4. DOMS vs. True Systemic Fatigue
* **Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness (DOMS):** Soreness stems from micro-tears in the sarcomere Z-discs and inflammatory remodeling during eccentric loading, peaking 24–48 hours post-session.
* **Soreness $\neq$ Functional Impairment:** Light active recovery (e.g., 15–20 minutes of very easy spinning at $<50\text{W}$ or gentle walking) circulates blood, compresses cartilage for nutrient diffusion, and alleviates stiffness.

## Practical Application & Prescriptions

```
                    Macrocycle Strength Entry Progression
                    
   ┌───────────────────────┐   ┌────────────────────────┐   ┌───────────────────────┐
   │ Phase 1: Adaptation   ├──►│ Phase 2: Hypertrophy / ├──►│ Phase 3: Max Strength │
   │ (Weeks 1–3)           │   │ Strength Base (Wks 4–8)│   │ (Weeks 9–14)          │
   │ • 2 sessions/wk       │   │ • 2 sessions/wk        │   │ • 2 sessions/wk       │
   │ • Light loads (RPE 5–6│   │ • RPE 7–8, 6–10 reps   │   │ • RPE 8–9, 3–5 reps   │
   │ • 8–12 reps, slow temp│   │ • Movement consistency │   │ • Maximal neural drive│
   └───────────────────────┘   └────────────────────────┘   └───────────────────────┘
```

### 1. Phase Progression Model

#### Phase 1: Anatomical Adaptation (Weeks 1–3)
* **Goal:** Bed in movement patterns, condition connective tissue, eliminate extreme DOMS.
* **Parameters:** 2 sessions/week, $2\text{ to }3\text{ sets of }8\text{–}12\text{ reps}$, RPE 5–6 (very light, leaving 4–5 reps in reserve).
* **Tempo:** Controlled eccentric (2–3 seconds down), 1-second pause in the hole, smooth concentric.

#### Phase 2: Hypertrophy & Work Capacity (Weeks 4–8)
* **Goal:** Build foundational muscular strength and joint integrity.
* **Parameters:** 2 sessions/week, $3\text{ sets of }6\text{–}10\text{ reps}$, RPE 7–8 (leaving 2–3 reps in reserve).

#### Phase 3: Maximum Strength & Neural Drive (Weeks 9–14)
* **Goal:** Maximize high-threshold motor unit recruitment and rate of force development without adding bulk.
* **Parameters:** 2 sessions/week, $3\text{ to }5\text{ sets of }3\text{–}5\text{ reps}$, RPE 8–9, with long rests ($3\text{ to }5\text{ minutes}$).

---

### 2. Gym Warm-Up & Set Protocol
1. **General Thermal Prep:** 5–10 minutes of low-intensity cycling ($<50\text{W}$, "Zone 0.25") to elevate core temperature without triggering AMPK fatigue.
2. **Dynamic Mobility & Glute Activation:** Banded lateral walks, monster walks, bodyweight squats.
3. **Empty Bar Acclimatization:** 10 reps with the empty barbell, feeling out hip depth and ankle dorsiflexion.
4. **Pyramid Warm-Up Sets (Example for 225 lb Working Weight):**
   * $10 \text{ reps} \times 45\text{ lbs (Bar)}$
   * $8 \text{ reps} \times 135\text{ lbs}$
   * $5 \text{ reps} \times 185\text{ lbs}$
   * $3 \text{ reps} \times 205\text{ lbs}$
   * *Working Sets:* $3 \times 5 \text{ at } 225\text{ lbs}$

---

### 3. On-Bike and Gym Scheduling Integration
* **Off-Season Base Phase:** Schedule 2 gym sessions per week. On-bike training should be restricted to Zone 2 endurance and low-stress tempo. Avoid high-intensity threshold/VO2max intervals during the initial strength adaptation block.
* **Double-Day Protocol:**
  * Morning: Easy endurance ride (Zone 2).
  * Midday: Refuel with a carbohydrate/protein meal, take a 30-minute nap, and rest $3\text{ to }4\text{ hours}$.
  * Afternoon: Strength training session.
* **Deload Weeks:** Every 3–4 weeks, perform a deload: maintain 2 gym visits but cut volume by $50\%$ (e.g., 2 sets of 3–5 reps at $60–70\%$ normal load) to allow joint and CNS regeneration.

## Common Pitfalls & Limitations

> [!WARNING]
> **The Ego Lifting Trap:** Sacrificing squat depth to load more weight onto the barbell increases patellar tendon strain and reduces glute/hamstring recruitment. If technique or depth degrades on a rep, that rep is counted as a failed attempt.

1. **Attempting 1RM Testing Early:** Novice lifters lack the neurological coordination to safely execute a true 1RM. Testing maximal singles creates unnecessary injury risk without providing actionable training data.
2. **Marathon Gym Sessions:** Spending 2–3 hours in the gym doing endless isolation machines induces severe muscle damage that completely derails on-bike training consistency. Workouts should take 45–60 minutes.
3. **Adding Weights Without Reducing Bike Stress:** Treating strength training as "free" volume. Heavy lifting depletes glycogen and taxes the central nervous system; overall cycling volume/intensity must be balanced accordingly.
4. **Over-Cueing Technique:** Trying to focus on 10 different biomechanical cues during a single lift. Pick **one cue** per set (e.g., *"knees out"* or *"drive through midfoot"*).

### Cyclist Gym Onboarding Checklist

* [ ] **Learn Form First:** Use bodyweight or empty bar until full range of motion is achieved comfortably.
* [ ] **Video Technique:** Record working sets from a side/quarter angle to verify standardized depth and spinal neutrality.
* [ ] **Use RPE / Reps in Reserve:** Stop each set 1–2 reps before technical breakdown; never grind to total failure.
* [ ] **Rest Fully Between Sets:** Take 3–5 minutes between heavy compound sets.
* [ ] **Fuel Glycogen Reserves:** Consume carbohydrate meals pre- and post-lift; strength sessions are glycolytically demanding.
* [ ] **Active Recovery for Soreness:** Perform 15–20 minutes of very light Zone 1 spinning to relieve stiffness.

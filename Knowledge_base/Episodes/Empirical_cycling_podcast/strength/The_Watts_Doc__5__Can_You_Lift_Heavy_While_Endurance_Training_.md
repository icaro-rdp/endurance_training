# Heavy Strength Training & The Interference Effect — Complete Guide
_Source: Empirical Cycling Podcast — Kolie Moore & Kyle Hanson (Watts Doc #5)_

---

## What Is Concurrent Training?

Concurrent training refers to the integration of heavy resistance training (aimed at increasing muscular force production, neuromuscular recruitment, and structural integrity) and endurance training (aimed at expanding oxidative capacity, mitochondrial density, and cardiovascular performance) within the same macrocycle.

For cyclists, strength training offers profound physiological benefits:
* **Neural Drive & Motor Unit Recruitment:** Increased central motor drive enables recruitment of high-threshold Type II motor units, raising maximal sprinting power and low-cadence torque capabilities.
* **Movement Efficiency & Force Economy:** Higher maximal force capacity reduces the fractional recruitment required per pedal stroke at sub-maximal wattages.
* **Bone Mineral Density (BMD):** Cycling is a non-impact, non-weight-bearing sport; heavy axial skeletal loading (squats, deadlifts) is critical to counteract osteopenia and fractures.
* **The Central Conflict:** Skeletal muscle fibers face competing biological signals: endurance stimuli demand metabolic efficiency and mitochondrial expansion, while heavy resistance training demands myofibrillar protein synthesis and mechanical tension adaptations.

          (Protein Synthesis & MPS)                                  │

### 1. Neural Drive vs. Hypertrophy
Initial adaptations to resistance training are almost exclusively neurological:
* **First 4 to 8 Weeks:** Strength increases rapidly due to improved rate of force development (RFD), motor unit synchronization, and reduction of antagonist co-activation. True structural myofibrillar hypertrophy is minimal during this phase.
* **Perceptual Myth of "Instant Bulking":** Cyclists often fear putting on massive muscle mass. However, gaining significant hypertrophy requires a caloric surplus, high volume, and low concurrent aerobic interference.
* **Strength "Loss" in Season:** When an athlete ceases lifting in-season and notices a decline in their 1-Repetition Maximum (1RM), they have not lost muscle fiber mass; rather, the nervous system has downregulated its high-threshold motor unit firing coordination. A brief 2-week reintroduction quickly restores baseline force.

### 2. The Hickson Interference Phenomenon (Hickson, 1980)
Dr. Robert Hickson’s landmark 1980 study established the scientific foundation of concurrent training interference:
* **Design:** 10-week study comparing three groups:
  1. *Strength Only:* 5 days/week heavy leg training (80% 1RM).
  2. *Endurance Only:* 6 days/week cycling/running ($6 \times 5\text{ min}$ at $VO_2max$ + continuous runs).
  3. *Concurrent:* Combined both full strength and endurance protocols.
* **Results:**
  * **Strength Group:** Continuous linear increase in 1RM squat (+44% over 10 weeks).
  * **Endurance Group:** Significant increase in $VO_2max$; no strength change.
  * **Concurrent Group:** Strength increased identically to the strength group for the first 7 weeks (neural phase). Between weeks 7 and 8, strength plateaued, and at weeks 9–10, strength **significantly declined**. However, their $VO_2max$ increased identically to the endurance-only group.
* **Core Conclusion:** Endurance training systematically interferes with maximal strength expression and hypertrophy over chronic timelines, but strength training does not impair $VO_2max$ development.

### 3. Molecular Signaling Antagonisms (Baar, 2014)
* **The mTORC1 Cascade (Anabolic):** High mechanical load triggers intracellular signaling via focal adhesion kinase (FAK) and phosphatidic acid, activating **mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1)**. This phosphorylates p70S6K and 4E-BP1, accelerating muscle protein synthesis (MPS). The mTOR signal remains active for 18–48 hours in novices and ~4–12 hours in trained lifters.
* **The AMPK / PGC-1$\alpha$ Cascade (Oxidative):** Endurance exercise depletes ATP, raising the AMP:ATP ratio and activating **AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)** and **SIRT1**, which drive **PGC-1$\alpha$** transcription for mitochondrial biogenesis.
* **The Molecular Cross-Talk:** AMPK phosphorylates and activates **TSC2** (tuberous sclerosis complex 2) and directly phosphorylates Raptor, thereby **potently inhibiting mTORC1**. When high-volume endurance training is performed, the muscle cell prioritizes energetic homeostasis (AMPK) over protein synthesis (mTOR).

### 4. Muscle Fiber Type Plasticity
* **Fiber Shifting:** Resistance training promotes Type IIa/IIx fiber cross-sectional area. Unchecked year-round heavy lifting preserves fast-twitch characteristics.
* **Concurrent Training Dynamics:** Concurrent training induces hypertrophy across both Type I and Type II fibers without rapid fiber-type transitions.
* **Diesel Adaptation Trade-Off:** For grand-tour riders and pure climbers, maximizing Type I oxidative efficiency and mitochondrial density may require minimizing heavy resistance loads during peak race blocks.

## Practical Application & Prescriptions

### 1. Off-Season vs. In-Season Periodization Model

```
 Macrocycle Strength Progression
 [ Off-Season: General Prep ] ──► [ Late Base / Early Build ] ──► [ Race Season / Peak ]
 • 2 sessions / week             • 1 session / week (Maintenance)  • On-bike high torque / sprints
 • Heavy compound lifts          • Heavy loads, ultra-low volume   • Heavy lifting ceased (pure road)
 • Squat, Deadlift, RDL          • 2–3 sets of 3–5 reps            • Small dose kept for sprinters/track
 • Progressive load increase     • Maintain neural recruitment      • Prioritize aerobic recovery
```

### 2. Exercise Selection for Cyclists
Focus on multi-joint compound movements with high axial loading and minimal eccentric muscle damage:
* **Primary Lifts:** Barbell Back Squat, Trap-Bar Deadlift, Romanian Deadlift (RDL), Bulgarian Split Squat.
* **Repetition Ranges:**
  * *Strength & Neural Drive:* $3 \text{ to } 5 \text{ sets of } 3 \text{ to } 5 \text{ reps}$ at $\ge 80–85\%$ 1RM with long rests ($2–3\text{ minutes}$).
  * *Avoid High-Rep Bodybuilding Sets (12–20 reps to failure):* High volume induces massive metabolic and muscular fatigue without maximizing peak neural recruitment.

### 3. Intra-Day Sequencing & Order Effects
* **Priority: Strength & Power:** Lift in the morning when glycogen stores and central nervous system (CNS) freshness are optimal; perform easy endurance riding $\ge 6\text{ to }8\text{ hours}$ later.
* **Priority: Endurance Performance:** Perform hard interval sessions on the bike first. If lifting must occur on the same day, perform resistance work several hours later.
* **The "Strength Without Bulk" Strategy:** If an athlete wants maximal neural strength gains with zero unwanted muscle hypertrophy, perform 15–20 minutes of moderate spinning (Zone 2 / Tempo) immediately after lifting. The resulting AMPK activation truncates post-exercise mTORC1 signaling.

### 4. Nutritional Support: The Leucine Threshold
* **Target:** Consume 20–25g of high-quality, leucine-rich protein (whey, dairy, eggs, meat) every 3–4 hours throughout the day.
* **Mechanism:** Leucine serves as a direct trigger for mTORC1 activation and muscle protein maintenance during high-volume endurance blocks.

## Common Pitfalls & Limitations

> [!WARNING]
> **The In-Season Recovery Trap:** Continuing heavy, fatiguing gym sessions during high-intensity race build blocks creates an energy deficit and systemic autonomic overload. Athletes often feel chronically sluggish on the bike and misdiagnose this fatigue as a loss of aerobic fitness.

1. **Lifting to Muscular Failure:** Grinding out reps to total failure generates severe delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) and prolonged CNS fatigue, compromising on-bike interval quality for days.
2. **Replacing Heavy Weights with High-Rep Endurance Lifting:** Doing sets of 25–30 light reps in the gym merely duplicates the muscular endurance stimulus of cycling while providing zero maximal strength or bone density benefits.
3. **Inadequate In-Season Transition:** Abruptly stopping lifting without maintaining on-bike high-torque or sprint work, leading to a loss of high-threshold motor unit recruitment over several months.

## Summary Checklist / Decision Table

### Concurrent Training Strategy by Athlete Specialty

| Discipline / Specialty | Off-Season Lifting | In-Season Lifting | Key Exercise Focus | Primary Objective |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Track Sprinter / Crit Racer** | 2–3 sessions/wk | 1–2 sessions/wk | Squat, Trap-Bar, Power Clean | Maximal force, RFD, peak sprint wattage |
| **MTB / Cyclocross** | 2 sessions/wk | 1 session/wk (low volume) | Split Squats, Deadlifts, Core | High-torque power, handling stability |
| **Time Trialist / Diesel** | 2 sessions/wk | Cease 6–8 wks pre-race | Back Squat, RDL | Core stiffness, pedaling economy, BMD |
| **Climber / Grand Tour** | 1–2 sessions/wk | Cease in-season | Trap-Bar Deadlift, Step-ups | Bone density, neural recruitment, minimal mass |

### Coach & Athlete Implementation Checklist

* [ ] **Off-Season Timing:** Dedicate 8–12 weeks in the off-season to 2x/week heavy compound lifting.
* [ ] **Prescribe Heavy, Low-Volume Sets:** Keep rep ranges at 3–5 reps ($\ge 80\%$ 1RM) to maximize neural drive while minimizing muscle hypertrophy and DOMS.
* [ ] **Manage In-Season Volume:** Cut gym volume by 50–70% (1 session/week, 2–3 sets) during early build, and fully drop heavy lifting 4–6 weeks prior to target peak events.
* [ ] **Fuel with Leucine:** Ingest 20–25g protein boluses distributed across the day to support muscular preservation.
* [ ] **Monitor On-Bike Freshness:** If heavy legs consistently impair key threshold or $VO_2max$ sessions, eliminate the gym session immediately.

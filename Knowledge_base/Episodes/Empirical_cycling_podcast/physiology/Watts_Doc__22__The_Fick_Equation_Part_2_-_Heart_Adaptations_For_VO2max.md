## What Determines Cardiac Output & Stroke Volume?

In the Fick equation, maximal cardiac output ($\dot{Q}_{\text{max}} = \text{Stroke Volume} \times \text{Heart Rate}$) represents the primary convective rate limiter of whole-body $\dot{\text{V}}\text{O}_2\text{max}$. Because maximal heart rate is genetically capped and declines with age, **long-term improvements in $\dot{\text{V}}\text{O}_2\text{max}$ are driven almost exclusively by increases in maximal Stroke Volume ($SV$)**.

Stroke volume is governed by two opposing hemodynamic forces:
1. **Preload (Diastolic Filling):** The volume of blood returning to the heart that stretches the left ventricle at the end of diastole (End-Diastolic Volume / EDV).
2. **Afterload (Vascular Resistance):** The systemic vascular resistance and arterial blood pressure against which the left ventricle must contract during systole to eject blood.

```
                  +-----------------------------------+
                  |      DETERMINANTS OF STROKE VOLUME |
                  +-----------------+-----------------+
                                    |
            +-----------------------+-----------------------+
            |                                               |
            v                                               v
    [ PRELOAD (Filling) ]                           [ AFTERLOAD (Resistance) ]
  - Venous return & muscle pump                   - Systemic vascular resistance
  - Blood & plasma volume                         - Arterial stiffness & hip angle
  - Frank-Starling stretch                        - Peripheral vasoconstriction
  - Stimulates ECCENTRIC remodeling               - Stimulates CONCENTRIC remodeling
```

## Key Physiological Mechanisms / How to Think About It

### 1. Diastolic Filling & Stroke Volume Kinetics (Trained vs. Untrained)
A classic exercise cardiology debate centered on whether stroke volume plateaus at moderate intensity:
* **Untrained Individuals:** Stroke volume rises slightly from rest to $\sim 120\text{ BPM}$ ($120\text{ mL} \rightarrow 130\text{ mL}$) and then **plateaus completely**. As heart rate climbs to $190\text{ BPM}$, diastolic filling time collapses from $342\text{ ms}$ to $170\text{ ms}$, preventing further filling. Peak cardiac output reaches $\sim 25\text{ L/min}$.
* **Endurance-Trained Cyclists:** Stroke volume exhibits **no plateau**, rising linearly from $90\text{ BPM}$ ($130\text{ mL}$) to $180\text{--}190\text{ BPM}$ ($190\text{ mL}$). Despite diastolic filling time dropping to a mere $99\text{ ms}$, their left ventricle achieves a filling rate exceeding **$1.8\text{ L/sec}$**, driving peak cardiac output to **$35+\text{ L/min}$**.

### 2. The Frank-Starling Law of the Heart
The Frank-Starling mechanism is an intrinsic autoregulatory property of myocardium:
* As venous return fills the left ventricle, the ventricular walls are stretched, lengthening cardiac sarcomeres toward their optimal length ($\sim 2.2\text{--}2.4\ \mu\text{m}$).
* This mechanical stretch increases **calcium sensitivity of troponin C** and reduces interfilament lattice spacing between actin and myosin, creating more cross-bridge formations.
* **Result:** The heart contracts with greater force during systole with zero neural delay, ejecting a greater stroke volume without requiring elevated sympathetic afterload.

### 3. Cardiac Remodeling: Concentric vs. Eccentric Hypertrophy

| Hypertrophy Type | Primary Hemodynamic Driver | Cellular Morphology | Functional Outcome | Typical Sports |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Eccentric Hypertrophy** | **High Preload / Volume Overload** (diastolic stretch) | Sarcomeres added **in series**; ventricular chamber diameter and internal volume expand; normal/proportional wall thickness. | Massive increase in Stroke Volume and maximal Cardiac Output ($\dot{Q}$). | Cycling, Rowing, Cross-Country Skiing, Running. |
| **Concentric Hypertrophy** | **High Afterload / Pressure Overload** (systolic resistance) | Sarcomeres added **in parallel**; ventricular wall thickness increases; internal cavity volume remains static or shrinks. | High contractile pressure capacity; zero increase in stroke volume or $\dot{\text{V}}\text{O}_2\text{max}$. | Olympic Weightlifting, Powerlifting, Wrestling. |

### 4. Mechanical Transduction: Costameric Integrins
Cardiac myocytes convert physical stretch into transcriptional adaptations via **integrin-adhesome complexes** located at costameres (the z-disc/sarcolemma interface) and intercalated discs. Sustained mechanical stretch under high diastolic filling triggers intracellular kinase cascades that upregulate serial sarcomere assembly.

### 5. Cross-Country Skiing vs. Cycling Hemodynamics
* **Cross-Country Skiing:** Engages four limbs through full ranges of motion with upright posture. The massive combined muscle pump generates immense venous return, presenting the heart with an abundance of preload. XC skiers can stimulate eccentric hypertrophy at $90\text{--}95\%\ \dot{\text{V}}\text{O}_2\text{max}$.
* **Cycling:** Involves restricted upper-body action, a closed hip angle that creates mechanical kinking and vascular impedance in iliac arteries, and lower upright venous assistance. 
* **Implication for Cyclists:** Cyclists face higher afterload; achieving maximal diastolic stretch and eccentric cardiac remodeling requires training at **$100\%\ \dot{\text{V}}\text{O}_2\text{max}$** with high cadence.

## Practical Application & Prescriptions

### 1. Training to Maximize Preload & Stroke Volume
* **Intensity Requirement:** In well-trained cyclists whose stroke volume does not plateau, eccentric cardiac remodeling is maximized when cardiac output and end-diastolic stretch are at their absolute limit ($100\%\ \dot{\text{V}}\text{O}_2\text{max}$).
* **Interval Design:**
  * **$3\text{ to }5\text{ Minute Hard-Start Intervals}$** ($4\text{--}5 \times 4\text{ min}$ or $4 \times 5\text{ min}$).
  * Paced to induce maximal hyperventilation and extreme cardiorespiratory strain within the first minute.
* **Cadence Prescription:**
  * Maintain high cadence ($100\text{--}115\text{ RPM}$) during severe intervals.
  * Rapid, rhythmic contractions maximize the skeletal muscle pump, increasing venous return velocity and overcoming peripheral resistance to fill the right atrium.

### 2. Blood Volume Maintenance for Cardiac Preload
* As established in the Coyle studies, stroke volume is tightly coupled to circulating blood volume.
* Athletes must maintain optimal hydration, dietary electrolyte intake, and regular aerobic volume to preserve plasma hypervolemia.
* After a period of detraining, 2–3 weeks of base riding rapidly restores plasma volume, restoring stroke volume before structural myocyte remodeling takes place.

## Common Pitfalls & Limitations

| Pitfall / Misconception | Physiological Reality | Practical Consequence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **"Submaximal Zone 2 riding maximizes cardiac stroke volume in elite athletes."** | In elite cyclists with high baseline blood volume, submaximal filling does not stretch the ventricle beyond resting elasticity. | Highly trained athletes will not increase stroke volume ceiling via base miles alone; targeted severe-domain intervals are required. |
| **"Low cadence grinding builds the heart."** | Low cadence ($<70\text{ RPM}$) increases muscular torque and arterial afterload, favoring concentric wall thickening over eccentric chamber expansion. | Spin at high cadence ($>100\text{ RPM}$) during $\text{VO}_2\text{max}$ intervals to maximize the venous muscle pump. |
| **"Cardiac output can be boosted by raising max heart rate."** | Max heart rate is genetically fixed and decreases with training; cardiac output improvements rely entirely on stroke volume expansion. | Focus training interventions on stroke volume drivers (preload, plasma volume, ventricular compliance). |

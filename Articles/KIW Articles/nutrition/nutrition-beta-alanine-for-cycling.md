What is Beta-Alanine?

Beta-alanine is a non-essential amino acid that serves as a building block for carnosine, a
compound stored in muscles. Unlike other amino acids, beta-alanine does not contribute
directly to protein synthesis but plays a crucial role in buffering acidity in muscle cells.
When ingested, beta-alanine combines with histidine to form carnosine, which helps
maintain pH balance during high-intensity exercise. This buffering effect is particularly
useful in delaying muscular fatigue and sustaining performance.

Why is Beta-Alanine Used in Cycling and Endurance Sports?

In endurance sports like cycling, sustained high-intensity efforts (above second
threshold/FTP/CP) generate hydrogen ions (H+), which lower muscle pH and contribute to
fatigue. Carnosine, facilitated by beta-alanine supplementation, helps neutralize these
hydrogen ions, potentially improving performance. Beta-alanine is particularly beneficial in
race scenarios involving efforts above the second threshold/CP/FTP (repeated
accelerations/sprints, time trials, or climbs where the drop in pH can limit performance.

The physiological mechanism and its area of application is very similar to what we saw (here)
for sodium bicarbonate. The difference is that sodium bicarbonate acts as an extracellular
buffer, while beta-alanine primarily enhances intracellular buffering capacity, making it
complementary rather than redundant when used in addition to sodium bicarbonate.

Does Beta-Alanine Boost Race Performance?

Beta-alanine supplementation has been shown to improve performance in events lasting
1–10 minutes, where acidosis is a limiting factor (Saunders et al., 2017). However, its
effects on endurance events beyond 60 minutes are less clear, likely related to the
varying nature of the exercise bouts. For instance, one study found that beta-alanine
supplementation significantly increased peak power output during sprints at the end of an
endurance cycling race (Van Thienen et al., 2009), but another study indicated no improvement
in short-duration sprints or time-trial performance during prolonged cycling (Perim et al., 2022).
In the later study, the sprints were only 10 seconds in duration, and the time-trial was roughly
12.5 mins, both of which are outside of the optimal 1–10 min window where beta-alanine may
shine.

Thus, its effectiveness may depend on the specific demands of the race. While it may
not directly enhance prolonged endurance efforts, it could improve the ability to attack,
surge, or sustain high power outputs during critical race moments.

For cyclists, beta-alanine may be particularly useful in:

Short-to-medium-duration effotts (3–10 min)

Repeated sprint efforts (e.g., criteriums, breakaways)

Final sprint finishes after prolonged efforts

However, its effects are less pronounced in ultra-endurance efforts where neuromuscular
fatigue and glycogen depletion rather than muscular acidosis is the primary limiter.

Does Beta-Alanine Improve Training Adaptations?

Beyond acute performance enhancement, beta-alanine may improve long-term training
adaptations by allowing athletes to push harder during high-intensity sessions. Studies showing
beneficial effects have typically not clamped training intensity, meaning athletes perform
intervals based on effort, rather than pre-determined wattages (Rothschild & Bishop, 2020). As
shown below, there may be additive effects between b-alanine and sprint interval training, likely
due to higher workloads that can be completed with supplementation:

However, overuse of beta-alanine in training should be approached cautiously. While it enables
harder efforts, excessive high-intensity training without adequate recovery may lead to
overtraining.

Dosage for Racing and Training

Racing: Since beta-alanine requires a loading phase to be effective, acute
supplementation on race day will have no immediate effect. Instead, maintaining elevated
muscle carnosine levels over weeks/months is the key. So, using chronic
supplementation during training periods until race day is the proper approach.

Training: A daily dose of 3.2–6.4 g of beta-alanine, divided into smaller doses (e.g., 800
mg x 4–8 times per day) over 4–6 weeks, is recommended to maximize muscle
carnosine stores for the race day.

Maintenance: After the loading phase, a lower daily dose (~1.2 g) can help sustain
elevated carnosine levels.

Side Effects

The primary side effect of beta-alanine isparesthesia, a tingling or itching sensation, particularly
on the face, hands, and arms. While harmless, this can be uncomfortable for some athletes.
Splitting doses into smaller amounts throughout the day and using a sustained release formula
can help mitigate this effect.

What is the Best Form of Beta-Alanine?

Beta-alanine is available in various forms:

Standalone powder or capsules – The most cost-effective and widely available form.

Pre-workout blends – Often combined with caffeine and other ergogenic aids but may
contain suboptimal beta-alanine doses.

Sustained-release formulas – Designed to minimize tingling sensations while
maintaining absorption.

Topical carnosine – There is a topical product which shows promise (Sharpe & Macias,
2016), but research is still in its early days. Topical carnosine can potentially avoid the
problems related to loading and side effects.

Does Combining Beta-Alanine with Other Supplements Enhance Its Effects?

Beta-alanine can be effectively combined with other ergogenic aids for synergistic effects,
however research on co-ingestion is more challenging to do and so we have far fewer studies
to look at. However, the potential for a ceiling effect also exists, meaning that beyond a certain
point, additional supplementation may not yield further benefits. More research is needed to
fully understand these interactions.

Nitrates (beetroot juice): While both beta-alanine and nitrates enhance performance
through different mechanisms, their interaction is not well studied. Some theories suggest

nitrates) pathways, so it is not clear if they bring to additional positive effects when
summed.

Summary

Beta-alanine is a well-supported supplement for endurance athletes, particularly those
engaging in high-intensity, repeated efforts. While its effects on ultra-endurance performance
remain inconclusive, it can be a valuable tool for cyclists seeking to improve time trial
performance, repeated sprint ability, and race-day surges. To maximize its benefits, consistent
daily supplementation is required over several weeks.

As with all supplements, individual responses vary, and it is best to trial beta-alanine in training
before relying on it for competition.

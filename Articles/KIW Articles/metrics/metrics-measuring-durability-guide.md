# Measuring Durability: the Guide

**Source:** knowledgeiswatt.substack.com/p/30-testing-durability-the-guide  
**Date:** September 20, 2024

Durability is the ability to decrease as less as possible performance during prolonged exercise.
In a previous post, we saw that durability is not related to traditional endurance physiological or
performance markers measured at rested state like VO2max, thresholds’ power and 20min
max mean power output (Read more here). So, a higher VO2max or FTP doesn’t necessarily
mean a higher durability. As a consequence, we can’t make assumptions on a given athlete’s
durability level based on physiological or performance measured at rested state: we have to
measure also durability to have a comprehensive physical evaluation.

So, how to measure durability?

There are 3 ways:

1.       Standardized test (preferred)

2.       Record Power Outputs after a certain energy expenditure (kilojoules)

3.       Power-Heart Rate Decoupling

1. STANDARDIZED TEST

This is the best solution as, while using approach 2 and 3 we are trying to estimate durability
(with some limitation that we will see below), durability’s standardized test protocols directly
measure this physical capacity.

How to design it?

Durability’s standardized testing consists in measuring a physiological and performance
marker at rested state (test 1), and then repeating the same measurement after a
prolonged fatiguing protocol (test 2) . Using this approach, durability’s score will be
represented by the % decrease in the chosen physiological/performance marker
between before and after the fatiguing protocol.

Durability score (%) = (B-A) / A * 100

where:

A is the physiological/performance marker measured at rested state (Test 1);

B is the physiological/performance marker measured after the fatiguing protocol (Test 2);

For example, a rider who has 20 min mean maximal power output of 420 W in a rested state
and 400 W after a fatiguing protocol, he/she has a durability score equal to -5% (a lower
decrease, -2% for example, would mean a better durability).

When designing a durability standardized test, it is important to consider each of the
following points:

1. Choose a proper fatiguing protocol

2. Choose a proper physiological/performance marker on which you want durability to be

measured

3. Choose a proper standardized nutrition protocol

4. Testing in one day or two days?

5. Right interpretation of the results

1.1 Choose a proper fatiguing protocol:

One of the common ‘mistake’ when choosing the fatiguing protocol is considering only energy
expenditure, 3000 kj for example, without defining a specific combination of duration and
intensity to be used to attain a certain energy expenditure.

In fact,

Work (kj) = Power Output (W) * Time (s) / 1000;

So, the same energy expenditure (kilojoules) can be completed with a shorter and higher
intensity fatiguing protocol (2h30min with multiple intervals in zone 4-5, for example) or a
longer and lower intensity’s one (4 hours in zone 2 in a 5/7 zones model, for example). In this
scenario, a recent study (2024) by James Spragg and Colleagues showed that when
kilojoules are matched, higher the intensity of the fatiguing protocol (so lower the
duration), lower the durability score. (Read more here)

So, when designing the fatiguing protocol, you can have in mind a general energy expenditure
to target (3000 kilojoules for example), but then you should pick a specific exercise
intensity to be performed for a certain duration.

Should the fatiguing protocol be standardized using relative or absolute intensity?

It is important to use the ‘relative’ (% of individual physiological thresholds) and not the
absolute (a given power output or heart rate, like 200 W, 5 w/kg or 130bpm) intensity!
This is pivotal to have a proper comparison of durability both between different athletes and
when comparing durability’s progress over time of the same rider.

Let’s understand this better comparing this two fatiguing protocol:

3hrs at 250W (absolute intensity)

3hrs at 90% of first lactate/ventilatory thresholds (relative intensity).

If we are comparing durability between different riders, if rider A have first threshold at 270 W
and rider B at 230 W, A would ride at 92% of first threshold, while B would ride at 108% of first
threshold. So, it is very likely that A vs B difference in performance after the fatiguing bout
would be influenced by exercising below (A) or above (B) the first threshold. In this scenario,
the durability score will be strongly influenced by cyclists’ fitness at rested state. Consequently,
using such approach can’t be considered a true durability assessment.

The same hold true when comparing longitudinally the same cyclist. Let’s make this example:

Test 1 january, beginning of preparation, first threshold 300 W

Test 2 april, competition period, first threshold 320 W (+20 W improvement)

If we decide to standardise the fatiguing protocol as 3hrs at 250 W, it is very likely that the
athlete would perform better after fatiguing protocol in april (test 2) just because he/she would
ride at a lower relative intensity and further from first threshold when compared to january (test
1). In fact, even if he/she would be riding below the first threshold in both cases, pedalling at 83
vs 78% of first threshold can still influence fatigue accumulation during the 3hrs at 250 W.

So, my suggestion is sticking with relative intensity (90% of power or heart rate at first
threshold for example) when standardising the fatiguing protocol. With this approach there
could be some difference in the absolute power output and so in the total energy
expenditure, but I consider this a minor limitation compared to exercising at two
different relative intensity.

Let’s go further.

What should be the combination between duration and relative intensity of the fatiguing
protocol when testing durability?

While as mentioned above, when kilojoules are matched, on average higher the relative
intensity of the fatiguing protocol lower the durability score, a cyclist could be more or less
durable according to the characteristics (duration and intensity) of the fatiguing protocol
chosen. So different durability types (high intensity durability vs low intensity long duration
durability) with different physiological underpinnings may exist. So, I think it make sense to
choose a fatiguing protocol specific to the effort of the goal race. For example:

4hrs at 90% of first threshold for an ironman

3hrs with many efforts around or above the critical power/second threshold for a under
23/continental road cyclist.

1.2 Choose a proper physiological/performance marker

Basically, as the definition of durability is ‘the % decrease of physiological/performance
between rested state and after a fatiguing protocol’, you are pretty free to choose the
physiological/performance marker (time trial, VO2max, lactate threshold, critical power,
anaerobic reserve, …) that you prefer. However, the same James Spragg and Colleagues’
work cited above (1), in addition to the importance of duration and intensity of the fatiguing
protocol, it also reported that, independently by the fatiguing protocol chosen, some cyclist are
more durable when the performance marker is a sprint, while others are more durable when it
is a longer effort (12min time trial). So, durability is also very specific to the
physio/performance marker used. Given this, when choosing the physio/performance marker
to test, I would use the same approach described above for choosing the fatiguing protocol:
specificity relative to the goal race.

1.3 Choose a proper standardized nutrition protocol

We all know (and several studies showed it) that performance after a prolonged fatiguing
protocol is improved when ingesting (exogenous) carbs during the fatiguing protocol itself (2).
Why?

In a previous post we saw that durability is in part linked to maintain glycogen (carbs stored in
the body) levels during prolonged exercise (read more here). In fact, while body fat reserves
are a virtually unlimited energy resource, body glycogen (carbs) stores are not. Consequently,
ingesting exogenous carbs (solid, gels or liquid) during prolonged exercise (read the fatiguing
protocol) to use them instead of glycogen to produce energy can delay fatigue and improve
durability.

There also seems to be an optimal amount and type of carbs to ingest to optimize performance
during prolonged exercise: ~90g/h with a fructose-glucose ratio of 1:2 or higher.

Performance after a fatiguing protocol ingesting different amounts and types of exogenous carbs. GLU is
glucose, FRU is fructose. Data from King et al. 2018 (2)

Looking at the graph above, it is very clear that we can’t compare different durability tests
performed ingesting different amounts of carbs during the fatiguing protocol.

Note that glucose’s gut transporter is saturated at ~60 g/h and this is probably why ‘75 g/h GLU
only’ didn’t further improve performance compared to ‘60 g/h GLU only’. For this reason, the
latest scientific studies are trying to verify if using a higher than 1:2 fructose to glucose ratio
when exceeding 90 g/h can further improve performance. However, apart from anecdotal
evidence from the world of professional cycling, to date (september 2024) there are still no data
available suggesting a clear benefit of such approach and one study (read more here) did not
show any glycogen sparing when ingesting 120 g/h with 0.8:1 ratio vs 90 g/h at 1:2 ratio. So we
can’t say that in general ‘more is better’.

So, how standardise exogenous carbs ingestion during the fatiguing protocol when
testing durability?

Again, as suggested above when choosing the duration and relative intensity of the fatiguing
protocol as well as the physiological/performance marker to test, I suggest to apply the
principle of specificity to the goal race. As performance during a prolonged race is
optimized ingesting ~90g/h with a fructose-glucose ratio of 1:2 or higher, this is the
exogenous carbs protocol you should use during the fatiguing protocol of durability’s
standardized test. If during the goal race for some reason you will ingest a lower
(because you are not able to ingest 90 g/h without nausea or because you can’t bring with
yourself 90 g/h and you have no external assistance) or higher (because you are a pro road
cyclist and you feel and believe that higher than 120 g/h is better for you) I suggest to
replicate the same protocol during the durability test. In other words, the goal is to test
durability with the individualized optimal amounts of carbs that the rider will ingest
during the goal race.

In addition to carbs during exercise, of course it is important to standardise also all the other
nutrition and supplements both during the fatiguing protocol and before the test (breakfast?)
that can influence the results: water, caffeine, bicarbonate, carbs at breakfast, … .

1.4 Testing in One Day or Two Days?

Until now, I describe only durability test protocols where the test at rested state and the test
after the fatiguing bout are both perform on the same day.

However, someone also perform test 1 and test 2 on two different days.

Generally, I prefer testing everything on the same day because I think it helps to minimize the
day-by-day variability in performance: if you split the test on two days, for any reason an athlete
can feel better on one day more than the other and so the durability score could be
overestimated (if the athlete feels better on day 2) or underestimated (if the rider feels better on
day 1). However there is nothing really wrong performing the two trials on two different days if
you are able to standardise the many things that can influence day-by-day variability in
performance, especially nutrition and training in the day/s before the tests. Dividing into two
different days is usually performed in research setting as it is easier to ask for standardising
nutrition and training sessions for some days to non-professional research participants
compared to pro athlete who are preparing for an important race.

1.5 Right Interpretation of the Results

It could be quite obvious, but it is better to make it clear: don’t be fooled by the results of the
durability test!

After finishing the test, you will have three outcomes;

the physiological/performance marker at rested state (A)

the physiological/performance marker after the fatiguing protocol (B)

the durability score

Durability score (%) = (B-A) / A * 100

Both when comparing results between different riders and when looking longitudinally at data
of the same rider (after a training block), the physiological/performance marker after the
fatiguing protocol is more relevant than the durability score for race performance(!).

So, when looking how fast a rider will be at the end of a long race, we always have to
look at performance after the fatiguing protocol and not at durability score. On the other
hand, durability score is very interesting to understand if durability can be an area of
intervention to improve performance at the end of a long race, for example adding more
low intensity training volume, performing heavy gym strength training or optimizing
some nutritional strategy, three things which have been shown to improve durability.

Until now, we focused on durability’s standardised tests. Let’s see now the other two methods
that can be used to describe this physical capacity: the record power outputs after certain a
certain energy expenditure (kilojoules) and the power-heart rate decoupling. As I indicated at
the beginning of this guide, these two methods are just estimations with some limitations that I
am going to elucidate below. So, my advice is that you can have a look at these two metrics
only when you can’t perform durability’s standardised test, which is the gold standard.

2. RECORD POWER OUTPUTS AFTER A CERTAIN ENERGY
EXPENDITURE (KILOJOULES)

This a widely spread method that many cycling analysis software, (WKO5 for example), can
calculate. It consists in comparing the maximal mean power outputs for a given duration
recorded during training and races before vs after a certain amount of energy expenditure
(kilojoules). It is a retrospective analysis of power meter data for a given period (it is better to
select at least 2/3 months to have sufficient data) and so it doesn’t require any standardised
test.

For example:

15s mean maximal power output with <1000 kilojoules spent (this is usually considered
as rested state): 1200 W

15s mean maximal power output after >3000 kilojoules spent: 940 W

durability score: -22%

3000kjs is the benchmark usually analysed for professional male road cyclists’ durability, as it is
approximately the energy expenditure accumulated in races before winning efforts are
performed in the last part of the race. However, you are pretty free to choose the kilojoules
benchmark more relevant for the athlete you are analysing. For example it could be 1500-2000
kj for women pro road cyclists.

Limitations of this method:

it doesn’t distinguish duration and relative intensity at which kilojoules have been spent,
and we saw above that this influences the durability score.

We are not sure that an all-out effort was performed both at rested state and after the
kilojoules benchmark chosen for the performance duration (15s for example) analysed.
Gregarious (helpers of the leader) usually perform at their maximum during the first part
of the race and then they drop and just pedal to the finish line. So, they almost never
perform maximal efforts after 3000 kj. This could underestimate their durability when
using this method.

3. POWER-HEART RATE DECOUPLING

The power-heart rate decoupling is a metric representing the heart rate drift to produce a
certain power output during the second compared to the first part of a relative long training
session/race.

Let’s make this example to understand better.

Image from personal Training Peaks account.

This workout represent a 8x10min zone 2 session with 1min recovery (plus warm-up and cool
down). During the second 10min @z2 interval, the average power output (purple) was 200 W
and the average heart rate (HR) 129 bpm (red). In the last 10min @z2 interval, avg power was
200W and avg HR was 139bpm. So the Power-Heart Rate decoupling score. was -7.7%.

Power-Heart Rate Decoupling Score =

(W-bpm ratio 2nd part - W-bpm ratio 1st part) / W-bpm ratio 1st part *100

My personal advice when using this method is comparing intervals in the same training zone,
better if at the same exact power output, so that you can just measure the difference in heart
rate.

Is this method valid? Does the power-heart rate decoupling really measure durability?

I am performing a scientific study with my Colleagues of Auckland University of Technology (Dr.
Ed Maunder and Dr. Jeff Rothschild) to understand more about this. At the present moment, it
looks like the power-heart rate decoupling can explain about 50% of durability (measured as
decrease in power output at first threshold). So the method is ‘half-valid’. You can have a look
at this metric to have an idea of durability when you can’t perform a standardised test but it is
not a 100% true durability assessment.

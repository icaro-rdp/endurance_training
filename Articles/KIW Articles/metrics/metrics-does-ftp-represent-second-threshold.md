# Does FTP Truly Represent the Second Exercise Threshold?

In the last KIW Research Notes, we saw that, in 2026, Critical Power (CP) is the gold standard for
identifying the second exercise threshold, also known as Maximum

Metabolic Steady State (MMSS), which is “the bioenergetic threshold above which fatigue development
is accelerated and both systemic and muscular homeostasis can no longer be maintained.” (1)

Indeed, scientific studies have consistently shown that exercising just below or above Critical

Power (-/+ 5/10%) elicits fundamentally different metabolic responses, corresponding respectively to
the medium/heavy and high/severe exercise intensity domains.

Below CP: oxygen uptake, lactate concentration, and ionic disturbance stabilize, allowing exercise
to be sustained for a relatively long duration.

On the other hand, above CP these variables canʼt reach a steady-state and increase progressively
until exhaustion.

Modified from Sitko et al. (2).

We also saw that lactate-related measurements canʼt be considered the best approach for estimating
the 2nd exercise threshold (MMSS). Specifically:

The Maximum Lactate Steady State (note the ‘Lʼ instead of ‘Mʼ). which is the highest power at which
the increase in blood lactate is less than 1 mmol/L between 10th and 30th minute of constant-load
exercise, is a good estimate of the MMSS but is highly impractical. Why? Because it requires 3-5
days of testing, repeated prolonged efforts at near-threshold intensity, and a large number of
lactate measurements.

The Second Lactate Threshold determined from an incremental step test (LT2) cannot currently be
considered an accurate estimate of MMSS. This is mainly because there is no combination of
standardized reference protocol and LT2 definition which has consistently been shown to reliably
estimate the MMSS.

Alternatively. one of the most widely used methods to estimate the second threshold in cycling is
the Functional Threshold Power, more commonly known as FTP. This concept was introduced by Andrew
Coggan in the early 2000s and is defined as the highest mean power that an athlete can sustain for
approximately one hour. In practice, FTP is often estimated using a 20-minute time trial, with FTP
calculated as 95% of the average power achieved during the effort. (3)

FTP = 20min max power - 5%

The advantage of FTP testing is that it is very simple and practical to perform in the field:

no blood lactate sampling only one maximal effort (20min), unlike CP determination, which typically
requires 2/3 trials (3, 8 and 12 min for example).

FTP is the default metric used by many popular training platforms, such as Training Peaks and

Zwift, to determine training intensity zones.

But an important question is:

Is FTP truly an accurate estimate of the second exercise threshold (MMSS)/Critical

Power?

As highlighted in a 2021 review of the scientific literature entitled “What is known about the

FTP20 test related to cycling?” (4), only two studies have directly compared Functional

Threshold Power (FTP) with Critical Power (CP)—currently considered the gold standard for
identifying the 2nd exercise threshold (MMSS)—within the same group of athletes.

These two studies are:

1. Morgan et al. (from University of Exeter, UK) published in 2019 on Journal of Sport

Sciences. (5)
2. Karsten et al. (from European University of Applied Sciences, Berlin) published in 2021 on Frontiers in Physiology. (6)

In both studies, the participants were trained amateur cyclists. Specifically:

Morganʼs study: 12 club-level cyclists, ~25 years old, FTP ~275 W.

Karstenʼs study: 17 trained cyclists and triathletes ~30 years old, FTP ~250 W.

Both studies followed similar procedures, comparing within the same athletes:

Critical Power (CP) calculated form 3-5 high/severe intensity all-out trials

FTP calculated as 95% of the mean power achieved during a 20-minute all-out effort

Critical Power was calculated using the ‘best individual-fitʼ approach, where the three common
twoparameter CP models are tested and the model with the lowest estimation error is selected. This
scientifically robust method can be automatically applied to your data using our KIW CP/W′
calculator here.

When putting together all the data and calculating the average CP and FTP, the values were quite
similar:

Morganʼs study: 3 W or 2% average difference

FTP: 278 ± 42 W

CP: 275 ± 40 W

Karstenʼs study: 7 W or 3% average difference

FTP: 249 ± 44 W

CP: 256 ± 50 W

However, here is the main point: in both studies, at the individual level the differences between
FTP and CP were large:

~50% of athletes showed differences >4%

Discrepancies arrived up to 15% 4 and 15% differences would mean:

→ For an athlete with CP = 275 W: 11 and 40 Watts

→ For an ahtlete with CP = 400 W: 15 and 60 Watts

At the individual level, CP and FTP differ by approximately 4% on average, with discrepancies
reaching up to 15% in some individuals.

Scientific literature data seems very clear and coherent:

At the individual athlete level, FTP doesnʼt represent an accurate estimation of CP, which in 2026
represents the gold standard for the 2nd exercise threshold (Maximum

Metabolic Steady State), which distinguish between steady-state and non-steady state oxidative
metabolism.

So, FTP and CP should not be used interchangeably. Given the stronger physiological background,
Critical Power should be preferred over FTP when determining training zones, training load metrics
and pacing strategies.

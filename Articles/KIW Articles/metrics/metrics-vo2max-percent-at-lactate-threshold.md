# The % of VO2max at Lactate Threshold Doesn't Discriminate the Performance Level

**Source:** knowledgeiswatt.substack.com/p/77-the-of-vo2max-at-lactate-threshold  
**Date:** May 6, 2025

A widespread belief is that higher level athletes have a second threshold/FTP at a higher
percentage of the maximum oxygen consumption (VO2max).

Is it really true?

Having a second threshold/FTP at a higher percentage of VO2max is one of the factors
that distinguish elite athletes from non-elite ones?

A hot off the press study published by Johansen and Colleagues (University of South-Eastern
Norway) on International Journal of Sport Physiology and Performance in 2025 tried to answer
this question.

WHAT DID THEY DO?

The lab metabolic incremental tests of 292 aerobic endurance athletes (212 Males and 80
females; 75 long-distance runners, 23 road cyclists, and 94 cross country skiers) were
analysed. Athletes were divided into 3 different performance level groups:

1. Elite Athletes (N=71): who were competing at international level.

2. National Athletes: (N=158) who were competing at national level.

3. Regional Athletes: (N=63) who were competing in regional recreational competitions.

The VO2max and the % of VO2max at which second lactate threshold occurred were
compared across the 3 different performance level groups.

WHAT DID THEY FIND?

As was easily guessed, VO2max differed significantly between performance levels:

Elite Athletes: 71.1 ± 6.5 ml/kg/min

National Athletes: 65.5 ± 8.0 ml/kg/min

Regional Athletes: 58.1 ± 6.4 ml/kg/min

This was also observed when males and females were analysed separately.

Data from Johansen et al. 2025 (1)

However, interestingly, the % of VO2max at which second lactate threshold
occurred was similar between the three groups:

Elite Athletes: 78.9 ± 6.4%,

National Athletes: 79.9 ± 6.7%

Regional Athletes: 80.3 ± 7.1%

This was also observed when males and females were analysed separately.

Data from Johansen et al. 2025 (1).

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYS:

While having a higher VO2max is a factor that discriminates the competitive level
of endurance athletes, the % of VO2max at which you have the second lactate
threshold is not.

GABRIELE’S COMMENT

I found these data very interesting. They seems to suggest us that VO2max discriminates
endurance performance level, while the % of VO2max at lactate threshold doesn’t.

Of course, this should not be read as ‘the % of VO2max at which you have lactate
threshold doesn’t impact endurance performance’. In fact when having similar VO2max,
having FTP at 90% instead of 80% of VO2max, clearly benefits performance. These data
suggest just that (when considered alone) this doesn’t discriminate being or not an elite athlete.

Overall, this study can also question that, in already trained athletes, the % of VO2max at
lactate threshold can be raised through training: in fact, despite their higher training volume
elite athletes didn’t record a second lactate threshold at a higher % of FTP compared to lower
performance level athletes.. As the authors commented, this is in line with previously published
data which showed that, after a training program, in already trained endurance athletes, similar
improvements in VO2max and power at second lactate threshold were observed, which in turn

resulted in a unchanged % of vo2max at second threshold . From a physiological perspective,
this makes sense: endurance training target mainly adaptations of the systems which deliver
oxygen to the muscles (stroke volume, haemoglobin level, capillarization) and use oxygen in
the muscle (mitochondria density). When these systems are enhanced by training, their
improvements are likely to benefit both VO2max and the power/velocity at second threshold.
So, it is not probably easy to isolate an improvement in threshold but not in VO2max (it should
be an adaptation more marked within the muscles and less at cardiovascular level?). However,
this is just a speculation and so it will be interesting to see future studies investigating whether
% of VO2max at which FTP occur is a trainable (which strategies?) or a genetic characteristic.
This can be very relevant to plan training as this physiological parameter could determine what
is the best high intensity interval training session to stimulate aerobic adaptations (learn more
